## The Challenge of Continuous Action Sampling in MDPs

In many real-world decision-making problems, an agent must choose the best possible action from a continuous set of options. Standard frameworks like Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) provide a mathematical foundation for this, where the goal is to maximize the expected discounted sum of future rewards. In a continuous action space $U_x \subset \mathbb{R}^{d_x}$, the optimal value function $V^\star$ is determined by the Bellman optimality equation. However, finding the exact maximum over a continuous space is often computationally prohibitive.

Practitioners frequently use a "rank-and-select" strategy: they sample $N$ candidate actions from a distribution and select the one that performs best according to a current estimate of the value function. This interface is common in sampling-based stochastic optimal control, reinforcement learning (RL) methods like the cross-entropy method, and motion planning. While intuitive, this approach introduces a fundamental approximation error because the best action in the finite sample of size $N$ is rarely the true optimal action.

A critical difficulty arises when the set of optimal actions $A^\star_x$ has zero mass under the sampling distribution. In such cases, the probability of sampling an exactly optimal action is zero, and the error in the value function $V_N$ (the fixed point of the sampled Bellman operator) relative to $V^\star$ is governed by extreme value theory rather than traditional discretization bounds. This paper develops a first-order asymptotic theory to characterize this "value gap" and the resulting policy selection rules as $N \to \infty$.

## The Geometry of Near-Optimal Actions

To understand how the value gap behaves, one must look at the "Bellman deficiency" $\Delta_x(u)$, which measures how much value is lost by choosing action $u$ instead of an optimal one:

$$\Delta_x(u) = V^\star(x) - Q_{V^\star}(x, u)$$

where $Q_{V^\star}(x, u)$ is the state-action value function. The set of optimal actions $A^\star_x$ is where $\Delta_x(u) = 0$. The paper assumes that $A^\star_x$ consists of several smooth components, or strata $M_{xj}$, such as isolated points, curves, or surfaces within the action space.

The rate at which $\Delta_x(u)$ grows as $u$ moves away from these optimal strata, combined with the density of the sampling distribution near those strata, determines the "near-optimal volume law." Specifically, the probability of sampling an action with a deficiency less than $t$ scales as $t^{\alpha_{xj}}$ for small $t$. The exponent $\alpha_{xj}$ is defined by:

$$\alpha_{xj} = (m_{xj} + \eta_{xj})/r_{xj}$$

Here, $m_{xj}$ is the codimension of the stratum, $r_{xj}$ is the power at which the deficiency grows (e.g., $r=2$ for a quadratic minimum), and $\eta_{xj}$ characterizes the sampling density's behavior. The overall convergence rate at state $x$ is dictated by the smallest exponent $\alpha_x = \min_j \alpha_{xj}$, which represents the "fattest" region of near-optimal actions.

## Marked Poisson Limits and the Tangential Operator

When $N$ is large, the best candidates in the sample are very close to the optimal set $A^\star_x$. The paper uses the theory of Poisson point processes to model these near-optimal candidates. By rescaling the deficiencies by $N^{1/\alpha}$, the set of sampled candidates converges to a Poisson process $\Pi_x$. Each point in this process is "marked" with the specific optimal action (or stratum) it is approaching.

This mark is essential because different optimal actions might lead to different future states via their transition kernels. A candidate that is slightly sub-optimal in the current step might lead to a much better future than a candidate that is closer to the current optimum but leads to a "dead end."

The central result of the paper is that the rescaled value gap $N^{1/\alpha}(V^\star - V_N)$ converges to a limit $h^\star$, which is the unique fixed point of a new operator $\Psi$, termed the "tangential extreme-value Bellman operator":

$$\Psi h(x) = \mathbb{E} \left[ \inf_{(z, u) \in \Pi_x} \{ z + \beta P_{x,u} h \} \right]$$

In this expression, $z$ represents the immediate deficiency (the "cost of sampling error"), $\beta$ is the discount factor, and $P_{x,u}$ is the transition kernel associated with the action $u$. Unlike the standard Bellman operator, $\Psi$ is nonlinear because the expectation of the minimum of several random variables is not the same as the minimum of their expectations.

## Dynamic Tie-Breaking and Selection Rules

One might assume that if two optimal actions have the same "volume" (same $C_{xj}$ and $\alpha_{xj}$), they would be selected with equal probability $1/2$. This is referred to as "static geometric selection":

$$\mathbb{P}(\text{select } j) = \frac{C_{xj}}{\sum_k C_{xk}}$$

However, the paper demonstrates that this is generally incorrect. Because the controller is solving a long-term optimization problem, the choice between two nearly optimal candidates depends on the future value $h^\star$ they provide. This is "dynamic geometric tie-breaking." The selection probability depends on both the local geometry (the constants $C_{xj}$) and the future costs $\beta P_{x,u} h^\star$ propagated through the transitions.

As shown in {fig:selection-frequency}, the dynamic selection rule can lead to significant deviations from static predictions. This happens because the "best" candidate in a finite pool is chosen to minimize the sum of current deficiency and discounted future error. Even if one action stratum is harder to hit (smaller $C_{xj}$), it might be preferred if the states it leads to have much smaller value gaps in the limit.

## Critical Switching and Heterogeneous Rates

The paper also investigates what happens when the tie between two optimal actions is not exact but is perturbed. If the perturbation is large, the controller will almost always pick the better branch. However, if the perturbation is small—specifically, if it scales at the same rate $N^{-1/\alpha}$ as the sampling error—the system enters a "critical switching layer." In this layer, the value gap and selection probabilities transition smoothly between the two competing branches. This transition is described by a family of nonlinear fixed points that interpolate between the branch-specific limits.

In systems where different states $x$ have different local exponents $\alpha_x$, the global convergence rate is dictated by the "slowest" states (those with the largest $\alpha_x$). The value gap at these critical states propagates throughout the entire MDP. If a state has a very fast local convergence rate, its own sampling error becomes negligible compared to the error "inherited" from slower states downstream. The paper uses a resolvent identity to show that in the unique-optimum case, the limit $h^\star$ simplifies to:

$$h^\star = (I - \beta P^\star)^{-1} c$$

where $P^\star$ is the transition matrix under the optimal policy and $c$ is a vector of constants derived from extreme value theory. This shows that the standard linear resolvent theory is a specific, simplified case of the broader nonlinear framework presented here.

## Summary of Contributions

This work provides a precise mathematical foundation for understanding the errors introduced by sampling actions in continuous spaces. Its unique contributions include:

1.  **Exact Scaling Laws**: Establishing that the value gap vanishes at the rate $N^{-1/\alpha}$, where $\alpha$ is determined by the interaction of Bellman deficiency geometry and sampling density.
2.  **Tangential Bellman Operator**: Introducing the operator $\Psi$ as the correct limit for the sampled Bellman equations, accounting for the inherent nonlinearity of extreme value statistics in dynamic programming.
3.  **Discovery of Dynamic Selection**: Proving that policy selection at ties is not just a function of sampling probability but is coupled to the global value function gap.
4.  **Propagation Theory**: Explaining how heterogeneous error rates at different states interact to determine the overall system performance.

By moving beyond simple discretization arguments and incorporating the dynamics of the MDP into the extreme value analysis, this research offers a more accurate tool for predicting the performance of modern, sampling-based reinforcement learning and control algorithms.
