Modeling extreme events—events that are rare but have high impact—is a central requirement in fields ranging from finance and insurance to climate science and engineering. However, modern generative models such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), flow-based models, and diffusion models often struggle to capture the "heavy tails" characteristic of these events. This research introduces a methodology for fine-tuning pre-trained generative models using a Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) penalized Wasserstein gradient flow. The resulting algorithm, the CVaR-penalized Generative Particle Algorithm (CVaR-GPA), provides a tail-agnostic way to improve the representation of extreme regions in high-dimensional distributions.

## The Limitations of Current Generative Models

Generative models typically aim to learn a transport map that transforms a simple source distribution (often a Gaussian) into a target distribution $P_{tar}$ that represents the data. When dealing with heavy-tailed data, these models face two primary hurdles: a mathematical obstruction and a statistical challenge.

The mathematical obstruction arises because the transport maps used in most deep learning architectures are Lipschitz continuous. A fundamental property of Lipschitz maps is that when they are applied to a light-tailed distribution like a Gaussian, they can only produce another light-tailed distribution. This means the model is structurally incapable of reaching the extreme values required to represent a genuinely heavy-tailed target.

The statistical challenge is known as "premature saturation." In the tail regions of a distribution, data points are by definition scarce. During training, the gradients (or "velocity fields") that drive the learning process are derived from the density of the data. Because there are so few observations in the tails, these gradients often vanish before the model has sufficiently explored those regions. In flow-based models, this leads to a premature termination of the learning process in extreme regions, causing the model to underestimate the probability and magnitude of extreme events.

## Wasserstein Gradient Flows and Fine-Tuning

To address these issues, the researchers propose a fine-tuning framework based on Wasserstein gradient flows. Instead of training a model from scratch, this approach takes samples from an existing pre-trained model $P_{pre}$ and evolves them toward the target distribution $P_{tar}$.

The evolution of the distribution $Q_t$ over time is governed by a partial differential equation (PDE) that describes how the probability mass moves to minimize a loss functional $F(Q; P_{tar})$:

$$\partial_t Q_t - \nabla \cdot [Q_t \nabla_x (\frac{\delta F(Q; P_{tar})}{\delta Q})] = 0$$

with the initial condition $Q_0 = P_{pre}$. This framework is implemented as a particle algorithm where a set of points (particles) are iteratively updated. This allows the model to refine the output of any generative model without needing to modify its internal neural network architecture.

## Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) Penalization

The choice of the loss functional $F(Q; P_{tar})$ is critical for capturing tail behavior. The authors employ a combination of the Lipschitz-regularized Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence and a penalty term based on Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR). CVaR, also known as Expected Shortfall, is a risk measure that focuses on the expected value of a variable given that it has exceeded a certain quantile (or Value-at-Risk).

The proposed functional is:

$$F_{CVaR}(Q; P_{tar}) = D_{L,KL}(Q \parallel P_{tar}) + \lambda [CVaR_{P_{tar},g, \alpha} - CVaR_{Q,g, \alpha}]^2$$

In this expression, $D_{L,KL}$ provides a robust way to compare probability measures even when they do not share the same support. The second term is the squared discrepancy between the CVaR of the target distribution and the generated distribution for a risk function $g(x) = \|x\|$ at a quantile level $\alpha$. This penalty specifically targets the tail regions: if the generated samples do not reach as far into the tails as the target samples, the CVaR term remains large, providing a signal to continue the optimization.

## Variational Subgradients and Restoring Tail Velocity

A significant technical contribution of this work is the derivation of the variational subgradients for CVaR. Standard derivatives for CVaR usually assume that the underlying distribution has a continuous density. However, in a particle-based algorithm, we work with empirical measures (sets of points), where densities do not exist in the classical sense.

The authors utilize Clarke’s generalized gradients to handle these non-smooth cases. They prove that the subgradients of the CVaR term are a family of functions related to the excess of the risk function over a threshold. Specifically, let $y$ be a threshold (the Value-at-Risk) for a distribution $Q$. The influence of the tail is captured by the function:

$$h_y(x) = \frac{(g(x)-y)^+}{1-\alpha}$$

Using this, the researchers derive a potential function $\Phi_y^Q = \phi^* - 2\lambda \Delta C(Q; P_{tar}) h_y$, where $\phi^*$ is the function that maximizes the dual form of the KL divergence and $\Delta C(Q; P_{tar}) = CVaR_{P_{tar},g, \alpha} - CVaR_{Q,g, \alpha}$ is the gap in risk between the target and the generated samples.

This leads to a velocity field $v_y^Q(x)$ that directs the particles:

$$v_y^Q(x) = \begin{cases} -\nabla_x \phi^*(x) + \frac{2\lambda}{1-\alpha} \Delta C(Q; P_{tar}) \frac{x}{\|x\|} & \text{if } \|x\| > y \\ -\nabla_x \phi^*(x) & \text{if } \|x\| \leq y \end{cases}$$

This velocity field has two critical properties. First, it is non-Lipschitz. By allowing the velocity to jump or behave non-linearly at the threshold $y$, the algorithm can generate heavy-tailed distributions from light-tailed inputs, overcoming the structural Lipschitz obstruction. Second, the term $\frac{x}{\|x\|}$ acts as a "tail velocity restoration" mechanism. Even when data is scarce, this radial push forces particles further into the tail as long as the CVaR discrepancy $\Delta C$ is non-zero.

## The CVaR-Penalized Generative Particle Algorithm (CVaR-GPA)

The CVaR-GPA algorithm implements this theory through a series of discrete steps. Starting with $M$ particles $Y_0^{(i)}$ sampled from a pre-trained model, the algorithm proceeds iteratively:

1.  **Estimate the KL component**: A neural network with spectral normalization is trained to find the function $\phi^*$ that best distinguishes the current particles from the target data.
2.  **Calculate Tail Statistics**: The algorithm computes the empirical VaR and CVaR for both the current particles and the target dataset.
3.  **Update Particles**: Each particle is moved according to the velocity field:
    $$Y_{k+1}^{(i)} = Y_k^{(i)} + v_k^{(i)} \Delta t$$
4.  **Adaptive Stopping**: Instead of training for a fixed number of steps, the algorithm monitors the empirical kinetic energy $K_k$:
    $$K_k = \frac{1}{2M} \sum_{i=1}^M \|v_k^{(i)}\|^2$$
    Training stops when $K_k$ falls below a threshold $\epsilon$, indicating that the particles have reached a stable configuration that matches the target distribution.

One of the most practical aspects of this approach is that it is "tail-agnostic." In many existing methods for heavy-tailed modeling, one must first estimate the tail index (the rate at which the probability decays). Estimating this index is notoriously difficult and prone to error. CVaR-GPA avoids this entirely by using the CVaR discrepancy as a self-regulating signal.

## Empirical Performance and Discussion

The effectiveness of CVaR-GPA was tested across several challenging benchmarks, focusing on both isotropic and anisotropic (direction-dependent) heavy-tailed distributions.

In experiments with Student-t distributions, which have tail indices $\nu = 1, 1.2, 1.5, 1.8$ (where lower values indicate heavier tails), CVaR-GPA consistently reduced errors in the tail regions compared to the pre-trained models. For the Cauchy distribution ($\nu = 1$), which is extremely heavy-tailed, the algorithm showed a dramatic reduction in log-tail error, effectively pushing particles to the extremes that the initial Lipschitz model could not reach.

The algorithm also scaled well to high-dimensional real-world data. Using the Fama-French 25 monthly portfolios dataset—a 25-dimensional financial dataset—CVaR-GPA improved the tail fit for all 25 marginal distributions. This demonstrates that the global CVaR penalty can effectively guide particles in high-dimensional spaces where extreme events might occur in many different directions simultaneously.

However, the research also identified a limitation. In cases of extreme heterogeneity—where some dimensions are very heavy-tailed while others are nearly Gaussian (light-tailed)—the global CVaR correction can sometimes over-correct the light-tailed dimensions. For instance, in a 5D experiment where one marginal was nearly Gaussian ($\nu = 30$), the error in that specific dimension increased while the heavy-tailed dimensions improved. This suggests that while a global radial push is powerful, future iterations might benefit from coordinate-specific risk functions to handle varying tail behaviors across different dimensions of the same dataset.

By deriving the necessary subgradients to apply risk measures to particle systems and integrating them into a gradient flow, this work provides a robust framework for refining generative models. It specifically addresses the vanishing velocity problem, allowing for the simulation of the rare but critical events that define the "tails" of our world.
