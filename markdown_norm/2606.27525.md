## Algorithmic Pricing and the Shifting Landscape of U.S. Housing

The integration of algorithmic pricing into the residential rental market has fundamentally altered how housing costs are determined in the United States. Traditionally, rents were set through decentralized negotiations between individual landlords and tenants. However, the rise of Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) and the adoption of sophisticated revenue management software have centralized this process. Recent investigations, including a 2024 civil antitrust complaint by the U.S. Department of Justice, suggest that these algorithmic tools may facilitate coordinated pricing, leading to inflated rents across major metropolitan areas.

![figure-1-mean-corporate-landlord-concentration-and-rent-growth-across-ten-u-s-metropolitan-areas](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27525v1/x1.png)

While research has established that algorithmic pricing contributes to aggregate rent increases—with estimates suggesting an inflation of approximately 4 percent in penetrated markets—the distributional equity of these effects has remained largely unexamined. This study addresses a critical gap in the literature by investigating whether the burden of corporate landlord concentration (CLC) is distributed unequally across racial groups at the neighborhood level. By geocoding property-level data from corporate filings and utilizing high-frequency rent indices, the research examines whether census-tract-level exposure to algorithmic landlords is associated with disproportionately higher rent growth in majority-minority neighborhoods.

## Integrating Disparate Research Domains

This work sits at the intersection of three previously separate areas of academic inquiry: the mechanics of algorithmic rent-pricing, the structural foundations of racial housing disparities, and the emerging study of algorithmic bias in real estate.

First, it builds on evidence that algorithmic pricing software leads to higher rents by encouraging joint profit maximization rather than independent competition. Previous studies have highlighted that this rent inflation particularly impacts areas where housing supply is constrained. Second, the study connects to the well-documented "rent gap" and homeownership gap between White and Black households. Black households are significantly more likely to face a housing cost burden, a reality rooted in historical and ongoing systemic inequalities. Third, the research incorporates insights from studies on automated valuation models (AVMs). Just as AVMs used by platforms like Zillow have been shown to systematically undervalue homes in Black-majority neighborhoods, this study asks if rent-setting algorithms might similarly produce racially heterogeneous outcomes.

The unique contribution of this paper lies in its neighborhood-level granularity. While prior work often looked at aggregate metropolitan data, this study analyzes outcomes at the census-tract level, allowing for a precise examination of how corporate ownership and algorithmic pricing interact with local demographic compositions.

## Measuring Corporate Concentration and Housing Burden

To quantify the presence and impact of institutional landlords, the researcher developed a multi-faceted methodology using several novel constructs.

**Corporate Landlord Concentration (CLC)**
The primary "treatment" variable, CLC, measures the degree of REIT control over a tract's rental stock. The data was constructed by extracting property addresses from SEC EDGAR 10-K annual filings for five major REITs named in the DOJ antitrust complaint: AvalonBay Communities, Equity Residential, Essex Property Trust, Mid-America Apartment Communities, and UDR. These addresses were geocoded and mapped to 2020 census tracts. The CLC for a tract is defined as the number of REIT-owned units divided by the total number of renter-occupied units. The primary analysis utilizes a $\log(1 + \text{CLC})$ transformation to account for the right-skewed distribution of corporate ownership.

**Algorithmic Housing Burden Index (AHBI)**
To isolate the marginal effect of CLC from pre-existing market conditions, the study introduces the AHBI. This composite index serves as a control variable, combining two measures of pre-existing housing stress: the share of renter households paying 30 percent or more of their income on rent and the overall housing market tightness (the inverse of the vacancy rate).

**Zillow Observed Rent Index (ZORI)**
For the outcome variable, the study moves away from standard American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year estimates, which are often too smoothed to capture acute rent surges. Instead, it utilizes the ZORI, a transaction-based index derived from actual rental listings. This allows for a more accurate calculation of rent growth between 2019 and 2023, capturing the significant volatility seen in the post-pandemic housing market.

## Econometric Modeling of Rent Growth

The study employs two primary linear regression specifications to test its hypotheses. The first, H1, examines the average association between corporate landlord concentration and rent growth across the entire sample of 583 tracts. The model is structured as:

$$\text{Rent Growth}_i = \alpha + \beta_1 \log(1 + \text{CLC}_{i,2019}) + \beta_2 \text{AHBI}_{i,2019} + X'_i \gamma + \epsilon_i$$

In this equation, $X'_i$ represents a vector of tract-level controls, including median household income, renter share, and racial composition. The initial findings for H1 indicated that a doubling of REIT concentration was associated with approximately 2.8 percentage points higher rent growth. However, this effect was primarily driven by differences *between* metropolitan areas, such as the high growth in the Sun Belt compared to the stagnation in coastal California cities.

To explore the racial dimensions of this effect, the researcher utilized the H2 specification, which includes metropolitan area fixed effects ($\delta_m$) to compare tracts within the same city and an interaction term:

$$\text{Rent Growth}_i = \alpha + \beta_1 \log(1 + \text{CLC}_{i,2019}) + \beta_2 \text{Minority}_i + \beta_3 [\log(1 + \text{CLC}_{i,2019}) \times \text{Minority}_i] + \beta_4 \text{AHBI}_{i,2019} + X'_i \gamma + \delta_m + \epsilon_i$$

Here, $\text{Minority}_i$ is an indicator for tracts where the Black and Hispanic population exceeds 50 percent. The coefficient of interest is $\beta_3$, which measures the differential impact of corporate concentration in minority versus majority-white neighborhoods.

## Key Findings: The Divergence of Impact

The analysis revealed a significant racial disparity in how corporate landlord concentration relates to rent growth. While the average effect across all tracts was modest, the interaction model (H2) showed a stark divergence.

![figure-2-scatter-plot-showing-the-relationship-between-clc-and-rent-growth-colored-by-tract-racial-composition](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27525v1/figure2_scatter_clc_rent.png)

The study found that within the same metropolitan area, the association between CLC and rent growth was significantly stronger in majority-minority tracts. Specifically, the interaction coefficient $\beta_3 = +0.0587$ suggests that the impact of corporate concentration on rent growth was 5.9 percentage points larger in minority neighborhoods than in majority-white neighborhoods.

Interestingly, the baseline effect in majority-white tracts ($\beta_1 = -0.034$) was slightly negative, suggesting that in these neighborhoods, higher corporate presence was associated with somewhat lower rent growth. Conversely, the combined effect in minority tracts was positive. This asymmetry suggests that the mechanisms of algorithmic rent-setting are highly sensitive to the local socioeconomic context, potentially exploiting lower tenant mobility or fewer affordable alternatives in minority-majority areas.

![figure-3-ols-coefficient-estimates-showing-the-significance-of-the-interaction-differential-in-h2](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27525v1/figure5_coef_plot.png)

## Validation via Machine Learning

To ensure these findings were not simply artifacts of the linear model's structure, the study employed a non-parametric XGBoost predictive model (H3). This model was trained on 2019 tract characteristics to predict the rent growth observed through 2023.

The XGBoost model achieved an $R^2 = 0.439$ on a held-out test set, meaning it successfully explained nearly 44 percent of the variance in rent growth. To interpret the "black box" of the machine learning model, the researcher used SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values, which quantify the contribution of each feature to a specific prediction.

![figure-4-feature-importance-based-on-mean-absolute-shap-values-ranking-clc-as-the-fifth-most-predictive-feature](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27525v1/h3_shap_bar.png)

The SHAP analysis corroborated the econometric results. REIT concentration (log CLC) emerged as an independent predictor of rent growth. More importantly, the model autonomously learned the racial disparity: the SHAP values for CLC were generally positive in minority tracts and negative in white tracts, mirroring the interaction effect found in the linear regression. This consistency across different modeling techniques strengthens the evidence for a racially heterogeneous impact of institutional landlord concentration.

![figure-5-shap-beeswarm-plot-showing-the-distribution-of-feature-impacts-on-the-model-s-output](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27525v1/h3_shap_beeswarm.png)

## Policy Implications and Conclusion

The findings of this research have significant implications for antitrust enforcement and fair housing policy. By demonstrating that algorithmic rent-setting may disproportionately burden minority communities, the study highlights that antitrust concerns should extend beyond aggregate market efficiency to include distributional equity.

Under the Fair Housing Act, a policy or practice can be challenged if it has a "disparate impact" on protected groups, even if the policy appears neutral on its face. The documented disparity in rent growth suggests that the centralized, algorithmic coordination of rents by corporate landlords may fall under this scrutiny.

Furthermore, the study points to a need for greater transparency. The opacity of the algorithms used by REITs creates a significant information asymmetry between landlords and tenants. The researcher suggests that requiring institutional landlords to disclose the use of revenue management software in public SEC filings could allow for better regulatory monitoring of these distributional effects.

In summary, this research provides some of the first neighborhood-level evidence that the shift toward algorithmic, corporate-led housing markets is not occurring on a level playing field. While algorithmic pricing may promise "efficiency," its actual implementation appears to exacerbate existing racial inequalities in the U.S. housing market. Future work may look to expand this analysis to more metropolitan areas ($n=10$ in this study) and utilize instrumental variable strategies to further isolate the causal pathways of these disparities.
