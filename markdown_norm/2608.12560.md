## Quantifying Resilience in Electric Power Systems

The reliable operation of the electric grid is a cornerstone of modern society. However, this infrastructure is increasingly vulnerable to extreme weather events, aging equipment, and operational failures. In response, the field of power system resilience has developed various metrics to quantify how well a system absorbs, adapts to, and recovers from disruptions. A common conceptual tool is the "resilience trapezoid," which visualizes the performance of a system as it degrades during an event and subsequently recovers. While these frameworks provide a useful summary of impact, they frequently rely on deterministic methods. These traditional approaches treat observed data as perfect representations of reality, ignoring the noise, reporting irregularities, and inherent variability found in real-world outage counts.

![observed-power-outages-in-dane-county-wisconsin-during-a-thunderstorm-event-in-may-2024](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12560v1/daneCountyDataMay2024.png)

A significant limitation of existing resilience metrics is the absence of uncertainty quantification. When a metric like the Area Under the Curve (AUC) is calculated from a single recovery trajectory, it provides a point estimate but offers no measure of how much confidence we should have in that number. An AUC derived from a county with erratic reporting or sparse data is treated with the same weight as one from a county with high-resolution, dense data. Researchers from Oak Ridge National Laboratory address this gap by proposing a hierarchical Bayesian framework that estimates the time-varying risk of power outages while capturing the uncertainty inherent in the recovery process.

## The Challenge of Overdispersion in Outage Data

Power outage data recorded at the county level often exhibits a phenomenon known as overdispersion. This occurs when the observed variance in the number of customers without power is greater than what would be predicted by standard statistical distributions like the Binomial or Poisson. Several factors contribute to this variability:
1.  **Heterogeneous Infrastructure:** Differences in grid topology and equipment age across a geographic area can cause varying outage patterns.
2.  **Weather Severity:** Micro-climatic variations during a storm may impact different parts of a county with unequal intensity.
3.  **Reporting Reliability:** The systems used by utilities to track and report outages (e.g., EAGLE-I™) may vary in their update frequency or precision.

Ignoring overdispersion can lead to overly optimistic confidence intervals and potentially biased estimates of recovery risk. To address this, the authors utilize a Beta-Binomial likelihood. Unlike the standard Binomial distribution, which assumes a fixed probability of failure for every customer, the Beta-Binomial distribution treats the underlying outage probability as a random variable following a Beta distribution. This provides an additional parameter, the concentration parameter $\kappa_g$, which allows the model to flex and accommodate the extra variance found in real-world data.

## A Hierarchical Spline-Based Model

The core of the proposed approach is a hierarchical Bayesian regression model. The model aims to infer a latent, time-varying outage probability $p_{g,t}$, representing the risk that a customer in group $g$ is without power at time $t$. 

### Likelihood and Parameterization
The observed number of outages $y_{g,t}$ is modeled as follows:

$$
y_{g,t} \sim \text{BetaBinomial}(n_{g,t}, \alpha_{g,t}, \eta_{g,t})
$$

where $n_{g,t}$ is the total customer base. The parameters $\alpha_{g,t}$ and $\eta_{g,t}$ control the shape of the Beta distribution and are linked to the latent probability $p_{g,t}$ and the concentration parameter $\kappa_g$ by:

$$
\alpha_{g,t} = p_{g,t} \cdot \kappa_g
$$

$$
\eta_{g,t} = (1 - p_{g,t}) \cdot \kappa_g
$$

The concentration parameter $\kappa_g$ determines the degree of overdispersion. As $\kappa_g$ increases toward infinity, the distribution approaches a standard Binomial distribution. Lower values of $\kappa_g$ indicate higher uncertainty and more "spread" in the data.

### Modeling Temporal Dynamics with B-Splines
To represent the smooth but complex trajectory of an outage event—from the initial spike in failures to the gradual restoration of service—the authors use cubic B-spline basis functions. The latent probability $p_{g,t}$ is modeled by transforming a linear combination of these basis functions using a sigmoid link function:

$$
p_{g,t} = \sigma(f_g(t)) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-f_g(t)}}
$$

$$
f_g(t) = \mathbf{B}(t)^\top \mathbf{\beta}_g
$$

Here, $\mathbf{B}(t)$ is the vector of spline basis functions and $\mathbf{\beta}_g$ represents the coefficients for group $g$. This structure allows the model to capture non-linear recovery patterns without requiring researchers to manually define the functional form of the curve.

### Hierarchical Smoothing and Information Sharing
A key advantage of the Bayesian approach is the ability to share information across different groups (e.g., different counties or events) through hierarchical priors. The model places a weakly informative prior on the spline coefficients:

$$
\mathbf{\beta}_g \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2 I)
$$

Furthermore, the dispersion parameter $\kappa_g$ is determined hierarchically. This allows the model to "borrow strength" from the overall population of outage events. If a specific event has very little data, the model's estimate for its dispersion will be informed by the patterns seen in other, data-rich events. This stabilizes the estimation and ensures that uncertainty estimates remain robust even when data is sparse. The hierarchy for $\kappa_g$ is defined as:

$$
\kappa_{\text{global}} \sim \text{Gamma}(5, 0.5)
$$

$$
\kappa_{\text{raw},g} \sim \text{Gamma}(5, 5/\kappa_{\text{global}})
$$

$$
\kappa_g = \kappa_{\text{raw},g} + 1
$$

## Deriving Reliability-Aware Resilience Metrics

Once the posterior distribution of the latent trajectories $p_{g,t}$ is obtained via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling, the model can generate posterior distributions for derived resilience metrics. The primary metric used is the Area Under the Curve (AUC), which integrates the outage probability over the duration of the event. To calculate this for each posterior sample $(s)$, the authors use Simpson's Rule across a dense grid of $J$ time steps:

$$
\text{AUC}^{(s)}_g = \frac{\Delta t}{3} \left[ p^{(s)}_{g,0} + 4 \sum_{j=1, j \text{ odd}}^{J-1} p^{(s)}_{g,j} + 2 \sum_{j=2, j \text{ even}}^{J-2} p^{(s)}_{g,j} + p^{(s)}_{g,J} \right]
$$

This process results in a full posterior distribution of AUC rather than a single number. This allows for the calculation of 95% credible intervals, providing a clear statistical measure of the reliability of the resilience assessment.

## Evaluating Diverse Outage Scenarios

The framework was applied to various real-world power outage events in Wisconsin to demonstrate its versatility across different scales and storm types.

### Rapid Restoration: Dane County
During a thunderstorm in May 2024, Dane County experienced a sharp spike of 40,000 outages followed by a relatively quick recovery. The model’s posterior mean tracked the observed data closely, and the 95% credible interval captured the majority of the data points. The resulting AUC was estimated at 7.88, with a narrow credible interval indicating high confidence in the recovery trajectory.

![posterior-estimation-for-dane-county-may-2024-the-blue-line-represents-the-posterior-mean-and-the-shaded-region-shows-the-95-credible-interval](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12560v1/daneCountyResultsMay2024.png)

### Persistent Impacts: Door County
In contrast to the quick thunderstorm recovery, a snowstorm in April 2024 led to a much longer outage event in Door County. The risk remained elevated for over 300 time intervals. The model successfully captured this "long tail," yielding a significantly higher AUC of 85.53. The model's ability to handle long-duration events without manual intervention demonstrates the flexibility of the B-spline approach.

![the-model-captures-the-gradual-recovery-and-high-cumulative-risk-associated-with-a-persistent-snowstorm-in-door-county](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12560v1/doorCountyResultsApr2024.png)

### Urban Blizzards: Milwaukee and Waukesha
The model was also used to compare two neighboring counties, Milwaukee and Waukesha, during a January 2024 blizzard. While both counties suffered severe outages, the model quantified a higher cumulative risk in Waukesha (AUC: 34.18) compared to Milwaukee (AUC: 11.06). Such comparisons are valuable for utilities managing multi-county service areas, as they provide a standardized way to measure which regions faced more persistent difficulty in restoration.

### Handling Sparse Data: Jefferson County
One of the most challenging scenarios for any statistical model is a "low-information" regime, where very few data points are available. The authors tested the model on several brief outage events in Jefferson County. In these cases, the hierarchical structure of the model becomes critical. Rather than failing or providing a misleadingly precise estimate, the Bayesian model produced wide credible intervals, accurately reflecting the lack of information and the resulting uncertainty in the AUC.

## Significance for Grid Resilience

The primary contribution of this work is the transition from deterministic point estimates to a probabilistic understanding of power system recovery. By incorporating a Beta-Binomial likelihood and hierarchical splines, the model addresses the issues of overdispersion and data sparsity that plague real-world outage monitoring.

For grid operators and policy makers, the practical value of this approach lies in the "reliability" of the metrics it produces. In a deterministic world, two events might look similar based on a simple AUC calculation. In the Bayesian framework, one might have a narrow 95% credible interval while the other has a very wide one. This distinction is vital; it tells decision-makers when they have enough information to form a solid conclusion and when they should be cautious due to data volatility. 

Furthermore, the hierarchical nature of the model provides a foundation for future extensions. By adding covariates—such as wind speed, precipitation levels, or infrastructure age—researchers could transform this diagnostic tool into a predictive one. This would allow for real-time estimation of recovery trajectories as a storm is still unfolding, providing emergency responders with a statistically grounded forecast of when and where the grid is most at risk. In an era of increasing climate-driven hazards, having a mathematically rigorous way to quantify exactly what we do and do not know about grid recovery is essential for building a more resilient energy future.
