## Overview of Spectral Theory and Discrete Systems

In the study of dynamical systems, physics, and biology, researchers often use linear operators to model the evolution of a state over time or space. A fundamental quantity in this analysis is the principal eigenvalue, which typically determines the long-term growth rate or the stability of a system. For symmetric (self-adjoint) operators, the Rayleigh–Ritz principle provides a well-known method to calculate this eigenvalue by maximizing a specific quadratic form. This principle is not just a mathematical curiosity; it allows researchers to understand how changing the "potential" or the environment—such as the distribution of nutrients in a biological model—can maximize the growth rate of a population.

However, many real-world systems are inherently asymmetric. For example, in a medium with a constant wind or current, the probability of a particle moving in one direction is higher than the other. This asymmetry breaks the self-adjoint property of the underlying operators. While these asymmetric operators still possess a principal eigenvalue, the standard Rayleigh–Ritz tools do not apply directly. The research presented by Xing Liang focuses on establishing new variational principles and optimization techniques for these asymmetric operators, specifically when they are defined on discrete periodic lattices. These lattices are grids of points that repeat a specific pattern, providing a bridge between continuous models and computational simulations.

## The Challenge of Asymmetry on Lattices

When dealing with asymmetric second-order difference operators on a periodic lattice, the mathematical landscape becomes significantly more complex. In continuous space, researchers like Nadin (2010) previously addressed this by transforming the problem into a "positive-profile–corrector" problem. This approach used the fact that continuous differential equations often satisfy simple conservation laws, such as a constant flux.

In a discrete setting, like a lattice $T_N$ with $m$ dimensions, these continuous identities do not translate easily. A discrete operator $M$ acting on a function $u$ at site $x$ might be defined by the rates of jumping to neighboring sites:

$$
(Mu)_x = \sum_{\nu} r^{+}_{\nu}(x) u(x+e_{\nu}) + \sum_{\nu} r^{-}_{\nu}(x) u(x-e_{\nu}) + q_x u_x
$$

Here, $r^{\pm}_{\nu}(x)$ represents the jump rates to neighbors, and $q_x$ represents a local potential. If the jump rate in one direction is different from the jump rate in the opposite direction, the operator is asymmetric. One of the primary difficulties in the discrete case is that the conservation laws—analogs to the continuous flux—become nonlinear. For instance, while a continuous diffusion model with an "exponential tilt" (representing a biased direction) leads to linear relationships, the discrete counterpart involves transcendental functions like hyperbolic sines and cosines. This nonlinearity necessitates a specialized approach to find a variational representation that is both mathematically rigorous and geometrically intuitive.

## A New Variational Principle for Asymmetric Operators

The paper’s first major contribution is the derivation of a discrete variational principle for the principal eigenvalue $\Lambda(M)$ of an irreducible, nearest-neighbor asymmetric operator on a periodic lattice. The author introduces a functional $F_M(a, \beta)$ that depends on two main components: a positive profile $a$ (a normalized distribution across the lattice) and a periodic logarithmic corrector $\beta$.

The principal eigenvalue is characterized as a saddle point of this functional. A saddle point is a point that is a minimum in one direction and a maximum in another. Specifically, the paper proves that:

$$
\Lambda(M) = \min_{\beta} \max_a F_M(a, \beta) = \max_a \min_{\beta} F_M(a, \beta)
$$

In this formula, $a$ belongs to a set of positive profiles where $\sum_x a_x^2 = 1$, and $\beta$ is a mean-zero periodic corrector. This representation is significant because it provides both an upper and a lower bound for the principal eigenvalue without requiring the use of abstract minimax theorems. Instead, the author uses a direct diagonal symmetrization argument. By taking the positive right eigenvector $\phi$ and the positive left eigenvector $\psi$ of the operator, and defining the profile as $a_x = \sqrt{\phi_x \psi_x}$, the asymmetry is "balanced" out. This choice of $a$ and a corresponding $\beta$ based on the ratio of the eigenvectors allows the asymmetric operator to be mapped onto a symmetric matrix, effectively revealing the underlying energetic structure of the system.

## The Geometric Structure of Principal Eigenvalues

A key aspect of this variational principle is the "corrector problem." When one minimizes the functional $F_M(a, \beta)$ over the periodic corrector $\beta$, the resulting Euler equation takes the form of a discrete flux-conservation law: $\text{div}_d J(x) = 0$. This means that at every site on the lattice, the total "flux" $J_{x,\nu}$ entering and leaving is balanced.

For a specialized case, such as an exponentially tilted discrete diffusion-growth operator $L^c_{p,V}$, the principal eigenvalue $\Lambda^c_p(V)$ can be written in a form that separates different physical effects:

$$
\Lambda^c_p(V) = \max_a \{ -\sum_{x,\nu} c_{x,\nu} (a_{x+e_{\nu}} - a_x)^2 + \sum_x V(x)a_x^2 + E^c_p(a) \}
$$

In this expression, the first term represents the discrete Dirichlet energy (the cost of spatial variation), the second term is the average potential, and $E^c_p(a)$ is a nonlinear periodic correction term. Unlike the continuous case where this correction might be a simple quadratic term, the discrete correction $E^c_p(a)$ is defined through a minimization involving hyperbolic functions:

$$
E^c_p(a) = \min_{\beta} 2\sum_{x,\nu} c_{x,\nu} a_x a_{x+e_{\nu}} [\cosh(\beta_{x+e_{\nu}} - \beta_x - p_{\nu}) - 1]
$$

This structure shows exactly how the discrete nature of the lattice and the asymmetry (parametrized by $p$) interact with the spatial distribution $a$. It provides a geometric objective function that researchers can use to analyze how the arrangement of the potential $V$ affects the overall growth rate of the system.

## Rearrangement Optimization in One Dimension

One of the most practical questions in spectral theory is: given a fixed set of potential values, how should they be arranged spatially to maximize the principal eigenvalue? For symmetric operators, the answer is often a "symmetric-decreasing" rearrangement. The paper extends this intuition to the asymmetric periodic case in one dimension.

In a 1D periodic lattice, the conserved flux $J$ is simply a constant scalar. This simplification allows the author to define a scalar-flux formula for the correction term $T_{\sigma}(a)$. To prove that a specific arrangement is optimal, the author employs Supnick's fixed-tour theorem from the field of combinatorial optimization. This theorem identifies optimal paths in graphs given certain conditions on the weights of the edges.

The paper demonstrates that the principal eigenvalue is maximized when the potential $V$ and the profile $a$ follow a "bell-shaped" rearrangement (denoted $a^{\#}$ and $V^{\#}$). In this arrangement, the largest values are clustered together, and the values decrease as one moves away from the center of the cluster in either direction around the periodic ring. Specifically, for any tilt $p$:

$$
\Lambda_p(V^{\#}) \geq \Lambda_p(V)
$$

This result is proven by showing that the underlying kernel $g_J(xy) = \sqrt{x^2y^2 + J^2} - J \text{arsinh}(J/(xy))$ satisfies a strict four-point inequality, making it a "Monge matrix." This mathematical property guarantees that the bell-shaped ordering is universally optimal for all possible values of the flux $J$ and the tilt $p$. This is a powerful conclusion because it implies that the same spatial arrangement maximizes the eigenvalue regardless of how strong the directional bias (the tilt) is.

## Applications to Wave Propagation and Quantum Mechanics

The theoretical results established in the paper have direct applications in modeling propagation phenomena. One prominent example is the KPP (Kolmogorov–Petrovsky–Piskunov) equation on a lattice, which describes how a population or a chemical reaction spreads through a periodic environment. The minimal speed at which a front moves, $c^*_{tw}(V)$, is closely related to the principal eigenvalue of a tilted operator:

$$
c^*(V) = \inf_{p>0} \frac{\Lambda_p(V)}{p}
$$

Because the bell-shaped rearrangement $V^{\#}$ maximizes $\Lambda_p(V)$ for every $p$, it follows that this arrangement also maximizes the minimal front speed $c^*_{tw}(V)$ and the spreading speed $c^*_{sp}(V)$. This means that if you want a reaction to spread as quickly as possible through a periodic grid, you should cluster the areas of high growth rate together.

Furthermore, the work connects to the Hatano–Nelson model in non-Hermitian quantum mechanics. In this model, the principal eigenvalue corresponds to the ground-state energy of a particle in a lattice with an imaginary vector potential (the tilt). The positive right and left eigenvectors, $\phi$ and $\psi$, relate to the density of the state. The variational principles derived here offer a new way to characterize the ground-state energy and its sensitivity to the arrangement of the potential, providing a more geometric understanding of these quantum systems.

Finally, the paper highlights the unique nature of periodic systems. By comparing the results to "open chains" (lattices with Dirichlet or Neumann boundaries), the author shows that the optimal arrangements change significantly when the topology is not periodic. For open chains, the tilt $p$ often has no effect on the principal eigenvalue, and different rearrangements, such as monotone decreasing orders, become optimal. This distinction emphasizes the specific role that periodic boundary conditions play in sustaining directional flux and influencing the spectral properties of asymmetric systems.
