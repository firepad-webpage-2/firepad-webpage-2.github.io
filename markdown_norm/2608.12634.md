## Understanding Institutional Constraints in Global Markets

In modern finance, the set of investors who can hold a particular stock is often restricted by formal mandates. These constraints can stem from environmental, social, and governance (ESG) criteria, regulatory requirements, or religious principles. One of the most transparent and institutionalized forms of such constraints is Shariah equity screening. For a stock to be deemed "Shariah-compliant," the underlying company must pass specific business activity tests (e.g., no involvement in alcohol or gambling) and financial ratio thresholds (e.g., limited debt-to-asset ratios).

This paper explores the financial implications of these binary "pass/fail" classifications. While the labels might seem static, the authors argue that they are subject to significant "classification uncertainty." This uncertainty arises because different Shariah standards (rulebooks) often disagree on a firm's status, and firms that are currently compliant may be hovering dangerously close to the financial thresholds that would trigger a reclassification. By examining these dynamics, the researchers seek to understand how changes in the "permitted investor base" influence asset prices and market behavior.

## The Concept of Classification Uncertainty

The core contribution of this work is the formalization of "classification uncertainty" as a multi-dimensional finance object. The authors move beyond the simple binary status of a stock to consider the stability and robustness of its investable status. This uncertainty is characterized by two primary dimensions:

1.  **Cross-Sectional Disagreement:** Different Shariah authorities (such as AAOIFI, Dow Jones Islamic Markets, or S&P) apply varying financial thresholds and definitions. A company might be eligible according to one rulebook but ineligible according to another, even if its underlying financials are identical. This creates a fragmented investor base where only a subset of Shariah-compliant capital is permitted to invest.
2.  **Dynamic Classification-Boundary Risk:** Financial ratios are not static. A company that is compliant today with a debt-to-assets ratio of 32% is much more "at risk" of becoming ineligible (if the threshold is 33%) than a company with a ratio of 10%. This proximity to the boundary represents a risk that the stock might soon be excluded from the investable universe of constrained managers, potentially triggering forced divestment.

To quantify these concepts, the authors introduce metrics such as "proximity risk" and "implemented disagreement." These tools allow investors to monitor the fragility of a stock's compliance status before any official change occurs.

## Theoretical Framework: Permitted Investor Mass

To explain why these classifications matter for asset pricing, the authors develop a theoretical model centered on the concept of "Permitted Investor Mass."

In this model, the market consists of unconstrained investors (who can buy anything) and segmented investors (who are bound by specific Shariah standards). Each firm $i$ has an eligibility vector $e_i$, where each entry corresponds to its status under one of $S$ different standards. If a firm is compliant under a specific standard, the capital governed by that standard is permitted to hold it.

The "Permitted Investor Mass" ($m_i$) for firm $i$ is defined as:
$$
m_i = \omega_U + \omega^T e_i
$$
where $\omega_U$ represents the capital of unconstrained investors and $\omega^T e_i$ represents the sum of capital governed by the specific standards that permit the stock.

The model predicts that, all else being equal, the required expected return for a stock ($\mu^*_i$) is inversely proportional to its permitted investor mass $m_i$. If more investors are allowed to hold a stock (an expansion of $m_i$), the required return should decrease, leading to a positive price reaction. Conversely, if a stock loses its compliant status, its $m_i$ shrinks, which could lead to selling pressure and a lower price.

## Measuring Boundary Risk and Disagreement

A key technical aspect of the paper is how the authors measure the distance between a firm's current financials and its compliance limits. They define the "active margin" ($\Delta_{i,s,t}$) for a firm $i$ under standard $s$ at time $t$ as the relative distance to the nearest binding threshold.

From this, they derive the "nearest active-boundary distance" ($D_{i,t}$), which is the minimum of these margins across all relevant screens. To make this easier to interpret as a risk index, they transform it into "proximity risk" ($P_{i,t}$):
$$
P_{i,t} = \exp(-5D_{i,t})
$$
This index ranges from 0 to 1, where a value closer to 1 indicates that a firm is extremely close to a reclassification boundary.

Similarly, they measure "implemented disagreement" ($DISC_{i,t}$) using the eligibility share ($\bar{e}_{i,t}$), which is the fraction of standards that currently permit the stock:
$$
DISC_{i,t} = 4\bar{e}_{i,t}(1 - \bar{e}_{i,t})
$$
This reaches its maximum when exactly half of the standards agree, reflecting the highest level of uncertainty about the firm's classification.

## Empirical Route A: The U.S. Cross-Standard Panel

The authors first test their metrics on a massive dataset of over 13,000 U.S. stocks from 1999 to 2024. Since official historical Shariah labels for all these stocks are not publicly available for every standard, the researchers "emulate" seven major Shariah rulebooks by applying the documented financial thresholds to standard accounting data (Compustat).

They find that their metrics—disagreement and proximity risk—are highly effective at predicting future changes in these emulated labels. A stock with high proximity risk today is significantly more likely to see a change in its compliance status in the following month.

However, when testing whether these metrics are directly "priced" into expected returns in the U.S. market, the results were more nuanced. While there was a baseline association between eligibility and returns, it largely disappeared after controlling for standard firm characteristics like profitability and investment. Furthermore, an "event study" of a major methodology change by Dow Jones and S&P in 2023—which admitted hundreds of new firms—did not show a robust, permanent price increase. This suggests that in large, liquid markets like the U.S., a change in formal eligibility alone might not be enough to move prices unless the affected "investor mass" is sufficiently large and active.

## Empirical Route B: Official Events in Malaysia

To find clearer evidence of price effects, the authors turned to the Malaysian market. Malaysia has a highly developed Islamic finance ecosystem where the Securities Commission (SC) Malaysia publishes official lists of Shariah-compliant securities twice a year. These lists are followed by a significant amount of "constrained capital"—domestic pension funds and Shariah-compliant mutual funds.

The researchers analyzed 25 of these official lists and focused on "inclusions" (stocks that were not on the previous list but appeared on the new one). Their findings were more striking here:
- For stocks that were sufficiently liquid (tradable), inclusion in the official Shariah-compliant list led to a significant positive price reaction.
- Specifically, these stocks saw an average market-adjusted return of 1.76 percentage points over the $[0, 10]$ day window following the announcement.
- This effect grew to 2.25 percentage points over the $[0, 20]$ day window.

The study used "matching" techniques to ensure these results weren't just driven by small companies or other factors. They compared the newly included stocks to very similar stocks that were already compliant, confirming that the price jump was likely tied to the change in status itself.

## Why Does Tradability Matter?

A crucial finding of the paper is that these price effects are conditional on liquidity. When the researchers included all stocks—including those that rarely trade—the results became much weaker and less statistically significant.

This makes intuitive sense: for a change in "permitted investor mass" to affect a stock's price, investors must actually be able to buy or sell the shares. In the Malaysian context, the price pressure likely comes from institutional funds rebalancing their portfolios to include the newly "permitted" stocks. If a stock is too illiquid, these institutions cannot easily build positions, and the price signal is lost in the noise.

The researchers also looked at ownership data and found evidence that Shariah-sensitive portfolios did indeed increase their holdings in these stocks following their inclusion, although identifying the exact "marginal buyer" remains challenging.

## Significance and Broader Impact

This research moves the study of Shariah finance from a niche topic of religious compliance into the mainstream of institutional asset pricing. By defining and measuring classification uncertainty, the authors provide a framework that is applicable to any market segment governed by binary rules.

### For Portfolio Managers
The introduction of boundary risk and disagreement metrics provides a new set of tools for risk management. A Shariah-compliant fund manager can use these metrics to identify "fragile" holdings that might soon become ineligible, allowing for more gradual and less costly divestment. Conversely, they can identify "near-compliant" stocks that might soon enter their investable universe, allowing for better liquidity planning.

### For Regulators and Standard-Setters
The paper highlights how disagreement between different standards can fragment the market. If different rulebooks provide different answers, the effective "investor mass" for a stock is reduced, which can impact the stock's liquidity and cost of capital. This provides a data-driven argument for the harmonization of screening standards.

### Beyond Shariah Finance
The findings have broad implications for other types of constrained investing, most notably ESG and "Green" taxonomies. Just as Shariah standards have thresholds for debt, green taxonomies have thresholds for carbon intensity or revenue from sustainable activities. This paper suggests that being "near the edge" of these categories is a distinct form of risk that the market should account for.

In summary, the "Price of Permission" is not a fixed cost, but a dynamic variable. While formal eligibility is a prerequisite for investment, the stability of that permission—and the total mass of capital that respects it—are the true drivers of market impact. The study demonstrates that while labels matter, the uncertainty surrounding those labels is a crucial, and previously overlooked, dimension of financial risk.
