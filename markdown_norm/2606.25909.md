## Introduction to Economy-Wide Carbon Accounting

Carbon offsets have become a prominent mechanism in global climate strategy, allowing entities to compensate for their own greenhouse gas emissions by funding projects that reduce or remove carbon elsewhere. Central to the credibility of these offsets is the concept of additionality: a project is only additional if it would not have occurred without the financial incentive provided by the offset payment. Historically, the literature and regulatory practice have focused on project-level assessments to determine additionality. However, this approach often overlooks the complex interdependencies within an economy.

Research by Isla Globus-Harris and Daniel H. Karney introduces an analytical general equilibrium framework to evaluate the broader economic impacts of carbon offset policies. By shifting the focus from individual projects to the entire economy, the study identifies market-based spillover effects—often referred to as leakage—that can either erode or amplify the intended emissions reductions. Their work suggests that traditional project-based carbon accounting may significantly diverge from the actual change in aggregate emissions, leading to systematic over-crediting or, in specific cases, under-crediting of carbon offsets.

## The General Equilibrium Framework

To capture the systemic effects of carbon offsets, the authors construct a model representing an economy with multiple sectors and resource constraints. The economy relies on a fixed amount of productive resources, denoted as capital $\bar{K}$. This capital is allocated across three primary activities: the production of a final consumption good $X$, renewable (clean) energy $R$, and fossil fuel (dirty) energy $F$. The resource constraint is defined as:

$$\bar{K} = K_X + K_R + K_F$$

In this model, the final consumption good $X$ is produced using a combination of capital $K_X$ and total energy $E$. The energy itself is an aggregate of renewable and fossil fuel components. A key distinction in the energy sector is the presence of a sector-specific fixed resource $\bar{Q}$ for renewable energy production, such as land suitable for wind turbines or solar panels. This fixed factor introduces diminishing marginal returns to capital in the renewable sector, which reflects physical and geographic constraints in the real world.

Carbon offsets are modeled as a per-unit subsidy $s$ paid to renewable energy producers. To maintain a balanced government budget $B = 0$, this subsidy is financed by an ad valorem tax $t$ on the total capital in the economy. The model solves for the changes in prices and quantities in response to a marginal increase in the offset price. By setting the price of the final consumption good $P_X = 1$ as the numeraire, the authors can trace how the subsidy influences resource allocation, energy consumption patterns, and ultimately, total emissions $Z$.

## Defining Four Margins of Additionality

One of the unique contributions of this work is the precise taxonomy of additionality. While many discussions treat additionality as a binary (either a project is additional or it is not), this research identifies four distinct margins through which renewable energy output responds to offset payments:

1.  **Inframarginal, non-additional offsets ($R_N$):** These are projects that would have been built even without the initial offset payment. They receive a windfall gain from the subsidy but do not change their initial presence in the market.
2.  **Extensive-margin, additional offsets ($R_A$):** These are new projects that enter the market specifically because the offset payment exists.
3.  **Intensive-margin, additional offsets ($dR_A$):** Once an additional project exists, an increase in the offset price might encourage it to produce more output (e.g., upgrading equipment to increase capacity).
4.  **Intensive-margin, non-additional offsets ($dR_N$):** This represents a previously unidentified margin. Even if a project is "non-additional" because it would have existed regardless of the initial price, a marginal increase in the subsidy still incentivizes it to expand its output.

Conventional carbon accounting typically focuses only on the direct emissions reductions associated with the increase in traditionally additional renewable energy. However, by identifying the $dR_N$ margin, the authors show that even non-additional projects contribute to the total change in clean energy output, a factor often ignored in standard regulatory frameworks.

## Discrepancies in Carbon Accounting

The central tension explored in the paper is the gap between conventional carbon accounting ($\Omega_C$) and aggregate carbon accounting ($\Omega_A$). Conventional accounting is defined as the direct emissions avoided by the increase in additional renewable energy:

$$\Omega_C \equiv -\xi_F dR_A$$

Where $\xi_F$ is the emission factor for fossil fuel energy. In contrast, aggregate carbon accounting measures the total change in emissions across the entire economy, accounting for shifts in fossil fuel use and changes in the final goods sector:

$$\Omega_A \equiv \xi_F dF + \xi_X dK_X$$

The authors introduce a metric $\Delta$ to quantify the discrepancy between these two approaches:

$$\Delta = \frac{\Omega_A - \Omega_C}{\Omega_C}$$

A negative value ($\Delta < 0$) indicates that conventional accounting overstates the emissions reductions. The research finds that for many realistic parameter settings, $\Delta$ is indeed negative. This occurs because an increase in renewable energy production does not perfectly displace fossil fuel energy one-for-one. Instead, it can lower energy prices, leading to an overall increase in energy demand that partially offsets the displacement effect. Furthermore, resources shifting into the clean energy sector must be drawn from elsewhere, potentially altering emissions intensities in other parts of the economy.

Interestingly, the study also highlights scenarios where $\Delta > 0$, meaning conventional accounting under-credits the policy. This tends to happen when the share of traditionally additional offsets is low, but the intensive-margin response from non-additional projects ($dR_N$) is significant. In these cases, the "non-additional" projects are doing more work to reduce aggregate emissions than the accounting rules give them credit for.

## The Risk of Emissions Backfire

A critical finding of the general equilibrium analysis is the potential for a "backfire" effect, where an increase in the carbon offset price $\hat{s} > 0$ actually leads to an increase in aggregate emissions $\hat{Z} > 0$. The model identifies two primary mechanisms for this counterintuitive outcome:

*   **Fossil Fuel Complementarity:** If renewable energy and fossil fuel energy are highly complementary (low elasticity of substitution $\sigma_E$), an increase in renewable energy output can effectively "drag" along an increase in fossil fuel use to meet total energy needs. This is particularly relevant when the final consumption sector is highly flexible ($\sigma_X$ is high) and shifts toward energy-intensive production.
*   **Capital Allocation Shifts:** Under specific conditions, the economic restructuring caused by the subsidy and the tax can shift capital $K_X$ toward emissions-intensive processes in the final goods sector. If the emissions factor $\xi_X$ in the final goods sector is high enough, the increase in $K_X$ can outweigh the benefits of displacing fossil fuels in the energy sector.

While the model consistently shows that increasing the offset price increases clean energy $\hat{R} > 0$ and decreases consumption $\hat{X} < 0$, the ambiguity of $\hat{Z}$ underscores the complexity of environmental policy. It demonstrates that simply subsidizing "green" activities is not a guaranteed path to lower emissions if the broader market responses are not considered.

## Welfare and Policy Implications

Beyond emissions counting, the paper evaluates the welfare implications of carbon offsets. The authors argue that emissions reductions alone are not a sufficient statistic for determining whether a policy is beneficial to society. A welfare-improving policy must balance the environmental benefits (reduction in disutility from emissions) against the economic costs (reduction in consumption).

The study derives a welfare change formula based on the marginal environmental damage $\mu$, which relates the marginal utility of income $\lambda$ to the marginal disutility of emissions $Z$:

$$\mu \equiv -\frac{1}{\lambda} \frac{\partial U}{\partial Z}$$

Using the Marginal Value of Public Funds (MVPF), the researchers provide a benefit-cost ratio that incorporates general equilibrium effects. They find that carbon offsets often function as an imperfect subsidy for abatement. Unlike a Pigouvian tax, which directly prices the externality, offsets subsidize a clean input. This introduces distortions and requires revenue generation through other taxes, which can be costly.

The significance of this work lies in its call for a more nuanced approach to carbon policy. By proving that even perfectly "additional" offsets ($\phi = 1$) can suffer from leakage and over-crediting due to market interactions, the authors suggest that policymakers should focus on sectors where clean and dirty energy are high substitutes (high $\sigma_E$). In these sectors, the general equilibrium effects are most likely to align with the intended environmental goals. Furthermore, the findings suggest that the current focus on "additionality" might be misplaced; a policy could be welfare-improving even if it involves non-additional offsets, provided it effectively reduces aggregate emissions at a social cost lower than the marginal environmental damage.

In conclusion, this research provides a rigorous theoretical foundation for understanding how carbon offsets interact with the broader economy. It highlights that the success of climate policy depends not just on the integrity of individual projects, but on the elasticities, resource constraints, and price adjustments that characterize the modern economic landscape.
