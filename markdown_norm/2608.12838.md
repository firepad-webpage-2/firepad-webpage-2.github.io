The multivariate Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process is a central model in continuous-time stochastic dynamics, used extensively across finance, econometrics, and physics to represent systems that revert to a mean. Traditionally, statistical inference for these models assumes a fixed number of dimensions, but modern data often present high-dimensional challenges where the number of parameters grows faster than the observation time.

In the high-dimensional setting, the drift matrix $A_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ contains $d^2$ unknown entries. When the dimension $d$ is large, the empirical information available from a trajectory over a finite time $T$ is often insufficient to identify all parameters using standard methods like the Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE). This paper addresses the problem when the drift matrix is exactly or nearly low-rank—meaning the dynamics are driven by a small number of latent directions.

## The Challenge of Low-Rank Dynamics and Non-Ergodicity

A significant hurdle in estimating low-rank drift matrices is the resulting non-ergodicity. In a stable, ergodic OU process, all eigenvalues of $A_0$ have positive real parts, allowing the process to reach a stationary distribution. However, if $rank(A_0) = r < d$, the matrix necessarily has zero eigenvalues. This means the process does not revert to the mean in all directions; instead, it behaves like a Brownian motion in the null space of $A_0$.

From a statistical perspective, this non-ergodicity manifests as ill-conditioning of the empirical covariance matrix:
$$
C_T = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T X_t X_t^\top dt
$$
In directions where the process is non-stationary, the "signal" grows differently than in stable directions, often leading to a matrix $C_T$ that is nearly singular. Standard estimation techniques that rely on the inverse of $C_T$ become numerically unstable and theoretically difficult to bound. While previous research has focused on sparse drift matrices (where most entries are zero), this work develops a framework for when the matrix is low-rank, a structure common in cointegrated systems where a few long-run equilibrium relationships govern a larger set of variables.

## The Weighted Nuclear Elastic Net Estimator (WNEE)

To estimate a (near-) low-rank matrix while maintaining stability, the authors propose the Weighted Nuclear Elastic Net Estimator (WNEE). This estimator minimizes a regularized objective function that balances the data fit with penalties that encourage both stability and low rank.

The objective function is based on the normalized negative log-likelihood of the OU process, defined as the contrast function $L_T(A)$:
$$
L_T(A) = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T \langle AX_t, dX_t \rangle + \frac{1}{2T} \int_0^T \|AX_t\|_2^2 dt
$$
The WNEE is defined as the solution to:
$$
\hat{A}_{\lambda,\eta} \in \operatorname{argmin}_{A \in M_d} \left\{ L_T(A) + \frac{\eta}{2} \|A\|_F^2 + \lambda \|A B_{T,\eta}\|_* \right\}
$$
This formulation includes three key components:
1.  **Likelihood Contrast:** Represents the fit to the observed diffusion trajectory $X_t$.
2.  **Ridge Penalty:** The term $\frac{\eta}{2} \|A\|_F^2$ (using the Frobenius norm) provides $\ell_2$ regularization. This stabilizes the estimator in directions where the data is uninformative and ensures the weighting matrix is invertible.
3.  **Weighted Nuclear Norm:** The term $\lambda \|A B_{T,\eta}\|_*$ uses the nuclear norm (the sum of singular values) as a convex proxy for the rank. Crucially, the penalty is weighted by $B_{T,\eta} = (C_T + \eta I_d)^{1/2}$.

The use of the data-adaptive weight $B_{T,\eta}$ is a distinctive feature of this approach. It rescales the nuclear-norm penalty according to the empirical information content in the trajectory. This ensures that the regularization is stronger in directions where the process provides less information (low variance) and weaker where the signal is strong.

## Theoretical Oracle Inequalities

The authors provide non-asymptotic guarantees for the WNEE in the form of oracle inequalities. These inequalities bound the estimation error even when the true matrix $A_0$ is only approximately low-rank.

The first major result establishes a bound in the "empirical Fisher geometry." This metric measures error weighted by the empirical covariance, which is the natural geometry of the likelihood. Under a general framework where $A_0$ is diagonalizable with non-negative eigenvalues, the error satisfies:
$$
\|\hat{A}_{\lambda,\eta} - A_0\|_{B_{T,\eta}}^2 \leq C_1 a_{r,T,\eta}(A_0) + C_2 \lambda^2 r + C_3 \eta^2 \|A_0 B_{T,\eta}^{-1}\|_F^2
$$
This bound consists of three parts:
-   **Approximation Error:** $a_{r,T,\eta}(A_0)$ represents how well $A_0$ can be approximated by a rank-$r$ matrix.
-   **Statistical Complexity:** The term $\lambda^2 r$ reflects the cost of estimating $r$ latent directions. The tuning parameter $\lambda$ is chosen to dominate the noise term $\varepsilon_T = \frac{1}{T} \int_0^T dW_t X_t^\top$.
-   **Ridge Bias:** The term involving $\eta^2$ represents the bias introduced by the ridge stabilization.

To make these results more interpretable, the authors translate this weighted bound into a standard Frobenius-norm bound. This translation requires an "empirical curvature condition," which ensures that the likelihood has a minimum level of curvature in all directions. If $\lambda_{\min}(C_T + \eta I_d) \geq c_0$ for some $c_0 > 0$, then:
$$
\|\hat{A} - A_0\|_F^2 \leq \frac{C}{c_0} ( \lambda^2 r + \eta^2 \|A_0 B_{T,\eta}^{-1}\|_F^2 )
$$

## Symmetric Low-Rank Models

A significant portion of the paper is dedicated to verifying the curvature condition for a specific, well-structured model class: symmetric positive-semidefinite (PSD) drift matrices that are exactly low-rank. This model class is particularly relevant for systems with underlying symmetric interactions.

The authors prove that for such systems, the empirical curvature condition holds with high probability $1-\delta$ as long as the observation time $T$ is sufficiently large relative to the dimension $d$. By decomposing the process into its stable components (which behave like standard OU processes) and its non-stable components (which behave like Brownian motions), they show that the minimum eigenvalue of $C_T + \eta I_d$ remains bounded away from zero.

For this class, they derive an explicit squared Frobenius-norm error rate of:
$$
rd \log(T) / T
$$
This rate is significant because it matches the optimal scaling for rank-$r$ matrix estimation (where one expects an error proportional to the number of degrees of freedom $rd$ divided by the sample size $T$), with only an additional logarithmic factor. This confirms that the WNEE is statistically efficient even in the presence of non-ergodicity.

## Numerical Evidence

The theoretical findings are supported by comprehensive simulations. The authors compare the WNEE against the MLE, a standard Ridge estimator, and an unweighted Nuclear norm estimator.

## Significance and Impact

This work provides a robust framework for high-dimensional inference in a regime that was previously under-studied: the low-rank, non-ergodic setting. By moving beyond simple sparsity and addressing the specific challenges of continuous-time diffusions, the paper offers several contributions:
-   **Theoretical Innovation:** It establishes non-asymptotic oracle inequalities for nuclear-norm-based estimation in OU processes, accounting for the unique martingale noise structure of diffusions.
-   **Practical Stability:** The weighted elastic net formulation provides a numerically stable way to handle nearly singular covariance matrices without sacrificing the ability to recover low-rank structures.
-   **Foundational Convergence Rates:** The verification of the curvature condition for symmetric models provides a benchmark for the amount of data $T$ required to reliably estimate high-dimensional drift dynamics.

These results have broad implications for fields that utilize high-dimensional OU models, such as modeling the joint dynamics of large portfolios in finance or identifying regulatory signals in biological systems. The WNEE offers a path to uncovering the latent, low-dimensional forces that drive complex, high-dimensional stochastic systems.
