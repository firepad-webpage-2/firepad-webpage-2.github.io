In macroeconomics and finance, researchers often seek to understand how aggregate variables—such as market prices or total demand—respond to shocks. A significant challenge arises because these variables are often jointly determined in equilibrium, creating a "circularity" or endogeneity problem. To solve this, econometricians use instrumental variables (IVs) to isolate variation in one variable that is independent of the others.

Traditional IV methods rely on "excluded" instruments—external factors that affect the system but are not themselves part of the main equation. However, such instruments are often difficult to find. The Granular Instrumental Variables (GIV) approach, introduced by Gabaix and Koijen (2024), offers an alternative. It suggests that in economies where a few large "granular" units (like massive investment firms or major industrial sectors) dominate, the idiosyncratic shocks to these specific units can serve as instruments for the aggregate.

While the GIV concept is powerful, its practical application is complicated by unobserved "common factors"—shocks that affect all units simultaneously, such as a change in national interest rates. Valid GIVs must be constructed by removing these common factors from the granular units' data. This requires knowing the "factor-loading space," which describes how much each unit is affected by the common shocks. In practice, this space is almost always unknown. This paper addresses the fundamental problem of how to estimate and perform inference with GIVs when the factor-loading space is unknown, particularly in settings where the number of entities $n$ is small or fixed.

## The Core Principle: Identification via the Orthogonal Complement

The primary contribution of this work is the realization that one does not need to estimate the latent factors or their loadings directly to construct a valid GIV. Instead, the authors show that valid GIVs can be characterized by the "orthogonal complement" of the factor-loading space.

Consider a simplified system where $y_t$ is a vector of observed variables for $n$ entities at time $t$. These observations are driven by common shocks $\eta_t$ and idiosyncratic shocks $u_t$:

$$
y_t = \lambda \eta_t + u_t
$$

Here, $\lambda$ represents the factor loadings. A valid granular instrument must be a linear combination of $y_t$ that is uncorrelated with the common shocks $\eta_t$. Mathematically, if we find a matrix $A$ such that $A' \lambda = 0$, then $A' y_t = A' u_t$. Since $u_t$ is idiosyncratic (uncorrelated with the common shocks), $A' y_t$ serves as a valid instrument.

The authors demonstrate that this matrix $A$ is linked to the covariance structure of the data. By analyzing the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the observed covariance matrix, one can identify the space that is "left over" after accounting for the common factors. Specifically, the eigenvectors corresponding to the smallest eigenvalues of a transformed covariance matrix span the space orthogonal to the factor loadings. This allows researchers to construct the GIV matrix $\hat{A}$ directly from the data without requiring $n$ to be large, which is a significant departure from previous methods that relied on Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and large-$n$ asymptotics.

## A Feasible Estimation Framework

The paper outlines a rigorous step-by-step procedure for using these instruments in a Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) framework.

1.  **De-meaning and Transformation**: The data is first de-meaned using a projection matrix $M_{1n}$ to remove constant aggregate effects:
    $$
    M_{1n} = I_n - n^{-1} 1_n 1_n'
    $$
    The de-meaned variables $\tilde{y}_t = M_{1n} y_t$ still contain the factor structure:
    $$
    \tilde{y}_t = M_{1n} \lambda \eta_t + \tilde{u}_t
    $$
2.  **Covariance Analysis**: The authors analyze the population covariance matrix $\bar{\Sigma}_{\tilde{y}}$:
    $$
    \bar{\Sigma}_{\tilde{y}} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T E[\tilde{y}_t \tilde{y}_t'] = \tilde{\lambda} \left( \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T E[\eta_t \eta_t'] \right) \tilde{\lambda}' + \bar{\sigma}_u^2 M_{1n}
    $$
    They prove that the smallest eigenvalues of this matrix are equal to the average variance of the idiosyncratic shocks $\bar{\sigma}_u^2$, and the corresponding eigenvectors identify the necessary instrument space.
3.  **GIV Construction**: In practice, the sample covariance matrix $\hat{\Sigma}_y$ is used. A transformed matrix $\hat{S}_y$ is formed:
    $$
    \hat{S}_y = Q_{-1}' \hat{\Sigma}_y Q_{-1}
    $$
    The eigenvectors corresponding to the smallest eigenvalues of $\hat{S}_y$ (denoted $\hat{A}_0$) are used to build the feasible instrument:
    $$
    \hat{A} = Q_{-1} \hat{A}_0
    $$
4.  **GMM Estimation**: These estimated instruments are then plugged into a GMM objective function to estimate structural parameters $\theta$ (such as demand or supply elasticities):
    $$
    \min_{\theta} \bar{g}_T(\theta; \hat{A})' W \bar{g}_T(\theta; \hat{A})
    $$

This approach handles the uncertainty inherent in estimating the factor space. The paper provides the theoretical proofs for consistency and asymptotic normality, ensuring that the resulting estimates and their standard errors are statistically valid for a fixed $n$ as the time dimension $T$ grows.

## Addressing Identification Failures

A critical part of this research is its critique of existing GIV implementations. Specifically, the authors revisit Proposition 7 in Gabaix and Koijen (2024), which suggests a method for identifying factor loadings using specific moment conditions and normalizations.

The authors demonstrate that those previous conditions do not always uniquely identify the loadings. There can be multiple sets of loadings that satisfy the same mathematical conditions (rotational non-uniqueness), which makes the resulting GIVs potentially invalid. By focusing on the *orthogonal complement* (the space $A$ where $A' \lambda = 0$), this paper's method is invariant to the specific rotation or representation of $\lambda$. This makes the new framework more robust against identification failures.

## Empirical Application: The Equity Market Multiplier

To demonstrate the utility of their method, the authors apply it to a major question in finance: how much does the aggregate stock market price move in response to a demand shock? This is known as the "aggregate equity market multiplier."

Using data from 12 investor sectors (including households, mutual funds, and pension funds), they estimate demand elasticities. They find that applying GIV to all 12 sectors results in a "J-test" rejection. In econometrics, a J-test rejection indicates that the model's assumptions—specifically that the instruments are valid and the behavior is homogeneous—are likely incorrect.

When they narrow the focus to a "granular core" of the 6 largest sectors, the J-test no longer rejects the model. For this core group, they estimate a multiplier of approximately 9. This means that a \$1 demand shock leads to a \$9 change in the aggregate market value. This result is significant because it is much larger than what standard "frictionless" models predict (which usually suggest a multiplier near 0). It provides strong empirical support for the "inelastic markets hypothesis," which suggests that stock prices are highly sensitive to flows because investor demand is relatively unresponsive to price changes.

## Determining the Number of Factors

A practical hurdle in any factor-based model is deciding how many common factors ($r$) exist. If a researcher assumes too many or too few factors, the constructed instruments will be biased. 

The authors propose a selection rule based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). This rule helps researchers consistently estimate the rank of the factor-loading space from the data. Their simulation results show that this BIC-type criterion is highly effective, correctly identifying the number of factors over 97% of the time across various test scenarios.

## Significance of the Work

This paper bridges a gap between theory and practice in granular instrumental variables. Its significance stems from three main areas:

1.  **Small-Sample Reliability**: By removing the requirement for a large number of entities ($n \to \infty$), the method becomes applicable to many real-world datasets where the number of sectors, countries, or major firms is strictly limited.
2.  **Statistical Rigor**: The derivation of valid standard errors and the J-test allows researchers to perform hypothesis testing and model validation with confidence, accounting for the fact that the instruments themselves were estimated.
3.  **Economic Clarity**: The empirical findings regarding the equity market multiplier demonstrate that GIV, when applied correctly with proper inference tools, can resolve long-standing debates about market behavior and price sensitivity.

By providing a complete toolkit for estimation, inference, and specification testing, the authors have made the GIV approach a more reliable and versatile instrument for empirical researchers in economics and finance.
