Modern protein structure prediction models, such as Boltz-1 and AlphaFold3, have fundamentally changed biological research by accurately mapping amino acid sequences to three-dimensional atomic coordinates. However, these models often function as "black boxes," where the internal logic driving the transition from a linear sequence to a complex fold remains opaque. A central architectural feature of these models is the division between a representational "trunk" (the Pairformer) and a generative "diffusion module." The trunk processes biological context, while the diffusion module iteratively refines physical coordinates.

Researchers have begun to apply the tools of mechanistic interpretability—originally developed for large language models—to these biological predictors. By probing and steering the internal activations of Boltz-1, this study investigates how biological information is encoded at the boundary between representation and generation. The work identifies which features the model actively utilizes to build structures and which information is discarded as noise during the generative process.

## The Architectural Divide: Trunk vs. Diffusion

Boltz-1 operates through a two-stage process. First, the Pairformer trunk processes the input sequence and evolutionary information (Multiple Sequence Alignments) to produce two primary internal representations: a single-residue representation $s$ and a residue-pair representation $z$. These representations act as the "knowledge base" that conditions the second stage: the diffusion module. The diffusion module then uses this conditioning to transform random noise into structured atomic coordinates over several hundred sampling steps.

The transition from the trunk to the diffusion module represents a critical bottleneck. Information must be compressed and formatted in a way that the diffusion module can interpret to place atoms in space. The researchers focused their analysis on the per-residue activations at this boundary, extracting data from every second layer of the trunk (up to $L47$) and across the grid of diffusion layers (up to $L22$) and sampling steps.

## The Representational Split: Geometry vs. Chemistry

A key finding of this study is the emergence of a "representational split" at the trunk-diffusion boundary. By training linear probes—simple logistic regression models designed to predict biological features from internal activations—the authors evaluated what information is "decodable" at different stages of the model.

The Pairformer trunk was found to be a rich repository of both geometric and chemical information. It accurately represents geometric features like alpha-helices, beta-strands, and disordered regions, while also maintaining high-fidelity information about sequence chemistry, such as amino-acid identity, signal peptides, and disulfide bonds. In fact, amino-acid identity is almost perfectly decodable ($F1$ $\approx 1.00$) at the output of the trunk.

However, as information crosses into the diffusion module, a selective filtering process occurs. While geometric information is retained with high fidelity to guide coordinate generation, sequence-chemical information is significantly attenuated. For instance, the decodability of signal peptides drops from an $F1$ score of 0.76 in the trunk to 0.35 in the final diffusion layer. This suggests that the diffusion module primarily "cares" about the geometric constraints defined by the trunk, treating specific biochemical annotations as auxiliary information that is not strictly necessary for the physical task of atom placement.

## Probing with Sparse Autoencoders

To move beyond supervised probes, which require pre-labeled data, the researchers employed Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs). SAEs are unsupervised neural networks trained to decompose complex model activations into a sparse set of interpretable "features" or "latents." The goal is to see if the model's internal "neurons" naturally align with biological concepts like "helix-ness" or "cysteine-residue."

The study compared the effectiveness of these unsupervised SAE features against supervised linear probes. While SAEs successfully identified latents that correlated with biological concepts, the supervised probes consistently achieved higher $F1$ scores for known concepts. For example, in decoding secondary structure, probes outperformed the best single SAE features by a margin of 0.13 to 0.35.

This comparison highlights a trade-off in mechanistic interpretability: supervised probes are more effective for "reading" specific, known biological signals, while SAEs serve as a powerful tool for "hypothesis generation," potentially surfacing internal features that do not yet have formal biological labels.

## Causal Steering: Moving from Correlation to Control

A major contribution of this work is the distinction between what a model *knows* (decodability) and what it *uses* (causal influence). Just because a probe can predict a feature from an activation does not mean that activation is what the model uses to generate that feature. To test this, the researchers performed causal steering experiments.

They targeted the final single representation $s$ of the trunk. By adding a multiple of a "steering direction" $u$ (derived from a probe or SAE feature) to the activation $x$, they attempted to force the model to change its output. The intervention followed the formula:

$$x' = x + k \cdot \text{mean}|(x - \mu) \cdot u| u$$

where $k$ is a scaling factor (dose) and $\mu$ is the mean activation.

The results showed that the model is indeed steerable for certain features. Adding a "helix" direction dose-dependently increased the helix content in the final predicted structure without degrading the overall quality of the fold (measured by $pLDDT$). Similarly, steering for "coil" increased coil content. Interestingly, these two directions were found to be anti-parallel; increasing one naturally decreased the other, reflecting the physical trade-off in protein folding.

## The Strand Paradox and Causal Necessity

One of the most surprising findings was the "strand paradox." Despite beta-strands being highly decodable from the trunk activations ($F1$ $> 0.80$), they were completely unsteerable via the single representation $s$. Intervening with a "strand" direction failed to increase the number of beta-strands in the output structure.

This paradox suggests that while the model represents the *concept* of a beta-strand in its single-residue activations, the actual *instruction* to form a strand resides elsewhere—likely in the residue-pair representation $z$. Because beta-strands are defined by long-range interactions between non-adjacent residues, a single-residue modification is insufficient to trigger their formation.

Furthermore, ablation experiments (removing the signal instead of adding to it) revealed that the trunk signal for helices is "sufficient" to steer the model but not "necessary" for the diffusion module to generate them. When the trunk's helix signal was removed, the diffusion module often still produced helices, suggesting that the pair representation $z$ or other internal redundancies provide sufficient information for the fold.

## The Impact of Annotation Sparsity

A significant portion of the paper is dedicated to a critique of how interpretability is evaluated. Most biological datasets, like SwissProt, are "sparse"—they only label regions that have been experimentally verified. This creates a problem for interpretability: if a model correctly identifies a helix in an unlabelled region, a standard evaluation script will mark it as a "false positive," lowering the model's score.

The researchers demonstrated this by comparing the model's performance against sparse SwissProt labels versus dense labels generated by the DSSP algorithm (which labels every residue). The $F1$ scores for the exact same internal activations were drastically lower when using sparse labels. For example, the helix $F1$ score dropped from 0.85 (dense) to 0.42 (sparse).

This finding warns researchers that current benchmarks may be severely underestimating the amount of biological knowledge contained within AI models. It highlights the need for "dense" ground-truth datasets or more sophisticated evaluation metrics that account for missing labels.

## Methodological Lessons for the Field

Based on their findings across the trunk-diffusion boundary, the authors propose three methodological recommendations for future research in mechanistic interpretability:

1.  **Specify the Readout Class:** Researchers should avoid generic claims like "the model knows $X$." Instead, they should specify how $X$ was detected (e.g., "feature $X$ is linearly decodable via a logistic probe"). As shown by the strand paradox, linear decodability does not imply causal use.
2.  **Restrict Causal Conclusions:** Statements about causality must be specific to the site of intervention. The fact that beta-strands are not steerable in the single representation $s$ does not mean they are not steerable in the pair representation $z$.
3.  **Account for Label Sparsity:** When evaluating model interpretations against biological databases, researchers must include controls (such as self-consistency checks using the model's own predictions) to ensure that low performance metrics are not simply the result of incomplete experimental annotations.

By systematically probing Boltz-1, this work provides a clearer picture of how protein structure predictors transform biological sequences into 3D shapes. It reveals a model that selectively retains geometric constraints while discarding chemical details, and it underscores the importance of causal interventions in truly understanding the "logic" of artificial intelligence in biology.
