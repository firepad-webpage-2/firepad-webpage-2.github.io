## Introduction to Jacobi Permutations and Combinatorial Statistics

Enumerative combinatorics often seeks to classify and count specific types of permutations based on their internal structural properties. A central figure in this field is the sequence of Euler numbers $E_n$, which appear in the Taylor series expansion of the trigonometric functions tangent and secant:

$$\tan(x) + \sec(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} E_n \frac{x^n}{n!}$$

Historically, these numbers were shown to count alternating permutations—sequences where the values alternately rise and fall. Over time, other classes of permutations have been discovered that are also counted by Euler numbers, including André permutations (of types I and II), simsun permutations, and Jacobi permutations.

Jacobi permutations were originally introduced by Viennot in the study of Jacobi elliptic functions. Recently, research by Henke, Hoffman, Stephens, Yuan, and Zhuang explored refined enumerations of these permutations. They investigated how specific "statistics"—numerical properties of a permutation like the value of the last entry or the number of ascents—distribute across the set. Their work established that the distribution of the last letter of a Jacobi permutation is governed by Entringer numbers $E_{n,k}$, which are a refinement of the Euler numbers. Specifically, they found:

$$E_{n,k} = |\{\pi \in J_n \mid \text{last}(\pi)=k\}|$$

where $J_n$ is the set of Jacobi permutations of length $n$. However, several deeper relationships remained conjectural. This paper provides the definitive proofs for these conjectures, utilizing both explicit bijective mappings and analytical generating functions.

## The Role of Entringer Numbers and Refined Statistics

The Entringer numbers $E_{n,k}$ provide a way to break down the Euler number $E_n$ into smaller components. While $E_n$ counts all permutations in a class like $J_n$, $E_{n,k}$ counts those ending in a specific value $k$. One of the primary objectives of the paper is to prove "Conjecture 1.2," which asserts a symmetric joint distribution for two statistics on Jacobi permutations: the last letter, `last`, and a transformed version of the penultimate letter, `cpen`.

For a permutation $\pi$ of length $n$, the statistic `cpen` is defined as:

$$cpen(\pi) = n+1-\pi_{n-1} \text{ for } \pi \in J_n$$

The authors prove that for any $n \ge 2$, the number of Jacobi permutations with $cpen(\pi) = i$ and $last(\pi) = k$ is given by:

$$E_{n-1,n+1-i-k} = |\{\pi \in J_n \mid cpen(\pi)=i, last(\pi)=k\}|$$

This result is significant because it shows that the relationship between the final two elements of a Jacobi permutation is highly structured and mirrors the fundamental properties of Entringer numbers. By establishing this joint distribution, the authors also automatically confirm "Conjecture 1.1," which deals with the individual distribution of `cpen`.

## Bridging Permutations and Trees through Bijections

To prove these identities, the researchers do not simply rely on counting; they construct bijections. A bijection is a one-to-one correspondence between two sets that proves they have the same number of elements. In this work, the bijections often pass through intermediate structures known as increasing trees.

A common technique in combinatorics is the recursive decomposition of a permutation $\pi$ based on its minimum element. If we let $\min(\pi)$ be the smallest value in the permutation, we can write:

$$\pi = u \cdot \text{min}(\pi) \cdot v$$

where $u$ is the prefix and $v$ is the suffix. This structure allows the permutation to be represented as a binary tree where the root is $\min(\pi)$, and the subtrees are formed by applying the same logic to $u$ and $v$.

The authors utilize and extend several known mappings:
1. **The $\kappa$ Bijection:** Maps Jacobi permutations to "increasing even trees."
2. **The $\mu$ Bijection:** Connects these trees to sequences that encode alternating permutations.
3. **Jacobi and André Trees:** The authors define "Jacobi trees" and "André I trees" to serve as specialized binary tree representations that preserve specific statistics like the first and last letters and the set of ascent/descent bottoms.

By composing these mappings, the authors can transform a problem about Jacobi permutations into a problem about André permutations or alternating permutations, where the distributions might already be known or easier to calculate.

## The André-Entringer Distribution and Theorem 1.4

A major result of the paper is the proof of "Conjecture 1.3," formulated as Theorem 1.4. This theorem establishes an equidistribution between Jacobi permutations and André permutations of the first kind (André I). 

The researchers show that the joint distribution of the set of ascent bottoms (`Ascbot`) and the `last` letter in Jacobi permutations is identical to the joint distribution of the set of descent bottoms (`Desbot`) and the `first` letter in André I permutations. This identifies these pairs as "André–Entringer" statistics. 

To achieve this, the authors introduce a recursive transformation $\phi$ that operates on Jacobi trees. This transformation is designed to modify the local structure of the tree nodes to satisfy the "André condition" (which relates to the values of children in a binary tree) while carefully preserving the properties that correspond to the permutation statistics. This recursive approach ensures that the entire tree is transformed into an André tree while keeping the "Lefttop" and "first" values intact.

## Extending the Results to Simsun and André II Classes

The paper goes beyond Jacobi permutations to unify the "André-Entringer" statistics across other classes. Theorem 1.5 demonstrates that the distributions found in Jacobi and André I permutations also appear in André II and simsun permutations.

Specifically, the authors prove:
- The (descent count, first letter) on André I permutations matches the (descent count, $n+1 - \text{last letter}$) on André II permutations.
- This, in turn, matches the distribution of (descent count, $n - \text{last letter}$) on simsun permutations of length $n-1$.

These connections are established through a chain of bijections. For instance, a new bijection $\psi$ is constructed between André II trees and simsun trees. These results suggest that there is a deep, underlying combinatorial structure shared by all these Euler-number-counted objects, regardless of their different definitions.

## Analytic Approach: Trivariate Generating Functions

While the bijective proofs provide structural insight, the authors also provide an analytical solution to an open problem regarding the "André–Entringer distribution" on Jacobi permutations. They seek a closed-form formula for the trivariate exponential generating function $F(t; x, y)$.

This function is defined to track three properties: the number of ascents ($t$), the number of elements smaller than the last letter ($x$), and the number of elements larger than the last letter ($y$):

$$F(t; x, y) = \sum_{n \geq 1} \sum_{\pi \in J_n} t^{asc(\pi)} \frac{x^{L(\pi)} y^{R(\pi)}}{n!}$$

where $L(\pi) = last(\pi)-1$ and $R(\pi) = n - last(\pi)$. Using a recurrence relation for the number of Jacobi permutations $J_{n,k}(t)$ with $asc=t$ and $last=k$:

$$J_{n,k}(t) = \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \binom{k-1}{j} J_{j,j}(t) J_{n-j-1,k-j-1}(t)$$

the authors derive a system of differential equations. By solving these, they obtain the following elegant closed-form expression:

$$F(t; x, y) = \frac{(1 + \rho)e^{\rho y} + (\rho - 1)e^{\rho x}}{(1 + \rho) + (\rho - 1)e^{\rho(x+y)}}$$

where the variable $\rho$ is defined as:

$$\rho = \sqrt{1 - 2t}$$

This formula is a powerful tool. It allows researchers to extract specific counts for permutations of any length $n$ with any number of ascents and any specific last letter just by looking at the coefficients of the Taylor expansion of $F$.

## Conclusion and Significance

The work by Lin, Wen, and Yan provides a comprehensive resolution to several standing conjectures in the study of Jacobi permutations. By successfully linking Jacobi permutations to André and simsun permutations through sophisticated bijections, the authors have unified various refined enumerations under the "André–Entringer" umbrella.

The significance of this research lies in its dual approach. The bijective proofs reveal the "why"—showing the explicit structural transformations that make these sets equivalent. Meanwhile, the generating function results provide the "how"—offering a precise mathematical formula to calculate these distributions. These results not only deepen our understanding of Jacobi permutations but also demonstrate the power of tree-based bijections in modern enumerative combinatorics. The techniques developed here, particularly the recursive tree transformations, are likely to be applicable to other classes of permutations and combinatorial objects in the future.
