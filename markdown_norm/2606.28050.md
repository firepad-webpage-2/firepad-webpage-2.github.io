Large Language Models (LLMs) are no longer just engines for text generation; they are increasingly deployed as evaluators, critics, and reward models. Frameworks such as "LLM-as-a-Judge" utilize these models to score the outputs of other systems, while self-reflection loops allow models to critique and refine their own answers. These applications rest on a fundamental assumption: that judging the correctness of an answer is an inherently easier or more reliable task for an LLM than generating that answer from scratch.

However, recent research suggests this relationship is not always straightforward. Previous studies in open-domain settings often suffered from a "parametric-knowledge confound," where a model might be able to evaluate a fact it cannot freely recall, making it unclear whether the performance difference was due to task difficulty or memory retrieval. To address this, current research investigates task asymmetry within a controlled in-context Question Answering (QA) framework. By providing the model with all necessary information in a context passage, the study isolates the model's reasoning and synthesis abilities from its internal memory.

## The Mechanics of Generation and Evaluation

To rigorously compare generation and evaluation, the research defines two distinct tasks performed by the same model $L$. In the Generation Task ($T_{gen}$), the model is provided with a context $c$ and a question $q$, and is tasked with producing a concise answer $a$. In the Evaluation Task ($T_{eval}$), the model is presented with the same context $c$ and question $q$, along with the answer $a$ it previously generated. Its goal is to provide a binary judgment $y$—either "Correct" or "Incorrect."

The formal setup can be described as follows:

$$
T_{gen} \to \{c, q\} \implies a \approx a^*
$$

$$
T_{eval} \to \{c, q, a\} \implies y \in \{\text{Correct, Incorrect}\}
$$

where $a^*$ represents the ground truth gold answer. To ensure a consistent baseline, an "oracle" model (specifically GPT-4o) is used to verify the generated answers against the gold standard. This allows for the calculation of two primary metrics: Generation Accuracy ($GA$), the rate at which the model generates correct answers, and Evaluation Accuracy ($EA$), the rate at which the model's self-judgment matches the oracle's verdict.

$$
GA = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{I}(y^*_i = \text{Correct})
$$

$$
EA = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{I}(y_i = y^*_i)
$$

The performance gap is then quantified as $\Delta = EA - GA$. A positive $\Delta$ suggests that the model is better at judging than generating, while a negative $\Delta$ indicates that the model struggles to verify its own outputs even when they are correct.

## Measuring the Generation-Evaluation Gap

The study evaluated these metrics across four diverse QA benchmarks: SQuAD 2.0 (extractive), DROP (numerical reasoning), HotpotQA (2-hop reasoning), and MuSiQue (multi-hop reasoning). The results revealed that self-evaluation is not universally easier than generation. In fact, for models like Llama-3.1-8B and GPT-4o-mini, the gap $\Delta$ was negative for the majority of the datasets.

On extractive tasks like SQuAD 2.0, the gaps were relatively small, likely because the task requires minimal synthesis. However, on tasks involving complex reasoning, the results diverged significantly. In HotpotQA, Llama-3.1-8B showed a substantial negative gap of $-14.2$, indicating that the model often failed to recognize its own correct answers or incorrectly labeled them. Conversely, MuSiQue presented a unique case where the gap was positive ($+3.6$ to $+4.0$).

The research suggests that the difficulty of generation on MuSiQue—which requires 3 or 4 hops of reasoning—is so high that the model's generation accuracy hits a floor. In these instances, having the candidate answer $a$ present in the prompt acts as an "anchor," simplifying the task for the evaluator and allowing it to outperform the generator. This "candidate-answer-insulation" effect becomes more pronounced as the number of reasoning hops increases.

## Mechanistic Interpretation: The Attention Problem

To understand why LLMs often struggle with evaluation, the researchers turned to mechanistic interpretability, specifically analyzing the attention patterns of Llama-3.1-8B. By examining the last-token attention—the distribution of focus when the model predicts its first output token—they discovered a striking structural asymmetry.

In the generation task, the model typically directs a significant portion of its attention ($11\%$ to $21\%$) toward the context passage. It performs a "needle-in-a-haystack" lookup, concentrating heavily on tokens that match the candidate answer. However, in the evaluation task, this behavior changes drastically. The attention mass directed toward the context drops to between $1.4\%$ and $5.4\%$.

This finding suggests that when acting as a judge, the model does not "re-read" the context to verify the answer. Instead, it engages in what the authors call "candidate-anchored shallow verification." The model relies more on the structure of the prompt and the presence of the candidate answer than on the evidence provided in the source text. This de-allocation of attention to the context explains why evaluation accuracy often lags behind generation: the judge is simply not looking at the evidence as closely as the generator did.

## Task Transferability and Fine-Tuning

A final area of investigation was whether the abilities to generate and evaluate are shared or distinct within the model's parameters. Using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), the researchers fine-tuned models on generation tasks, evaluation tasks, and both combined.

The experiments revealed that fine-tuning can significantly shift the model's behavior, but often at the cost of introducing new biases. For instance, fine-tuning solely for evaluation (LoRA-Eval) often degraded generation performance. Conversely, fine-tuning for generation (LoRA-Gen) frequently led to an "acquiescence bias," where the model became overly prone to labeling any answer as "Correct." 

This suggests that the generation-evaluation asymmetry is an intrinsic property of the model's architecture and the way it processes in-context information. Simply providing more examples of evaluations through supervised fine-tuning does not necessarily teach the model to "reason better" as a judge; instead, it often just shifts the model’s internal decision threshold, leading to more "Correct" or "Incorrect" labels without a corresponding increase in true understanding.

## Significance and Practical Implications

The findings of this research have broad implications for the development of robust AI systems. The observation that LLMs often fail to judge better than they generate challenges the reliability of several popular paradigms:

1.  **LLM-as-a-Judge:** If models have a tendency to ignore the context or exhibit systematic biases during evaluation, using them to score other models may produce misleading leaderboards and benchmarks.
2.  **Self-Correction:** For a model to correct its own errors, it must first be able to identify them. If the evaluation accuracy is lower than the generation accuracy, self-correction loops may fail to improve output quality or even introduce new errors.
3.  **RLHF and Reward Modeling:** Reward models used in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback often rely on the evaluative capabilities of LLMs. If these models are prone to "shallow verification," the resulting preference signals may reward structural fluency over factual accuracy.

By highlighting the "context de-attention" phenomenon, this work points toward a potential path for improvement. Future architectures or prompting strategies might need to explicitly enforce "evidence-grounded evaluation," requiring the model to cite specific spans of the context before rendering a judgment. This would move the model away from shallow, candidate-anchored checks toward a more rigorous, independent verification process.

Ultimately, this study serves as a reminder that the ability to synthesize information (generation) and the ability to verify it (evaluation) are distinct cognitive-like processes in LLMs. Understanding the gaps between them is essential for building systems that are not only fluent but also reliably self-consistent.
