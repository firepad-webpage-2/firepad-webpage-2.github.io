## Understanding Secondary-Electron Emission in Helium Ion Microscopy

Helium Ion Microscopy (HIM) has become a powerful tool for high-resolution imaging and nanofabrication. Unlike traditional Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), which uses an electron beam, HIM utilizes a focused beam of helium ions ($He^+$). This provides several advantages, including a smaller beam spot size, a larger depth of field, and a high sensitivity to surface topography. The primary signal used for imaging in both SEM and HIM is the emission of secondary electrons (SEs). These are low-energy electrons ejected from the sample surface as a result of inelastic collisions with the primary beam.

In standard HIM configurations, the sample is thick enough to stop the ion beam entirely. Consequently, the detector only collects SEs emitted from the "top" surface—the side facing the incident beam. However, when the sample is an ultrathin membrane, such as molybdenum disulfide ($MoS_2$), the ion beam can pass through it. This creates a "transmission" geometry where SEs can be emitted from both the top (entrance) and bottom (exit) surfaces. 

This research investigates how the SE signal changes as a function of the thickness of suspended $MoS_2$ membranes. By correlating image brightness with independent thickness measurements, the study provides a detailed map of how energy deposition and electron transport behave in two-dimensional materials under ion irradiation.

## Experimental Design and Methodology

To study these effects, the researchers used exfoliated $MoS_2$ flakes. These flakes were suspended over the holes of a lacey-carbon copper grid, which is a standard support structure used in Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM). This setup ensures that the flakes are "freestanding" or suspended, allowing the ion beam to exit the material without hitting a substrate immediately.

The primary imaging tool was a Zeiss Orion NanoFab HIM operating at an accelerating voltage of 30 kV. To accurately quantify the thickness of the $MoS_2$ flakes, the researchers used Electron Energy-Loss Spectroscopy (EELS) in a separate scanning TEM (STEM). The thickness $t$ was calculated using the log-ratio formula:

$$t = \lambda \ln(I_t/I_0)$$

where $I_t$ is the total spectral intensity, $I_0$ is the intensity of the zero-loss peak, and $\lambda$ is the inelastic mean free path of the electrons in the material. For $MoS_2$ at the given electron energy, $\lambda \approx 132 \text{ nm}$. This provided a "ground truth" thickness for various regions of the flakes, ranging from approximately 10 nm to 200 nm.

In the HIM, images were taken with fixed detector settings (gain and black level). To account for fluctuations in the beam current, the researchers used an internal normalization method. The mean signal from the $MoS_2$ regions ($\bar{I}_{\text{MoS}_2}$) was divided by the signal from the bare lacey-carbon support ($\bar{I}_C$) within the same image:

$$I_n = \bar{I}_{\text{MoS}_2} / \bar{I}_C$$

This normalized intensity $I_n$ allows for a direct comparison of brightness across different flakes and imaging sessions.

## Deconstructing the Secondary Electron Signal

One of the central challenges in this work was determining exactly where the collected SEs were coming from. In a transmission setup, several sources can contribute to the final signal:
1.  **$SE1_{top}$:** Electrons generated and emitted from the surface where the beam enters.
2.  **$SE1_{bottom}$:** Electrons generated and emitted from the surface where the beam exits.
3.  **$SE3$:** Electrons generated when transmitted or scattered ions hit other parts of the microscope chamber or the sample holder below the membrane.

The researchers conducted clever "blocking" experiments to isolate these components. By applying copper tape to the back of the sample grid, they could physically block any electrons coming from the bottom surface or the chamber below. When the back was blocked, the signal became very weak and showed almost no dependence on thickness. This result was significant because it proved that the "top-surface" emission ($SE1_{top}$) is a relatively small and constant contributor.

Further experiments were performed to see if the signal was dominated by "instrumental" SEs ($SE3$). They placed additional copper surfaces below the stage to see if scattered ions hitting these surfaces would increase the brightness. While there was a small increase, it was not large enough to explain the primary signal. Consequently, the researchers concluded that the dominant source of the thickness-dependent brightness is $SE1_{bottom}$—electrons emitted from the exit surface of the $MoS_2$ membrane.

## The Thickness-Intensity Relationship

The most striking finding was the non-linear relationship between the $MoS_2$ thickness and the SE intensity. As the thickness of the membrane increased from 10 nm, the brightness also increased. It reached a maximum peak at a thickness between 40 and 55 nm. Beyond this point, the intensity began to decline, eventually leveling off as the flake became thick enough to stop the 30 kV helium ions entirely.

This peak exists because of the way helium ions deposit energy as they travel through matter. When the ions first enter the material, they travel at high velocity and primarily lose energy through electronic excitations. As they penetrate deeper, they slow down. According to the "stopping power" theory, ions actually deposit *more* energy per unit length as they slow down, up until a certain point (often related to the Bragg peak). 

However, for an electron to be detected as an SE, it must not only be generated but also escape the material. The probability of an electron escaping from a depth $z$ follows an exponential decay:

$$P(z) \propto e^{-z/\lambda_{\text{esc}}}$$

where $\lambda_{\text{esc}}$ is the escape depth. Because the signal is dominated by the bottom surface, the intensity is a reflection of the energy deposited near the exit interface. As the flake gets thicker (up to ~50 nm), the ions reaching the bottom surface have slowed down more and thus deposit more energy there, increasing the SE yield. If the flake gets too thick, the ions stop before they reach the bottom, and the exit-surface signal disappears.

## Modeling Asymmetric Escape Depths

To quantify these observations, the researchers compared their data to simulations using SRIM (Stopping and Range of Ions in Matter). SRIM calculates the electronic ionization profile, or the energy deposited by the ions at different depths. The total yield $\gamma$ is modeled as an integral of this ionization profile weighted by the escape probability:

$$\gamma = \int \left( \frac{dE}{dz} \right) C \exp \left( -\frac{z}{\lambda_{\text{esc}}} \right) dz$$

The researchers tested several models. The "Symmetric Model" assumed that the escape depth $\lambda_{\text{esc}}$ was the same for both the top and bottom surfaces (typically around 2 nm for most materials). This model failed to fit the data accurately.

The most successful model was the "Asymmetric Model." In this version, the entrance escape depth was kept at the standard 2 nm, but the exit escape depth ($\lambda_{back}$) was allowed to vary as a fitting parameter. The best fit occurred when $\lambda_{back} \approx 9.6 \pm 1.3 \text{ nm}$. This means that electrons or energy excitations are somehow able to reach the exit surface from much deeper within the material (nearly five times deeper) than they can at the entrance surface.

Several physical reasons might explain this asymmetry. In thin foils, high-energy "delta electrons" (recoil electrons) are kicked forward by the incoming ions, carrying energy toward the exit surface. However, at 30 kV, the maximum energy transfer is quite low (~40 eV), making it unlikely that delta electrons alone account for a 10 nm escape depth. Other possibilities include more efficient transport of low-energy excitations (like plasmons or electron-hole pairs) or crystal-lattice effects like "channeling," where ions travel between atomic planes with less resistance, depositing energy deeper than predicted by the amorphous models used in SRIM.

## Practical Significance and Impact

This work has both practical and theoretical implications for the nanotechnology community.

**1. In-situ Thickness Mapping:** 
Measuring the thickness of 2D materials usually requires slow, external methods like Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM). This research demonstrates that the grayscale intensity in a HIM image can serve as a proxy for thickness. By calibrating the brightness, researchers can now estimate the thickness of suspended membranes instantly while imaging. This is particularly useful for manufacturing membrane-based sensors or filters where specific thicknesses are required for device performance.

**2. Refining Ion-Solid Interaction Theory:**
The finding that the exit-surface escape depth is significantly larger than the entrance-surface depth suggests that standard models of SE emission are incomplete for ultrathin, anisotropic materials. The discrepancy between the EELS thickness scale and the SRIM depth scale (a factor of ~0.7) also indicates that current simulations may overestimate the "stopping power" of $MoS_2$ for low-velocity helium ions.

**3. Optimization of Nanofabrication:**
Understanding where energy is deposited is crucial for focused ion beam (FIB) processing. If energy deposition is concentrated near the exit surface of a membrane, it affects how the material is milled or modified. This study provides a quantitative framework for predicting these effects in suspended 2D systems.

By bridging the gap between fundamental ion physics and practical microscopy, this research offers a new way to characterize and utilize suspended nanomaterials, providing a deeper look at the complex dance between ions and electrons at the atomic scale.
