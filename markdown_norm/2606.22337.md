## The Tooling Gap in Economic Theory

In the field of economics, there is a pronounced asymmetry between the infrastructure available to empirical researchers and that available to theorists. Empirical economists benefit from standardized data cleaning packages, replication archives, and shared codebases that allow them to build upon existing work efficiently. In contrast, theoretical economists often start from scratch, deriving proofs and models on a "blank slate" with very little automated support.

While Large Language Models (LLMs) offer a potential solution to this problem by acting as mathematical assistants, they introduce a significant challenge: trust. LLMs are prone to "hallucinations," producing derivations that appear elegant and correct but contain subtle logical flaws. This work addresses this "trust bottleneck" by developing a structured methodology for using LLM agents in economic theory research, focusing specifically on how different verification architectures affect the reliability of the resulting theory.

## The Eigengrade Challenge: A Benchmark for AI Reasoning

To test these methodologies, the research focuses on a complex problem in mechanism design derived from the Gans-Kominers "eigengrade" model. This model addresses the issue of grade inflation and the difficulty of disentangling a student's ability from the difficulty of the courses they choose.

In this model, student performance $P_{ic}$ in course $c$ is defined by the student's ability $a_i$, the course difficulty $d_c$, and a noise term $\epsilon_{ic}$:

$$P_{ic} = a_i - d_c + \epsilon_{ic}$$

The "eigengrade" approach uses a graph Laplacian $L$ constructed from the bipartite graph of student enrollments and course selections, where $L = B^T B$. By applying the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse $L^+$, researchers can recover an estimate of student abilities $\hat{x}$:

$$\hat{x} = L^+ B^T p$$

The precision of these recovered abilities is fundamentally limited by the spectral properties of the enrollment graph. Specifically, the variance of the error is bounded by:

$$E ||\tilde{\alpha} - (a - \bar{a}\mathbf{1})||^2 \leq \sigma^2 \text{tr}(L^+)$$

This creates an "informational externality": every time a student chooses a course or an instructor assigns a grade, they affect the precision with which everyone else's ability is estimated. The challenge posed to the LLMs was to design a Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) or Pigouvian mechanism that internalizes this externality, incentivizing students and instructors to act in a way that maximizes the global informativeness of the grading system. This problem is non-trivial because it requires differentiating through a pseudoinverse and navigating the inherent tensions of budget-balanced mechanisms.

## Three Architectures for Theory Research

The core contribution of this work is the evaluation of three distinct methods for organizing LLM agents to conduct theoretical research, each offering a different level of verification.

### Method 1: The Single Disciplined Pass
In this approach, a single high-capability model acts as both the proposer and the critic. It is instructed to follow a strict mathematical standard: showing every intermediate algebraic step, signing all derivatives, and explicitly flagging any logical gaps or conjectures. 

While this method produces highly "polished" and readable LaTeX documents, its primary weakness is internal consistency. It can only identify errors that it is capable of recognizing itself. If the model is fundamentally mistaken about a mathematical property, it will confidently report the error as truth. In the test case, this method produced a student-side enrollment mechanism but failed to perform any numerical verification, making it the most aesthetically pleasing but least rigorously checked output.

### Method 2: The Adversarial Prover-Verifier Pair
This method introduces a second, independent model to serve as an adversary. While the first model proposes results, the second model is tasked with refuting them by finding counterexamples, identifying boundary conditions, or challenging the logic. A human researcher acts as a triager, investigating the flags raised by the adversary.

For the eigengrade problem, this method proved superior at catching subtle errors. For instance, the adversary successfully refuted an initial claim that a certain mechanism was "ex-post incentive-neutral," showing instead that the property only held in expectation. It also forced the removal of an incorrect claim about budget balance, leading to the discovery of a more honest impossibility result.

### Method 3: The Structured Multi-Agent Project
This is the most complex architecture, mimicking a human research team. It includes specialized agents for different roles: a coordinator to manage sub-goals, a literature reviewer to map existing theorems, a "prover" to derive results, a "coder" to run numerical simulations, and a "reviewer" to gate all progress.

The defining feature of this method is the "Explicit Gap Ledger." Every unproven claim must be marked with a specific tag (e.g., `\unproven{}`). The reviewer agent has the power to reject entire sub-projects if they fail to meet the required rigor. For example, during the eigengrade test, the reviewer rejected a sub-goal involving "peer prediction" because the statistical logic was flawed. This resulted in a final document that appeared "ragged" with many flags but was actually the most trustworthy, as it clearly delineated what had been verified and what remained an open question.

## Key Findings: Convergent Discovery and the Illusion of Polish

By applying these three methods to the same economic problem, the researcher identified several recurring phenomena that have implications for how AI should be used in academia.

### Convergent Discovery
One of the most striking results was that two independent runs (Method 1 and Method 3) arrived at the exact same mathematical object: an "externality kernel" based on effective resistance in the enrollment graph. The marginal social informativeness of an enrollment or grading decision was independently derived as:

$$\frac{\partial W}{\partial w_e} = ||\Pi L_W^+ b_e||^2$$

This "convergent discovery" acts as a form of cheap replication. When two different AI architectures starting from different perspectives arrive at the same non-obvious formula, it significantly increases confidence that the mathematical object is a fundamental property of the model.

### The Decoupling of Polish and Rigor
The research highlights a counterintuitive relationship: the more "finished" an AI-generated paper looks, the less it should be trusted. Method 1 produced a beautiful, error-free-looking PDF, but it was riddled with unverified assumptions. Method 3 produced a document filled with "unproven" flags and rejected sub-goals. 

In a traditional human context, a polished draft signals effort and careful checking. In the LLM context, polish is a commodity that the model provides regardless of correctness. Therefore, the true indicator of rigor in AI-assisted theory is not the quality of the prose, but the presence of a "verification infrastructure"—a visible record of adversarial checks, numerical simulations, and a transparent list of logical gaps.

### The Value of Numerical Cross-Checking
Across all methods, numerical simulations played a critical "load-bearing" role. While LLMs are excellent at symbolic manipulation, they can easily lose track of the economic meaning of a formula. By requiring the agents to write Python code to simulate the mechanisms they just derived, the researcher created an objective ground truth. If the analytical derivation claimed that a strategy was "dominant," but the numerical simulation showed a student could gain by deviating, the error was immediately exposed.

## Implications for the Future of Economic Theory

This research suggests that LLMs can indeed accelerate the early stages of theoretical research, but only if they are embedded in a "disciplined research frontier." The goal is not to have an AI generate a complete, certified theorem from scratch. Instead, the AI provides a "verified core" of preliminary results and a structured map of the remaining challenges.

The paper proposes a new standard for AI-assisted work:
1. **Adversarial Scrutiny:** All major claims must survive a refutation attempt by an independent model.
2. **Explicit Obligation Ledgers:** All logical gaps must be labeled, shifting the focus from what is claimed to what is actually proven.
3. **Numerical Anchoring:** Symbolic results should be cross-checked with Monte Carlo simulations or numerical audits whenever possible.

By moving away from using LLMs as simple "answer engines" and toward using them as "agentic research teams" with built-in skepticism, theorists can overcome the trust bottleneck and create a more robust infrastructure for economic research. This work provides the "toolbox" (released as reusable AI skills) to make this disciplined approach operational for other researchers.
