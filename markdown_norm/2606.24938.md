## Understanding Multicellular Programs in Single-Cell Biology

Population-scale single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has transformed our ability to observe the immune system, allowing researchers to see how thousands of individual cells react to disease, treatment, and genetic variation. A key concept emerging from this field is the "multicellular program"—a coordinated axis of transcriptional variation that spans multiple cell types within an individual. For example, a systemic immune response to a virus might involve specific gene activation in both T cells and monocytes simultaneously. Identifying these programs is essential for stratifying patients, predicting disease outcomes, and understanding the fundamental biology of immune coordination.

Currently, the most effective way to discover these programs is through tensor decomposition methods, such as interpretable tensor decomposition (scITD). However, a significant barrier exists: patient-derived single-cell data is highly sensitive and subject to strict data governance and privacy regulations. Large-scale studies often require data from multiple institutions, but pooling raw, donor-level single-cell data into a central repository is frequently prohibited by legal and ethical constraints. This creates a "data silo" problem where important biological insights remain hidden within individual institutions because the data cannot be merged for analysis.

Federated learning (FL) offers a solution by allowing models to be trained across decentralized data sources without the data ever leaving their original location. This research introduces a federated estimator for multicellular tensor decomposition. By developing methods that can handle both shared features across sites and partitioned features (where different sites profile different cell types), the authors enable the discovery of global immune programs while adhering to privacy requirements. This work ensures that the resulting federated models are as accurate as those trained on centralized data, while providing quantifiable protections against data leakage.

## The Technical Framework: From Centralized to Federated Tensor Decomposition

To understand how the federated approach works, it is first necessary to look at the centralized process. The raw data for a study involving $D$ donors, $C$ cell types, and $G$ genes is represented as a 3D tensor $T \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times C \times G}$. Each entry in this tensor represents the "pseudobulk" expression of a specific gene in a specific cell type for a specific donor.

In the standard scITD approach, this 3D tensor is "unfolded" into a large 2D matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times CG}$ by flattening the cell-type and gene dimensions. A rank-$K$ decomposition is then performed to find the top-$K$ right singular vectors $V \in \mathbb{R}^{CG \times K}$. These vectors represent the multicellular programs. The donor-specific strengths of these programs, known as donor scores, are calculated as $U = (X - \bar{x})V \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times K}$.

The researchers developed two primary schemes to achieve this in a federated environment:

### The Complete-Panel Scheme (Horizontal FL)
In this scenario, every institution (or "site") profiles the same set of cell types and genes, but for different groups of donors. To avoid sharing raw data, the sites follow a coordinated protocol:
1.  **Global Centering:** The sites first collaborate to calculate a global mean $\bar{x}$ without sharing individual data points. The coordinator collects the local donor counts $n_s$ and local means $\bar{x}_s$ to compute:
$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{D} \sum_{s=1}^{S} n_s \bar{x}_s$$
2.  **Local Subspace Computation:** Each site $s$ locally centers its data and performs a partial Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). Instead of sending the full data, it only sends a scaled, low-dimensional representation of its local loadings $M_s = \Sigma_s^{(K)} V_s^{(K)T} \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times CG}$.
3.  **Global Merge:** The coordinator stacks these local representations and performs a final SVD to recover the global program basis $V_{fed}$. This "stacked-SVD" approach is mathematically designed to be equivalent to the centralized result.

### The Cell-Type-Incomplete Scheme (Vertical FL)
A more complex challenge occurs when different sites profile different cell types for the *same* group of donors. For instance, Site 1 might have data on B cells and T cells, while Site 2 has data on monocytes and NK cells for the same patients. Standard federated PCA cannot handle this "vertical" partitioning of features.

The authors address this by using a "donor-mode coupling" strategy. Each site computes a block of the Gram matrix $G_s = (X_s - \bar{x})(X_s - \bar{x})^T$. The coordinator then sums these blocks to get the full Gram matrix:
$$G = \sum_{s=1}^{S} G_s = (X - \bar{x})(X - \bar{x})^T$$
By performing an eigendecomposition on $G$, the coordinator can recover the donor scores $U$, which are then used to pull the corresponding program loadings from each site. This method guarantees an exact algebraic recovery of the centralized program.

## Privacy and Security: Quantifying the Shield

A primary motivation for federated learning is privacy, but merely keeping data local does not guarantee that no information is leaked. A "curious" coordinator could theoretically use the received model updates to infer whether a specific individual was part of a dataset—a risk known as a Membership Inference Attack (MIA).

The researchers quantified this risk by calculating a "reconstruction-residual" $\rho(x)$ for a target donor $x$:
$$\rho(x) = ||(x - \bar{x}) - (x - \bar{x})VV^T||_2^2$$
If a donor was part of the training set, their residual $\rho(x)$ will typically be smaller because the model was optimized to fit their data. The study found that releasing per-site subspaces without protection led to significant leakage (MIA-AUC of 0.906).

To mitigate this, the authors integrated cryptographic techniques:
*   **Secure Aggregation:** By using secure aggregation, the coordinator only sees the sum of the updates from all sites, not the individual updates. This reduced the leakage to an MIA-AUC of 0.609, which is much closer to the "zero-leakage" baseline of 0.5.
*   **Homomorphic Encryption (HE) and Multiparty Computation (MPC):** For the cell-type-incomplete scheme, the Gram matrix $G$ is highly sensitive. The authors propose performing the summation and subsequent eigendecomposition within a secure computation environment (HE or MPC). This ensures the coordinator only learns the final program loadings, maintaining the same level of privacy as the complete-panel scheme.

## Experimental Validation: Accuracy and Robustness

The framework was tested on five major single-cell atlases, including Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE), COVID-19, and Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD). The results confirmed that the federated estimator is "statistically lossless"—meaning it performs as well as a centralized model.

In the SLE atlas, the centralized method identified a multicellular program strongly linked to the type-I interferon response, reaching a donor-separation AUC of 0.958. The federated estimator achieved the exact same AUC. More importantly, the federated method proved robust to common real-world problems:
*   **Label Skew:** In federated settings, different sites often have different proportions of healthy vs. diseased donors. The authors showed that their global-mean centering strategy prevents these imbalances from biasing the results. Even in an adversarial scenario where one site had 0% cases and another had 90% cases, the federated program remained accurate.
*   **Ancestry Imbalance:** The model successfully recovered the same immune program even when donors were split by ancestry across different sites.
*   **Real-world Site Heterogeneity:** Using a COVID-19 dataset with three distinct physical collection sites, the federated estimator recovered the severity-linked program with a 98.9% correlation to the centralized version, despite differences in sample sizes and processing across the sites.

## The Utility Map: When does Multicellularity Matter?

While the federated method works well, the researchers also asked a fundamental question: when is identifying a multicellular program actually better than just looking at the single "best" cell type? They created a "utility map" across different tissues and diseases to answer this.

They found that the benefit of multicellular analysis is highly tissue-dependent:
*   **High Advantage:** In Interstitial Lung Disease (ILD) and certain liver diseases, multicellular programs showed a significantly higher predictive power than any single cell type alone. In ILD, the multicellular program achieved an AUC of 0.959, compared to 0.910 for the best single compartment. These diseases involve complex coordination across dozens of cell types.
*   **Parity:** In blood-based immune responses like SLE or COVID-19, a single cell type (like classical monocytes) often carries most of the signal. In these cases, the multicellular program is "at parity"—it performs just as well as the best single cell type but does not necessarily exceed it.

This map helps researchers decide when the extra effort of federated tensor decomposition is most likely to yield new biological insights.

## Broader Implications and Future Research

One of the most promising applications of this work is the ability to perform cross-disease meta-analyses. The authors demonstrated this by federating data from lupus, COVID-19, and IBD as separate sites. They successfully recovered a shared interferon-type program that was relevant across all three diseases, identifying a common immune axis without ever pooling the data.

Looking forward, this framework provides a blueprint for large-scale, privacy-preserving collaborations in single-cell genomics. While the current implementation relies on secure aggregation and empirical privacy testing, future iterations could integrate formal differential privacy as cohort sizes grow. Additionally, the vertical federation scheme (cell-type-incomplete) opens the door for integrating different types of data—such as combining RNA sequencing from one site with protein expression or spatial data from another—to create even richer multicellular maps.

By solving the data-silo problem through federated learning, this research enables the biomedical community to harness the full power of global single-cell atlases, leading to a deeper and more inclusive understanding of human health and disease.
