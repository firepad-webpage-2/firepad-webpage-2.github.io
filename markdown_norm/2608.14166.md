## Statistical Complexity and the Concept of Emergence

In the study of dynamical systems, a fundamental goal is to describe the long-term statistical behavior of orbits. When we iterate a function $f$ on a space, such as the unit circle $S^1$, we often observe that the history of a point $x$ becomes representative of some underlying probability measure. However, a major challenge in ergodic theory is that different starting points might lead to vastly different statistical futures. The concept of "emergence," as proposed by Berger, provides a quantitative framework for this complexity. 

High emergence describes systems where the variety of possible statistical outcomes is so vast that they cannot be easily summarized by a few measures. Conversely, low emergence suggests a level of "tameness" or coherence, where a small number of measures can effectively approximate the long-term behavior of almost every starting point. This research by Costa and Santiago focuses on $C^2$ immersions of the circle—smooth maps that may wrap around the circle multiple times and are not necessarily reversible, but contain no "stalling" points (critical points where the derivative is zero).

The authors demonstrate that all such $C^2$ immersions exhibit low emergence with respect to the standard Haar (Lebesgue) measure. This result is significant because it shows that despite the potential for chaotic motion, the global statistical profile of these one-dimensional systems remains fundamentally manageable. While previous studies focused on simpler homeomorphisms or maps with critical points, this work bridges the gap for non-invertible smooth maps, providing a definitive classification of their emergence properties.

## Quantifying Statistical Complexity

To rigorously define emergence, the authors utilize the notion of empirical measures. For a starting point $x$ and a map $f$, the empirical measure at time $n$ is defined as the average of Dirac masses along the orbit:

$$e_n(x) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \delta_{f^j(x)}$$

As $n$ tends to infinity, these measures may accumulate at certain points in the space of probability measures. The set of all such accumulation points is denoted by $A_f(x)$, representing the potential "asymptotic statistical profiles" of the orbit starting at $x$. 

To compare these profiles, the authors employ the Kantorovich–Wasserstein-1 distance, often called the "earth mover's distance." For two measures $\mu$ and $\nu$, $W_1$ measures the minimum work required to transform one distribution into another:

$$W_1(\mu, \nu) = \inf_{\gamma \in \Gamma(\mu, \nu)} \int_{S^1 \times S^1} d(x, y) d\gamma(x, y)$$

Emergence, $E_\mu(f)(\epsilon)$, is then defined as the minimum number of probability measures needed to form an $\epsilon\text{-net}$ for the asymptotic measures of a set of points with total measure $1 - \epsilon$:

$$E_\mu(f)(\epsilon) = \inf \{ N \in \mathbb{N} : \exists \nu_1, \dots, \nu_N \in \mathcal{P}(S^1) \text{ s.t. } \mu(\{ x : A_f(x) \subset \cup_{i=1}^N B_{W_1}(\nu_i, \epsilon) \}) > 1 - \epsilon \}$$

The complexity of the system is captured by the order of emergence $OE_\mu(f)$, which measures how fast the number of required measures grows as the precision $\epsilon$ increases:

$$OE_\mu(f) = \limsup_{\epsilon \to 0} \frac{\log E_\mu(f)(\epsilon)}{- \log \epsilon}$$

A system has low emergence if its order of emergence is zero, meaning the growth of required measures is sub-polynomial relative to $1/\epsilon$. The core of the paper is proving that for every $C^2$ immersion of the circle, this value is indeed zero.

## The Case of Circle Homeomorphisms

The analysis begins with circle homeomorphisms, which are maps of degree one. These are invertible transformations that preserve the circular structure. The statistical behavior of these maps depends heavily on their "rotation number."

For homeomorphisms with an irrational rotation number, the system is uniquely ergodic. This is a very strong form of simplicity: regardless of the starting point, the empirical measures $e_n(x)$ always converge to a single invariant measure. In this scenario, $E_\mu(f)(\epsilon) = 1$ for all $\epsilon$, and the emergence is trivially zero.

The situation is more nuanced for maps with a rational rotation number. In these cases, the empirical measures don't necessarily converge to a single point but rather to averages along the orbits of points on a "limit curve" in the space of measures. The authors show that the length of this curve in the $W_1$ metric is finite. Because a curve of finite length can be covered by a number of balls that grows only linearly with $1/\epsilon$, the set of asymptotic measures remains "small" in a topological sense, ensuring low emergence. Similar arguments are applied to orientation-reversing homeomorphisms, where empirical measures typically accumulate on 2-cycles of Dirac masses.

## Mañé’s Dichotomy and the Julia Set

Moving beyond homeomorphisms, the authors address immersions with degree $|deg f| \geq 2$. These maps are non-invertible and can exhibit much more complex behavior. The research relies heavily on the work of Ricardo Mañé, who established a fundamental dichotomy for such maps based on the Julia set $J(f)$.

The Julia set $J(f)$ can be thought of as the "chaotic core" of the map. The complement of the Julia set consists of "plateaux"—open intervals where the dynamics eventually collapse to a point when compared to a standard linear map like $z \mapsto z^d$. Mañé proved that for $C^2$ immersions, either the Lebesgue measure of the Julia set is zero ($Leb(J(f)) = 0$), or the Julia set is the entire circle ($J(f) = S^1$).

In the first case, where $Leb(J(f)) = 0$, almost every point $x$ eventually lands in a plateau. The dynamics on these plateaux are essentially equivalent to interval homeomorphisms. Since the authors already established low emergence for homeomorphisms, and since there are only finitely many such "periodic" plateau cycles, the global emergence remains low. The variety of statistical behaviors is limited by the fixed points or periodic cycles within these plateaux.

## Addressing High-Degree Immersions and Indifferent Points

 The most challenging case occurs when the Julia set is the entire circle ($J(f) = S^1$). In this regime, chaos is pervasive, and a simple reduction to homeomorphisms is impossible. To handle this, the authors invoke an entropy formula that relates the measure-theoretic entropy $h_\mu(f)$ of an invariant measure $\mu$ to the average rate of expansion (the Lyapunov exponent):

$$h_\mu(f) = \int \log |Df| d\mu$$

This formula is a cornerstone of partially hyperbolic theory. The authors show that for a set of points with full Lebesgue measure, any asymptotic measure $\mu$ must satisfy this formula. This allows for a classification of the possible ergodic components of $\mu$.

A critical role is played by "indifferent" periodic points—points $p$ where the derivative $|Df(p)| = 1$. These points are neither expanding nor contracting and can cause the empirical measures to "drift" or fail to converge. If the system has physical measures (measures that attract the orbits of a large volume of points), the emergence is low because most points will converge to those measures. If physical measures do not exist, the system might seem to have the potential for high emergence. 

However, the authors prove a key contradiction: if a system lacks physical measures but is a $C^2$ immersion, then for almost every point, the only possible components of its asymptotic measures are Dirac masses supported on the finite set of indifferent periodic points. 

## Synthesis of Results and Theoretical Implications

The final proof of Theorem 1 synthesizes these distinct cases. By examining the set of indifferent periodic points $P$, the authors show that any asymptotic measure must lie within the convex hull of Dirac masses supported on $P$: $\text{conv}(\{\delta_p : p \in P\})$. Because $P$ is a finite set, this convex hull is a finite-dimensional simplex in the space of measures. The covering number of such a simplex grows only polynomially with $1/\epsilon$.

The logical flow can be summarized as follows:
1.  **Iterates:** If an iterate $F = f^N$ has low emergence, then $f$ does too.
2.  **Convergence:** If the set of points where empirical measures converge ($C_f$) has full measure, emergence is minimal ($E=1$).
3.  **Dichotomy:** If convergence does not occur for most points ($\#A_f(x) > 1$ for $\mu$-a.e. $x$), the entropy formula and Mañé's contraction results force the asymptotic measures to be combinations of Dirac masses on indifferent points.
4.  **Compactness:** In all scenarios, the set of possible limit behaviors $A_f(x)$ for almost all $x$ is contained within a compact set with polynomial covering numbers.

This result confirms that $C^2$ immersions of the circle are not "wild" enough to produce high emergence. The study differs from current approaches by successfully integrating Mañé's structural results for one-dimensional maps with modern entropy formulas to provide a global guarantee of statistical simplicity. While maps with critical points (like the quadratic map) can hide high emergence in the way orbits linger near points with zero derivative, the absence of such points in immersions creates a structural regularity that forces low emergence. This work provides a significant step toward a complete classification of the complexity of one-dimensional dynamical systems.
