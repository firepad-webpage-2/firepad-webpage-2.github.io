## Addressing Asymmetry in Option Pricing Frameworks

The foundations of modern finance, particularly the Bachelier and Black–Scholes–Merton (BBSM) models, are built on the assumption of symmetric asset price movements. These models typically employ a standard Brownian motion to represent the random fluctuations of market prices. While this approach provides analytical tractability and a clear framework for no-arbitrage pricing, it frequently fails to account for empirical realities observed in financial markets. Markets often exhibit directional bias, where returns are skewed (typically negatively in equities), and volatility is asymmetric, giving rise to the well-known "volatility smile" or "smirk" in option prices.

Research by Gnawali et al. introduces a principled extension to the BBSM paradigm by integrating asymmetry directly into the driving stochastic processes. Rather than relying solely on the introduction of jumps or purely stochastic volatility—common techniques used to fix the shortcomings of Black-Scholes—this work leverages the Cherny–Shiryaev–Yor invariance principle (CSYIP). This principle allows for the construction of asymmetric diffusions that emerge as the continuous-time limits of biased random walks. By embedding path-dependent features like local time and state-dependent drift directly into the model, the researchers provide a framework that captures complex risk behaviors while maintaining the theoretical rigor of arbitrage-free valuation.

## The Foundations of Asymmetric Diffusion

The core theoretical innovation of this work involves moving beyond the standard Brownian motion $B_t$ toward processes that incorporate directional sensitivity. A fundamental building block in this framework is the Asymmetric Brownian Motion (ABM). One specific form explored is a process $A^{(\alpha)}_t$ defined as:

$$A^{(\alpha)}_t = B_t + \alpha \int_0^t \text{sgn}(B_s)dB_s$$

In this construction, the term $\int_0^t \text{sgn}(B_s)dB_s$ introduces a perturbation that depends on whether the underlying Brownian motion is positive or negative. This is closely related to the concept of "local time," which measures the amount of time a process spends near a specific state (often zero). By utilizing the Cherny–Shiryaev–Yor invariance principle, the researchers show that these continuous-time asymmetric diffusions are the natural limits of discrete random walks where the steps taken depend on the current position of the walker.

The generalized version of this process, denoted as $Z^{(\beta)}_t$, incorporates multiple asymmetric components:

$$Z^{(\beta)}_t = B_t + \beta_1 X^{(CSY)}_t + \beta_2 L_t + \beta_3 Y^{(CSY)}_t$$

Here, $L_t$ represents the local time at the origin, and $X^{(CSY)}_t$ and $Y^{(CSY)}_t$ are path-dependent integrals that account for skewness and excursions. This formulation allows practitioners to tune parameters $\gamma_-$, $\gamma_L$, and $\gamma_+$ to explicitly control the behavior of the process during negative excursions, its "stickiness" at zero, and its behavior during positive excursions, respectively. This structural flexibility enables the model to match the non-Gaussian characteristics of real-world asset returns without abandoning the diffusion-based backbone of classical finance.

## Geometric Asymmetric Brownian Motion (GABM)

To apply these asymmetric processes to equity markets, the research develops a Geometric Asymmetric Brownian Motion (GABM). The risky asset price $S_t^{(h)}$ is modeled as:

$$S_t^{(h)} = S_0 \exp(\nu t + \sigma B_t + \gamma C_t)$$

where the asymmetric perturbation is captured by the stochastic integral $C_t$:

$$C_t = \int_0^t h(B_s)dB_s$$

In this model, $h(x)$ is a piecewise-continuous function that determines how the state of the underlying driver $B_t$ influences the asset's growth and volatility. The presence of $h(B_s)$ makes the volatility of the asset price path-dependent. Unlike traditional local volatility models where volatility is a function of the asset price itself, here volatility is a function of the underlying Brownian state $B_s$. This distinction is crucial; it implies that two paths with the same current price might have different instantaneous volatilities if their historical trajectories relative to the origin differ.

Applying Itô's lemma to this price process reveals the dynamics:

$$dS_t = S_t \left[ \left(\nu + \frac{1}{2}(\sigma + \gamma h(B_t))^2\right)dt + (\sigma + \gamma h(B_t))dB_t \right]$$

This expression shows that the "local volatility" $\sigma + \gamma h(B_t)$ is state-dependent, allowing for an endogenous generation of return skewness and heavy tails.

## Option Pricing and the Market Price of Risk

The introduction of asymmetry does not break the requirement for a no-arbitrage environment. The researchers demonstrate that because the model relies on a single Brownian driver $B_t$, the market remains complete. To price derivatives, they identify the market price of risk $\theta_t$:

$$\theta_t = \frac{\nu + \frac{1}{2}(\sigma + \gamma h(B_t))^2 - r}{\sigma + \gamma h(B_t)}$$

where $r$ is the risk-free rate. By defining a new process under the risk-neutral measure $Q$ such that $dB^Q_t = dB_t + \theta_t dt$, the discounted asset price becomes a martingale. The value of a European option $v(t, b)$ then satisfies a specific parabolic partial differential equation (PDE):

$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 v}{\partial b^2} + \frac{r - \nu - \frac{1}{2}(\sigma + \gamma h(b))^2}{\sigma + \gamma h(b)} \frac{\partial v}{\partial b} - rv = 0$$

A significant finding here is that the option price is a function of the state $b$ of the Brownian motion, not just the asset price $S_t$. This highlights the fundamental path-dependence of the model. The filtration of the market includes the full history of the Brownian path, meaning that for perfect replication, a hedger must track the underlying driver's position relative to its starting point.

## Discrete-Time Binomial Approximations

While continuous-time theory provides the foundation, practical application requires numerical efficiency. The researchers develop a discrete-time binomial lattice that approximates the GABM. Standard binomial trees (like Cox-Ross-Rubinstein) use fixed up and down moves. In this asymmetric framework, the local volatility $\eta_{k,\Delta t}$ varies based on the cumulative history of the random walk:

$$\eta_{k,\Delta t} = \sigma + \gamma h\left(\sum_{i=1}^k \xi_i \sqrt{\Delta t}\right)$$

where $\xi_i$ are the Bernoulli random signs. Despite this state-dependence, the lattice remains recombining because the sum of the signs determines the state, rather than the specific order of moves. This property is vital for computational feasibility, preventing the "exploding tree" problem common in many path-dependent models. The researchers rigorously prove that as the number of time steps $n \to \infty$, this discrete binomial price process converges in law to the continuous GABM, ensuring that the numerical implementation remains consistent with the theoretical model.

## Empirical Calibration and Implied Volatility

The model's effectiveness is validated through calibration to real-world options data for major equities like Apple (AAPL), Amazon (AMZN), and Microsoft (MSFT). A key choice in the calibration process is the form of the function $h(x)$. The researchers utilize a functional form based on the Normal Inverse Gaussian (NIG) density:

$$h(x; \mu, \alpha, \beta, \delta) = \frac{\alpha \delta K_1\left(\alpha \sqrt{\delta^2 + (x - \mu)^2}\right)}{\pi \sqrt{\delta^2 + (x - \mu)^2}} e^{\delta \sqrt{\alpha^2 - \beta^2} + \beta(x - \mu)}$$

The NIG distribution is well-regarded for its ability to model semi-heavy tails and flexible skewness. By using this as a smoothing function for the state-dependent volatility, the model can capture the specific risk profiles of different stocks.

The empirical results demonstrate that the GABM framework successfully generates theoretical call and put surfaces that are economically consistent and smooth. More importantly, the model-implied volatility surfaces reproduce the "volatility smirk" typically seen in equity markets, where implied volatility increases as moneyness decreases. The calibration to AAPL and MSFT data shows a particularly steep skew, indicating a high risk premium for downside protection, while the AMZN surface shows a different curvature, illustrating the model's ability to adapt to asset-specific tail risks.

## Multi-Asset Extensions and Perspectives

Beyond single-asset pricing, the work extends the GABM framework to multi-asset markets. In a market with two perfectly correlated risky assets, the research identifies that the pricing equations remain path-dependent. Even if the assets are perfectly correlated in their Brownian driver, their individual asymmetric perturbations $h_1(B_t)$ and $h_2(B_t)$ can differ, leading to complex hedging dynamics. Furthermore, the analysis reveals that in the absence of an external riskless asset, a "synthetic" riskless rate can be endogenously determined, though it becomes a stochastic, path-dependent quantity.

This research bridges the gap between the analytical elegance of diffusion models and the messy reality of market asymmetry. By utilizing invariance principles to derive asymmetric processes, it provides a toolset for quantitative analysts to model smiles, skews, and heavy tails within a consistent no-arbitrage framework. The combination of rigorous convergence proofs for binomial trees and successful empirical validation suggests that GABM offers a viable and computationally efficient alternative to standard option pricing methodologies. Future directions for this work include incorporating time-changes to model volatility clustering and extending the framework to more complex path-dependent derivatives like barrier or Asian options.
