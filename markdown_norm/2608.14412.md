## The Landscape of High-Energy Blazars

Blazars are among the most energetic phenomena in the universe. They are a specific class of active galactic nuclei (AGN) powered by the accretion of material onto supermassive black holes at the centers of galaxies. What distinguishes a blazar from other AGN is the orientation of its relativistic jet—a powerful stream of plasma ejected at nearly the speed of light—which is pointed directly, or nearly directly, toward Earth. This alignment causes the emission to be significantly amplified and shifted to higher frequencies due to relativistic beaming.

Within the blazar family, BL Lacertae (BL Lac) objects are particularly notable for their extreme spectral characteristics. Their emission is dominated by non-thermal radiation across the electromagnetic spectrum, from radio waves to very high-energy gamma-rays. A subset of these, known as TeV-emitting BL Lacs, emit photons with energies exceeding $0.1 \text{ TeV}$ (teraelectronvolts), with some reaching beyond $10 \text{ TeV}$. These objects provide a unique window into the physics of relativistic jets and particle acceleration.

However, a fundamental challenge exists in studying BL Lacs: their optical spectra are often "featureless." The non-thermal synchrotron radiation from the jet is so intense that it masks the underlying emission and absorption lines from the host galaxy and the gas surrounding the black hole. These lines are essential for determining a source's redshift, denoted as $z$, which is a primary measure of its distance from Earth. Without a spectroscopic redshift, it is difficult to calculate the intrinsic luminosity of the source, its physical size, or its location in cosmic time. Currently, approximately half of the known gamma-ray-emitting BL Lacs lack these precise measurements, relying instead on less certain photometric estimates.

## Scientific Motivation and the Role of the Cherenkov Telescope Array

The motivation for obtaining precise redshifts for TeV-emitting BL Lacs extends beyond simply mapping the universe. These sources act as cosmic beacons that allow researchers to probe the intergalactic medium. As high-energy gamma-rays travel through space, they interact with the extragalactic background light (EBL)—the diffuse "fog" of photons produced by all the stars and galaxies that have ever existed. This interaction results in the production of electron-positron pairs, which effectively attenuates the gamma-ray signal reaching Earth.

To use TeV observations to study the EBL, or to search for exotic physics like axion-like particles or cosmic magnetic fields, researchers must know exactly how far the photons have traveled. The amount of attenuation is a function of both the energy of the photon and the redshift $z$ of the source. Precise spectroscopic redshifts are therefore a prerequisite for deconvolving the effects of propagation through intergalactic space from the intrinsic physics of the blazar jet.

Furthermore, this research is timed to support the next generation of gamma-ray astronomy. The Cherenkov Telescope Array (CTA) is an upcoming ground-based observatory that will be significantly more sensitive than current instruments. It is expected to discover hundreds of new TeV-emitting blazars. For the first time, this will allow for population-level statistical studies, moving beyond the analysis of individual "extreme" sources. This study aims to provide the necessary redshift data for 16 TeV-emitting candidates to ensure they can be fully utilized by the CTA once it begins operations.

## Observational Methodology and Instrumentation

Determining the redshift of a BL Lac requires high-quality, medium-resolution optical spectroscopy capable of detecting faint absorption features from the stellar population of the host galaxy. For this study, the researchers targeted 16 candidates and utilized two major astronomical facilities: the Southern African Large Telescope (SALT) and the MDM Observatory.

The majority of the targets (14 out of 16) were observed using the 11-meter SALT telescope, located in South Africa. The observations employed the Robert Stobie Spectrograph (RSS) in a long-slit mode. To maximize the chances of detecting faint lines, each source was observed for a total of 2250 seconds, split into three exposures. The PG0900 grating provided a wavelength range of $4500 \text{ \AA} \text{ to } 7500 \text{ \AA}$ with a spectral resolution of $5.67 \text{ \AA}$.

The remaining two sources were observed with the 2.4-meter Hiltner Reflector at the MDM Observatory in Arizona. These observations used the Ohio State Multi-object Spectrometer (OSMOS). With a blue-sensitive disperser and a narrow slit, this setup achieved a higher resolution of $3.1 \text{ \AA}$ over a wavelength range of $3980 \text{ \AA} \text{ to } 6860 \text{ \AA}$.

Following data collection, standard reduction techniques were applied to remove instrumental artifacts, such as electronic noise (bias), pixel-to-pixel sensitivity variations (flat-fielding), and cosmic ray hits. Wavelength calibration was performed using comparison lamps (mercury, neon, and xenon) to establish a precise relationship between pixel position and wavelength. This was further refined by measuring the positions of known atmospheric "sky lines" to correct for any minor instrumental shifts during the night.

## Extracting Redshifts from Featureless Spectra

The core scientific challenge of this work is identifying spectroscopic signatures in data where the signal-to-noise ratio of the lines is very low. In a typical galaxy spectrum, one looks for the prominent Balmer series of hydrogen or strong emission lines from ionized gas. In BL Lacs, researchers must often search for subtle "dips" in the continuum caused by the absorption of light in the atmospheres of stars within the host galaxy.

The most common features used in this study were the Calcium II H and K doublet—two narrow absorption lines—and the G-band (a feature caused by molecular carbon-hydrogen). Once a candidate feature is identified, it must be confirmed by finding other features at the same redshift. For example, if a line is identified as CaII H at a specific wavelength, the corresponding CaII K line must appear at a mathematically predicted distance.

To precisely measure the redshift, the researchers applied Gaussian fitting to the identified absorption lines. The redshift is defined by the relationship between the observed wavelength $\lambda_{\text{obs}}$ and the rest-frame wavelength $\lambda_{\text{rest}}$:

$$z = \frac{\lambda_{\text{obs}} - \lambda_{\text{rest}}}{\lambda_{\text{rest}}}$$

To ensure the reliability of the results, the authors calculated the uncertainty $\sigma_z$ by accounting for both the statistical error in the Gaussian fit and the systematic error from the wavelength calibration. This is expressed through a quadrature sum:

$$\sigma_z = \sqrt{\sigma_{\text{fit}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{cal}}^2}$$

This rigorous approach allowed the team to extract secure redshifts from spectra that had previously been classified as featureless or of too poor quality for measurement by other research groups.

## Analysis of Redshift Results and Revisions

The study successfully determined spectroscopic redshifts for all 16 targeted sources, with values ranging from $z = 0.059$ to $z = 0.400$. The results provide a mix of new measurements, confirmation of existing data, and critical corrections to previously published values.

Five sources were assigned spectroscopic redshifts for the first time. For instance, J0001-419 was found to have a redshift of $z = 0.137$. Interestingly, J2322-492, which had a prior photometric estimate of 0.38, was measured spectroscopically at $z = 0.240$. This discrepancy of 0.14 highlights the importance of spectroscopic follow-up, as photometric methods—which rely on broad-band colors rather than specific lines—can sometimes lead to significant errors in distance estimation.

One of the most impactful results involves the source J2221-524. Previous studies had either failed to find any lines or reported a low-quality redshift of 0.24. By resolving the CaII H and K doublet, the researchers established a new, secure redshift of $z = 0.400$. This significantly changes the physical interpretation of the source, as it is much more distant and inherently more luminous than previously thought.

Similarly, the study resolved long-standing disputes for other sources. For J0123-231, where prior literature was split between $z = 0.2$ and $z = 0.4$, this work confirmed $z = 0.397$, siding with the more distant estimate. For J0956-099, the team measured $z = 0.158$, providing a precise value that mediates between conflicting historical measurements.

## Impact on Future Gamma-Ray Research

The successful measurement of these 16 redshifts has direct implications for the high-energy astrophysics community. By providing these distances, the researchers have effectively "calibrated" these sources for future use as probes of the EBL.

When the CTA begins its survey, these targets will be prime candidates for deep observations. With the redshifts now known, astronomers can use the observed gamma-ray spectra to calculate the opacity of the universe to high-energy photons. For example, a source at $z = 0.4$ (like J2221-524) will experience much more significant gamma-ray attenuation than one at $z = 0.059$. Accurate redshifts allow scientists to build a 3D map of gamma-ray absorption across cosmic time, which in turn reveals the history of star formation and galaxy evolution that produced the EBL.

Furthermore, this work demonstrates that with 8-to-10-meter class telescopes like SALT, it is possible to "unlock" the redshifts of even the most difficult BL Lac targets. This provides a roadmap for the ongoing effort to complete the redshift catalogs of all known gamma-ray blazars, a task that remains a high priority for the success of future multi-messenger and high-energy astronomical observations. By moving 16 candidates into the category of "well-characterized sources," this study contributes to a more robust and statistically significant understanding of the most extreme accelerators in our universe.
