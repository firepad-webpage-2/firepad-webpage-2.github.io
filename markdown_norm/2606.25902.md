## Modeling Malaria in Urban Environments

Malaria continues to be a significant public health challenge in tropical and subtropical regions, particularly within densely populated urban centers like Mumbai, India. Effective management of the disease requires not only clinical intervention but also robust surveillance systems capable of predicting future incidence. Accurate forecasting allows public health authorities to allocate resources efficiently, plan vector control activities, and issue early warnings to the community. However, modeling malaria incidence is complicated by the nature of the data: it consists of discrete counts of cases over time, which often exhibit complex statistical properties such as seasonality, long-term trends, and dependence on climatic factors.

In urban settings, the transmission dynamics of *Plasmodium vivax*, the primary parasite species in Mumbai, are influenced by a combination of meteorological variables—such as rainfall, temperature, and sunshine—and biological factors like parasite relapses. These relapses can cause new clinical episodes months after the initial infection, creating a natural temporal dependence in the data. Traditional statistical models often struggle to account for this autocorrelation while simultaneously handling the discrete, non-negative nature of case counts. This research addresses these challenges by implementing a Generalized Linear Autoregressive Moving Average (GLARMA) framework, which integrates regression techniques with time series state processes to provide a more accurate and stable forecasting tool for malaria incidence.

## Statistical Foundations: The Count Data Challenge

The foundation of epidemiological modeling for disease counts usually begins with Generalized Linear Models (GLMs). In a GLM, the response variable $Y_t$ (the number of malaria cases at time $t$) is assumed to follow a specific probability distribution from the exponential family. For count data, the Poisson distribution is the natural starting point. The relationship between the expected mean incidence $\mu_t$ and a set of predictors $X_t$ is established through a log-link function:

$$\log(\mu_t) = X_t^T \beta$$

Here, $\beta$ represents the vector of coefficients for predictors including trend, seasonality, and climatic variables. While Poisson regression is widely used, it relies on a strict assumption: the mean of the data must equal its variance. In real-world epidemiological data, this assumption is frequently violated. Malaria counts often exhibit overdispersion, where the variance is significantly larger than the mean. This can happen due to unobserved heterogeneity, clustering of cases, or the inherent variability in transmission dynamics.

If overdispersion is present but ignored, the resulting model may produce biased standard errors, leading to artificially low p-values and misleading conclusions about which factors—such as temperature or rainfall—are actually driving the disease. Diagnostic checks of the Poisson residuals often reveal significant structural failures, including extreme outliers and systematic patterns that indicate the model is not adequately capturing the data's underlying variability.

## Addressing Overdispersion with Negative Binomial Models

To overcome the limitations of the Poisson distribution, researchers often turn to the Negative Binomial (NB) distribution. The NB model introduces an additional dispersion parameter $k$ (or its reciprocal $\alpha$), which allows the variance to exceed the mean. The variance in a Negative Binomial model is typically defined as $\text{Var}(Y_t) = \mu_t + \alpha \mu_t^2$. As $\alpha$ approaches zero, the distribution converges back to a Poisson.

![poisson-diagnostic-plots](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.25902v1/poiss.png)
*{fig:poisson-diagnostic-plots}: Diagnostic plots for the Poisson regression model. The Q-Q plot of randomized quantile residuals (left) shows severe deviation from the red reference line, and the residual vs. predicted plot (right) shows numerous outliers (red asterisks), confirming significant overdispersion.*

When applied to the Mumbai malaria data, switching from a Poisson to a Negative Binomial distribution leads to a marked improvement in model fit. The estimated dispersion parameter usually confirms that the data is indeed overdispersed. Interestingly, the transition to the NB framework often changes the interpretation of environmental covariates. In the study, once overdispersion was accounted for, meteorological variables like rainfall and temperature lost their statistical significance at $p < 0.05$, while seasonal dummy variables remained significant. This suggests that in the presence of high variability, broad seasonal patterns are more reliable predictors than month-to-month climatic fluctuations.

The residuals now align much more closely with the theoretical uniform distribution in the Q-Q plot, and the residual vs. predicted scatter is homoscedastic, indicating that overdispersion has been successfully addressed.*

## The GLARMA Framework: Integrating Temporal Dependence

Even when overdispersion is corrected, a second major challenge remains: temporal dependence. In a standard GLM, observations are assumed to be independent given the covariates. However, malaria cases at time $t$ are likely correlated with cases at time $t-1$ or $t-2$. This autocorrelation can stem from the biological cycle of the mosquito vector or the relapse cycles of the parasite.

The Generalized Linear Autoregressive Moving Average (GLARMA) model addresses this by extending the linear predictor to include an autoregressive state process $Z_t$. The modified linear predictor $W_t$ is defined as:

$$W_t = X_t^T \beta + \text{offset}_t + Z_t$$

In this equation, $X_t^T \beta$ captures the systematic components like long-term trends and seasonality, while $\text{offset}_t$ accounts for changes in the underlying population size. The term $Z_t$ is the autoregressive component that models the serial dependence. In a GLARMA $AR(p)$ model, $Z_t$ depends on its own past values and the past residuals of the model. By incorporating $Z_t$, the model can "remember" recent spikes or dips in incidence that are not explained by climatic factors alone. This allows the model to adjust its predictions based on the most recent trajectory of the disease, significantly improving its short-term forecasting performance.

## Diagnostic Tools for Count Time Series

Validating models for count time series requires specialized diagnostic tools. Standard residuals used in linear regression are often inappropriate for discrete count data. Instead, this work utilizes randomized quantile residuals. These residuals are calculated by mapping the discrete cumulative distribution of the observed counts onto a continuous scale, resulting in residuals that should follow a standard normal distribution $N(0,1)$ or a uniform distribution $U(0,1)$ if the model is correctly specified.

![residual-autocorrelation-in-negative-binomial-glm](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.25902v1/nb_acf.png)
*{fig:residual-autocorrelation-in-negative-binomial-glm}: Analysis of residuals from the standard Negative Binomial GLM. The Autocorrelation Function (ACF) plot (right) shows significant spikes at multiple lags, indicating that the model fails to capture the temporal dependence in the data.*

Statistical tests such as the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test are used to check if the residuals deviate from the expected distribution. Furthermore, the Durbin-Watson test and Autocorrelation Function (ACF) plots are employed to detect remaining serial correlation. In the Mumbai study, while the Negative Binomial GLM fixed the overdispersion issue, the ACF plots still showed significant spikes, proving that the model was missing temporal information. Only after moving to the GLARMA framework did the residuals pass these autocorrelation tests, resulting in an ACF plot where all spikes remained within the bounds of white noise.

![corrected-acf-via-glarma](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.25902v1/glarma_acf.png)
*{fig:corrected-acf-via-glarma}: Residual diagnostics for the GLARMA Negative Binomial model. The ACF plot (right) now shows no significant spikes, confirming that the autoregressive component $Z_t$ has successfully accounted for the serial correlation.*

## Forecasting Strategy: Monte Carlo and Stability

Forecasting with GLARMA models is more complex than with standard regression because the model is "observation-driven"—the prediction for time $t+1$ depends on the realization of the count at time $t$. For multi-step-ahead forecasts (e.g., predicting 6 months into the future), the model must simulate future paths.

The study utilizes a Monte Carlo simulation approach. For a forecast horizon $h$, the model generates $M$ potential future paths for the disease incidence. A common pitfall in this process is "error inflation," where a single high simulated value at step $t+1$ can lead to an explosive and unrealistic forecast at step $t+h$, particularly when using heavy-tailed distributions like the Negative Binomial. To mitigate this, the research uses the **median of the conditional means** $\mu^{(m)}_t$ across all $M$ simulations as the final point forecast, rather than the average of the raw simulated responses. This approach provides a more robust estimate that is less sensitive to extreme simulated outliers.

To evaluate how well the model performs in a real-world setting, the researchers used a "rolling forecasting origin" cross-validation. This involves training the model on a portion of the data, forecasting the next $h$ months, then moving the training window forward and repeating the process. This systematically tests the model's performance across different periods and horizons (3, 6, 9, and 12 months).

## Comparative Performance and Public Health Impact

The study benchmarked the GLARMA Negative Binomial model against several alternatives: a standard linear model with ARIMA errors (LM+ARIMA), a log-transformed version (Log-LM+ARIMA), and a GLARMA Poisson model. The results showed a clear hierarchy in performance.

![accuracy-degradation-across-horizons](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.25902v1/MAPE.png)
*{fig:accuracy-degradation-across-horizons}: Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) across different forecasting horizons. The GLARMA models (red and green) maintain much lower and more stable error rates compared to Gaussian-based models (black and blue) as the forecast horizon increases.*

The GLARMA Negative Binomial model consistently outperformed the others, achieving the lowest Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) at every horizon. While all models see their accuracy decrease as they try to predict further into the future, the GLARMA models showed much slower degradation. For instance, the LM+ARIMA model's error rate climbed sharply, whereas the GLARMA NB model remained relatively stable even at the 12-month horizon.

![forecast-stability-analysis](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.25902v1/boxplot.png)
*{fig:forecast-stability-analysis}: Boxplots showing the distribution of MAPE across different cross-validation folds. The GLARMA NB model (green) exhibits the narrowest range and fewest outliers, indicating it is the most reliable and consistent model across different time periods.*

The implications for public health are significant. By using a model that correctly accounts for both the discrete nature of the data and its temporal momentum, health officials in Mumbai can obtain reliable forecasts up to a year in advance. The stability of the GLARMA NB model suggests it is less likely to produce "false alarms" or miss true outbreaks compared to simpler regression models. This framework provides a statistically rigorous path toward developing early warning systems that can adapt to the shifting dynamics of infectious diseases in urban environments. The methodology is not limited to malaria and could be applied to any disease surveillance system dealing with overdispersed and autocorrelated count data.
