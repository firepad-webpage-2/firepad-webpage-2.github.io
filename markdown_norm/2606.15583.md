The integration of Large Language Models (LMs) into scientific workflows has transformed how researchers draft and refine their work. While early studies focused on whether AI-generated abstracts could pass as human-authored or save time, there has been a significant gap in understanding the actual *process* of revision. Most evaluations look only at the final product, ignoring the sequence of edits that lead to that result.

This research introduces a process-oriented evaluation of AI-assisted scientific writing. By analyzing keystroke-level logs, the study explores how humans interact with AI-generated text compared to human-authored drafts. The work moves beyond treating revisions as isolated operations, instead modeling the dynamic relationship between a draft’s linguistic properties and the subsequent editing strategies employed by the author.

## Modeling the Revision Process

To understand the mechanics of scientific editing, the researchers collected 869 keystroke-level edit logs from domain experts revising abstracts for 45 computer science papers. Each session was decomposed into "behaviorally separable bursts"—sequences of edits separated by pauses or significant cursor movements.

These bursts are characterized across four dimensions:
1.  **Pauses**: The time taken before and during an edit sequence.
2.  **Navigation**: The movement of the cursor (forward, backward, or stationary).
3.  **Actions**: The specific operations performed (insertion, deletion, or substitution).
4.  **Scope**: The amount of text affected, categorized as word-level, phrase-level, or sentence-level and larger (sentence+).

Using a Gaussian mixture model, the study identifies sixteen distinct behaviors which are grouped into four general "families":
*   **Expansion (28%)**: Primarily adding new material to a draft.
*   **Pruning (23%)**: Removing unnecessary material.
*   **Substitution (30%)**: Replacing existing text with new content.
*   **Restructuring (19%)**: Complex edits involving multiple actions across broader scopes.

## Quantifying Linguistic Quality

To measure the impact of these edits, the research uses 25 computational metrics grouped into five theory-driven linguistic dimensions. These dimensions provide a holistic view of scientific writing quality:
*   **Agency**: The clarity of actors and actions (e.g., avoiding excessive passive voice).
*   **Economy**: Efficiency in information conveyance (e.g., avoiding filler words or excessive nominalization).
*   **Structure**: The grammatical arrangement of words and phrases (e.g., managing subject-verb gaps).
*   **Coherence**: The logical connection of information across the entire text.
*   **Framing**: The placement of claims and context within the scientific narrative.

The study standardizes these metrics so that a higher score consistently reflects better writing quality relative to a reference distribution.

## The Influence of Provenance and Disclosure

A central question in AI-assisted writing is whether authors treat AI-generated text differently from human-authored text. The study found that the initial linguistic quality of a draft is a much stronger predictor of editing behavior than the draft's "provenance" (whether it was written by a human or an AI). Specifically, the pre-edit score explained a significant portion of the variance in quality improvement ($R^2_{pre\text{ edit}} = 0.085$), whereas the source explained almost none ($R^2_{source} = 0.004$).

However, the researchers discovered a "stigmatic behavior" among highly educated editors (those with a Master's degree or higher). When these experts were told they were editing an AI-generated abstract, their strategy shifted.

As shown in {fig:predicted-probability-of-restructuring-and-substitution-edits-based-on-whether-the-ai-source-was-disclosed-to-the-editor}, disclosure of the AI source led to a 16.1% decrease in restructuring edits ($p < .001$) and a corresponding 18.8% increase in substitution edits ($p < .001$). This suggests that expert editors may adopt a more superficial revision strategy—focusing on local word changes rather than structural overhauls—when they perceive the underlying text as machine-generated.

## The Efficacy of Human Revision

Despite the effort humans put into editing—averaging 300 individual edit actions per session—human revision did not close the fundamental quality gap between human and AI drafts. 

![distributions-of-linguistic-properties-for-original-and-edited-abstracts-across-ai-and-human-sources](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.15583v1/x4.png)
*{fig:distributions-of-linguistic-properties-for-original-and-edited-abstracts-across-ai-and-human-sources}: Linguistic distributions before and after human editing. Significant differences between AI and human-authored abstracts persist even after revision.*

AI-generated abstracts were initially stronger in sentence-level agency ($d_z = 0.45$) and structure ($d_z = 0.54$) but significantly weaker in global coherence ($d_z = -0.47$). After human editing, these distributional differences remained largely unchanged. While humans were effective at improving low-quality text (the bottom 25% of drafts), they often inadvertently degraded drafts that were already high-quality (the top 25%).

Analysis of behavior efficacy ({fig:heatmap-showing-the-effectiveness-of-different-edit-families-substitution-pruning-expansion-restructuring-across-linguistic-properties}) shows that "Substitution" is the most versatile tool for improving poor drafts. Interestingly, "Restructuring" showed a relatively weaker effect on improving low-quality regions, potentially because it is a more difficult operation to execute effectively without introducing new errors.

## Language Models as Autonomous Editors

The study also evaluated how current Language Models (such as GPT-4 and Claude) perform as scientific editors under various prompting conditions: "Unguided" (general instructions), "Rubric-guided" (using the five linguistic dimensions), and "Scoped" (constrained to human-like edit boundaries).

LMs demonstrated a notable "homogenization effect." They were highly effective at lifting the quality of the poorest abstracts, moving them from the 26.8th percentile to the 46.7th percentile. However, they frequently degraded high-quality abstracts, particularly in the dimension of global coherence.

![the-change-in-percentile-for-low-quality-vs-high-quality-abstracts-when-edited-by-an-lm-using-a-rubric](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.15583v1/x6.png)
*{fig:the-change-in-percentile-for-low-quality-vs-high-quality-abstracts-when-edited-by-an-lm-using-a-rubric}: LMs consistently improve low-scoring abstracts while often degrading high-scoring ones, particularly in coherence and framing.*

Furthermore, when LMs were instructed to act as "Scoped Assistants"—performing only local edits equivalent to human bursts—they consistently "betrayed" the requested scope. Even with explicit instructions to limit themselves to a word or phrase, LMs defaulted to much larger, sentence-level revisions.

![comparison-of-edit-scopes-between-humans-and-various-lm-prompting-strategies](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.15583v1/x7.png)

As illustrated in {fig:comparison-of-edit-scopes-between-humans-and-various-lm-prompting-strategies}, LMs under unguided and rubric-guided conditions perform significantly more "Sentence+" edits than humans. Even the "Scoped" prompt only partially aligned the LM with human behavior, highlighting a fundamental misalignment between current AI capabilities and fine-grained human editing intentions.

## Significance of the Findings

This process-oriented analysis reveals that the impact of AI on scientific writing is more complex than simple productivity gains. The findings point to three major implications:

1.  **Macro-level Homogenization**: Both human and machine editing processes tend to push texts toward a mediocre average. While AI is excellent at "fixing" bad writing, its tendency to degrade top-tier work suggests it may reduce the stylistic diversity and depth of scientific literature if used without careful oversight.
2.  **Psychological Barriers to AI Assistance**: The discovery of "stigmatic behavior" among experts suggests that the utility of AI tools is constrained by human perception. If experts subconsciously put less effort into structurally improving AI text, the resulting scientific output may lack the critical structural rigor found in purely human-authored or "blindly" edited work.
3.  **Need for Better Intent Alignment**: The failure of LMs to respect editing "scopes" indicates that current models are not yet optimized to act as surgical assistants. Future AI development should focus on models that can adhere to specific user-defined constraints, allowing researchers to use AI for targeted improvements without losing control over the broader narrative structure.

By linking the local behavior of editors to the global quality of the text, this research provides a framework for developing more effective, human-centric AI writing tools that preserve the integrity and excellence of scientific communication.
