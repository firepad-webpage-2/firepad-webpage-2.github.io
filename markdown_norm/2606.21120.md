In many institutional settings, a central authority—the "sender"—manages both the internal development of individuals (agents) and their external reputation. Consider a school that must decide which students receive specialized tutoring (a limited resource) and later determines which students to recommend to prestigious universities. Similarly, a startup incubator allocates seed funding to entrepreneurs and later introduces them to venture capitalists.

In the paper "Coordinating Treatment Allocation and Recommendation," researchers Li Guo and Penghuan Yan explore how an institution should ideally coordinate these two functions: resource allocation and information disclosure. They demonstrate that when a sender possesses the power to commit to a joint strategy, they can achieve better outcomes than if they treated these tasks as independent problems.

## The Institutional Framework

The paper models an environment consisting of three parties: a sender, a unit mass of agents, and an external receiver. Each agent possesses a private "initial quality" or type, denoted as $\theta \in [0, 1]$. This initial quality is unknown to the sender at the beginning of the process.

The sender has two primary instruments at their disposal:
1.  **Treatment Allocation ($p$):** The sender can provide a "treatment" (like a scholarship, training, or funding) that increases an agent's quality by a fixed amount $v$. However, this treatment is a scarce resource; the sender can only treat a total mass of agents up to a capacity limit $\kappa \in (0, 1)$.
2.  **Recommendation ($q$):** After the treatment has been assigned and its effects realized, the sender can send a recommendation signal $s \in \{0, 1\}$ to the receiver.

The receiver must decide whether to "accept" an agent based on the recommendation. The receiver's goal is to ensure that the expected final quality of the accepted agents meets a certain threshold $\tau$. If the recommendation signal $s=1$ is sent, the receiver accepts the agent if:
$$\mathbb{E}[\theta + vd \mid s=1] \geq \tau$$
where $d \in \{0, 1\}$ represents whether the agent was treated.

Agents are self-interested. They gain a utility $u$ from receiving the treatment and a utility of $1$ from being accepted by the receiver. Since $0 < u < 1$, agents value external acceptance more than the internal resource, but they still desire the resource.

## The Core Problem: Informational Timing

The fundamental challenge in this setting is the timing of information. When the sender must allocate the limited treatment, they do not know the agents' true types $\theta$. However, by the time the recommendation is made, the sender has gained more information.

In a "separated" design, the sender might allocate treatment randomly (due to the lack of initial information) and then simply recommend the "best" agents from the resulting pool. The authors argue that this is sub-optimal. Instead, the sender can use the *promise* of a future recommendation to incentivize agents to reveal their true quality early on. By coordinating these two stages, the sender uses the recommendation as both a tool of persuasion for the receiver and an incentive device for the agents.

## The Structure of the Optimal Coordinated Mechanism

The paper's most striking result is the characterization of the optimal joint mechanism. The sender divides the population of agents into four distinct groups based on their quality $\theta$. Surprisingly, the allocation of treatment is not simply "more for the better agents." Instead, it follows a non-monotone pattern.

As shown in the authors' analysis, the agents are categorized into the following groups (from highest to lowest quality):

1.  **The Top Group ($\theta > \theta_3$):** These are the star performers. They receive a high probability of treatment $P$ and are always recommended, regardless of whether they actually received the treatment or not.
2.  **The Upper-Middle Group ($\theta \in (\theta_2, \theta_3]$):** These agents also receive the high treatment probability $P$. However, they are only recommended if they are treated. If they are in the unlucky fraction $(1-P)$ that did not receive treatment, they are not recommended.
3.  **The Lower-Middle Group ($\theta \in (\theta_1, \theta_2]$):** This is the "Target Group." To save treatment capacity for higher-quality agents, the sender gives this group a *lower* treatment probability:
$$p(\theta) = \frac{u}{1+u} P$$
In exchange for this lower chance of treatment, these agents are guaranteed a recommendation if they are treated.
4.  **The Bottom Group ($\theta \leq \theta_1$):** These agents receive the high treatment probability $P$ but are *never* recommended.

In this mechanism, $P$ represents the maximum treatment probability allowed, and the authors prove that $P > \kappa$, meaning some agents receive treatment at a rate higher than the average capacity.

## The Logic of Non-Monotone Allocation

Why does the "bottom group" receive more treatment than the "lower-middle group"? This seems counter-intuitive at first glance—why give resources to those you have no intention of recommending?

The reason lies in **Incentive Compatibility (IC)**. The sender needs the agents to self-sort into these groups. If the sender offered the "Target Group" (Group 3) a lower treatment probability and no other benefits, those agents would simply lie and pretend to be part of the "Bottom Group" (Group 4) to get a higher chance of treatment.

To prevent this, the sender "taxes" the bottom group by ensuring they are never recommended. The lower-middle group accepts a lower chance of treatment because they are compensated with a "lenient" recommendation standard—they only need to be treated to be recommended. The bottom group, having very low $\theta$, would "dilute" the recommendation pool too much if they were recommended, making it impossible to satisfy the receiver's threshold $\tau$. Thus, they are given treatment (which they value) but are excluded from recommendation to protect the sender's reputation with the receiver.

## Implementation via Self-Selection

In practice, a sender doesn't need to explicitly know every agent's $\theta$ to implement this. They can offer a "menu" of two contracts:

*   **The Default Contract:** High treatment probability $P$, but a strict recommendation rule (only recommend if treated and if the agent is of sufficiently high type).
*   **The Targeted Contract:** Low treatment probability $\frac{u}{1+u} P$, but a lenient recommendation rule (always recommend if treated).

High-quality agents choose the default contract because they are confident they can meet the strict recommendation standard. Low-quality agents choose the default contract because they just want the treatment and know they won't be recommended anyway. Only the middle-tier "Target Group" finds the targeted contract attractive—they trade off some treatment probability for a "sure thing" recommendation if they do get treated.

## Significance for Policy and Research

The paper's findings have broad implications for how we view institutional selection processes.

### Causal Inference and Program Evaluation
In empirical economics, researchers often try to measure the "treatment effect" of a program. Usually, if treatment isn't assigned randomly, researchers look for "selection on observables." This paper suggests that even if agents look identical to the researcher, they might be self-selecting into different treatment probabilities based on their private knowledge of their own potential. This "selection on unobservables" is a direct result of the sender's optimal design, complicating the task of measuring a program's true impact.

### Redesigning Honors and Support Programs
For educational institutions, this research suggests that "honors tracks" shouldn't just be about more resources. An optimal honors track might actually offer *fewer* resources but a higher "prestige floor" (guaranteed recommendation), while the general track offers more broad-based resources but no special endorsement. This structure helps the school identify "diamonds in the rough" who are willing to sacrifice immediate resources for future signaling value.

### Industrial Policy
Governments often face the "picking winners" dilemma. This model provides a framework for how a government can use the promise of future certification or state-backed endorsement to screen which firms are truly worthy of limited subsidies, even when the government cannot initially tell which firms have the most potential.

By moving beyond simple "top-down" allocation, the paper provides a mathematical foundation for how commitment and coordinated information design can overcome the barriers of initial ignorance and resource scarcity.
