## Introduction to Adaptive Epidemic Forecasting

The ability to accurately predict the trajectory of an infectious disease outbreak is a cornerstone of modern public health. During the COVID-19 pandemic, these forecasts informed critical decisions regarding hospital resource allocation, the timing of health warnings, and the implementation of social distancing measures. However, forecasting epidemics is inherently difficult because the "best" model often changes as the outbreak evolves. A model that performs well during a period of exponential growth might fail completely once the epidemic peaks or begins to decline due to human behavioral changes or public health interventions.

To address this, researchers often turn to ensemble forecasting—the practice of combining multiple different models into a single prediction. Traditionally, these ensembles use simple averages or fixed weights based on past performance. This paper explores a more dynamic approach: using algorithms inspired by the "Multi-Armed Bandit" (MAB) problem to adaptively weight different models in real-time. By treating each forecasting model as an "arm" in a bandit problem, the system can learn which models are currently reliable and adjust their influence on the final ensemble forecast accordingly.

## The Problem of Structural Uncertainty

A primary obstacle in epidemic modeling is structural uncertainty. This refers to the fact that different mathematical structures capture different aspects of disease dynamics. For example, a "mechanistic" model attempts to simulate the actual biological process of transmission (susceptible individuals becoming infected and then recovering), while a "phenomenological" model focuses on the aggregate growth patterns seen in the data without necessarily modeling the underlying biological mechanism.

Because the relative skill of these different modeling paradigms fluctuates, relying on a single model can lead to significant errors. If a model assumes a specific type of growth that is no longer occurring, its predictions will deviate from reality. Ensembles mitigate this by diversifying the types of models used, but static ensembles lack the agility to respond to rapid epidemiological shifts. The research presented here aims to bridge this gap by implementing an adaptive weighting system that evaluates model performance over short calibration windows and shifts weights toward models that have demonstrated recent accuracy.

## A Diverse Pool of Base Models

To create a robust ensemble, the researchers utilized a pool of $K=10$ base models, categorized into three distinct families. This heterogeneity is essential because it ensures that the ensemble has access to various "hypotheses" about the epidemic's future path.

### family 1: Mechanistic Compartmental Models
These models divide the population into "compartments" such as Susceptible ($S$), Exposed ($E$), Infectious ($I$), and Recovered ($R$). The transitions between these states are governed by differential equations.
*   **SIR Model:** A classic model focusing on $S$, $I$, and $R$.
*   **SEIR Model:** Includes an "Exposed" period to account for the incubation time of the virus.

*{fig:sir-model-diagram}: Schematic of the SIR compartmental model showing the flow of population and the calculation of reported incidence $y(t) = \rho \frac{\beta S I}{N}$.*

![seir-model-diagram](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.18575v1/img-9.jpeg)
*{fig:seir-model-diagram}: Schematic of the SEIR model, which adds an exposed compartment to the SIR framework, calculating incidence as $y(t) = \kappa \rho E(t)$.*

### Family 2: Phenomenological Growth Models
These models describe the "shape" of the epidemic curve using flexible mathematical functions.
*   **Generalized Logistic Model (GLM):** Captures sub-exponential growth through a scaling parameter.
*   **Gompertz and Richards Models:** Flexible sigmoidal functions often used to model the "saturation" phase of an outbreak.

![growth-models-diagram](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.18575v1/img-10.jpeg)
*{fig:growth-models-diagram}: Visual representation of the equations governing the GLM, Gompertz, and Richards phenomenological growth models.*

### Family 3: Statistical and Time-Series Models
These models are purely data-driven, looking for patterns in the historical time series without explicit epidemiological assumptions.
*   **ARIMA:** A standard statistical approach for forecasting stationary time series.
*   **Simple Exponential Smoothing (SES):** Gives more weight to recent observations.
*   **Holt’s Linear Trend and Random Walk:** Capture local trends and drift in the data.

## Multi-Armed Bandit Adaptive Weighting

The core of this research is the application of MAB-inspired strategies to assign weights $w^{(t)}_k$ to each of the 10 models. In a typical MAB scenario, a decision-maker must choose between several options (arms), where the reward of each option is only revealed after it is chosen. In this forecasting setting, the researchers utilize a "full-information" variant, where the "reward" (or rather, the loss/error) of every model can be calculated for the calibration period.

The study evaluates three main algorithms:

1.  **EXP3 (Exponential-weight algorithm for Exploration and Exploitation):** This algorithm maintains a probability distribution over the models. Models that result in smaller prediction errors during the calibration phase receive an exponential increase in their weights. To ensure the system doesn't become too narrow-minded, a learning rate $\eta = \sqrt{\frac{2 \log K}{Km}}$ is used to balance how quickly weights are updated based on new information.

2.  **$\epsilon$-Greedy (EPS):** This is a simpler strategy that chooses the model with the best historical performance with probability $1-\epsilon$ (exploitation) and chooses a model at random with probability $\epsilon$ (exploration). The researchers used a fixed exploration rate $\epsilon = \sqrt{\frac{K}{m}}$, where $m$ is the length of the calibration window.

3.  **Upper Confidence Bound (UCB):** UCB selects models by calculating an index that combines the model's average past performance with a term representing the uncertainty or potential of that model. This naturally prioritizes models that are either currently performing well or have not been "tested" enough recently.

The researchers generated two types of ensembles for each algorithm:
*   **Deterministic:** The final forecast is a weighted average of all component models.
*   **Stochastic:** At each step, a single model is randomly sampled from the pool based on the calculated weights to provide the forecast.

## Methodology: Calibration and Uncertainty

To test these strategies, the study used daily U.S. COVID-19 incidence data across three distinct national waves between 2020 and 2022.

![covid-19-waves](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.18575v1/img-0.jpeg)
*{fig:covid-19-waves}: The three national COVID-19 waves in the U.S. used to evaluate the forecasting models, representing different epidemiological characteristics.*

A critical aspect of the methodology was the handling of uncertainty. The researchers used a **bootstrap** method: they generated $B=300$ noisy realizations of the observed data. Each of the 10 models was then fitted to every one of these 300 realizations. This process allowed the researchers to produce not just a single "best guess" (point forecast) but a range of probable outcomes (prediction intervals).

Two calibration strategies were compared:
*   **Fixed Short-Window:** Using only the last $10$ days of data to update weights, focusing on very recent trends.
*   **Growing Calibration:** Increasing the data used for calibration from $30$ up to $80$ days, providing more historical context.

## Results and Performance Analysis

The performance of the models was measured using the **Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)** for point accuracy and the **Weighted Interval Score (WIS)** for probabilistic accuracy. WIS is a "proper scoring rule" that penalizes both lack of accuracy and overly wide (vague) prediction intervals, rewarding models that are both precise and correctly confident.

Key findings include:

*   **Probabilistic Superiority:** The MAB-inspired ensembles, particularly the stochastic versions like **EXP3Stoch** and **EPSStoch**, consistently achieved the lowest average WIS. This means they provided the best balance between accurate point estimates and reliable uncertainty intervals.

![wis-comparison](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.18575v1/img-11.jpeg)
*{fig:wis-comparison}: Average WIS across configurations. Lower scores indicate better probabilistic forecasting. MAB-inspired stochastic methods (top rows) show strong performance.*

*   **Point Accuracy Variance:** Interestingly, for simple point accuracy (RMSE), the statistical model **SES** (Simple Exponential Smoothing) was often the strongest individual performer. This suggests that for short-term "best guesses," simple smoothing is hard to beat, but for understanding the *range* of possible futures, adaptive ensembles are superior.

*   **Interval Coverage:** Stochastic adaptive ensembles generally provided better coverage for the 95% prediction intervals than deterministic ones. However, on average, the coverage for all methods remained slightly below the nominal 95% level, highlighting the difficulty of fully capturing the volatility of epidemic data.

![pi-coverage](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.18575v1/img-12.jpeg)
*{fig:pi-coverage}: 95% Prediction-Interval Coverage. Methods closer to the 95% dashed line are better calibrated. Stochastic MAB methods tend to outperform individual models.*

*   **Configuration Dependency:** The "winning" method changed depending on the specific wave and forecast horizon. This reinforces the core argument of the paper: because model skill is time-varying and context-dependent, an adaptive framework is necessary.

## Broader Implications for Public Health

The unique contribution of this work is the demonstration that reinforcement learning principles, specifically Multi-Armed Bandits, can be effectively repurposed for model ensemble weighting in the context of infectious disease. Unlike standard ensemble methods that may update weights slowly or require large historical archives, MAB-inspired strategies are designed to react quickly to non-stationary environments—precisely what is needed during an evolving pandemic.

While simpler methods like Inverse-WIS weighting or unweighted averaging remain robust benchmarks, the adaptive strategies offer a more flexible toolset for public health officials. By improving uncertainty quantification, these methods help decision-makers understand not just what is *likely* to happen, but the extent of what *could* happen, allowing for more informed risk management and contingency planning. Future work in this area may involve integrating real-time data revision processes and expanding the pool of models to include more complex deep learning or spatial models.
