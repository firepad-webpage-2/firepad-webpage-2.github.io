## The Challenge of Safe Lithium-Ion Fast Charging

The commercial viability and widespread adoption of electric vehicles (EVs) are deeply tied to the efficiency of charging protocols. While fast charging is essential for consumer convenience, it introduces significant physical stressors on lithium-ion batteries. Two primary safety constraints dominate the fast-charging landscape: the thermal limit of the battery cell and the prevention of metallic lithium plating on the graphite anode.

The thermal limit is typically defined by a peak temperature threshold, beyond which the risk of accelerated degradation or even thermal runaway increases. This temperature is a result of the balance between internal heat generation and the heat removal capacity of the thermal management system. Simultaneously, lithium plating occurs when the potential of the negative electrode drops below the potential of lithium metal, typically under conditions of high current, high state-of-charge (SOC), and low temperatures. Plating not only reduces capacity but can also form dendrites that cause internal short circuits.

Most current charging strategies assume that the operating environment—specifically the ambient temperature and the health of the cooling system—is known and constant. However, in real-world scenarios, ambient temperatures can fluctuate dramatically, and cooling systems can suffer from "silent" faults, such as blocked coolant channels or degraded fans. When these conditions are unobserved, a charging protocol that is safe for a healthy cell at room temperature can inadvertently lead to overheating or plating.

## The Repair-before-Veto Principle

Traditional safety mechanisms in battery management systems (BMS) often rely on "veto" actions. If a safety limit is approached, the system simply stops the charge to protect the hardware. While safe, this results in a poor user experience and fails to deliver the requested energy. Alternatively, some systems use conservative, "worst-case" fixed protocols that are slow for all users to ensure safety for the few operating in extreme conditions.

This research proposes a different philosophy: **Repair-before-Veto**. Instead of adhering to a pre-calculated current schedule or resorting to an immediate shutdown, the controller (named RACL-B) continuously "repairs" the charging current in real-time. It starts with an aggressive current request but modifies it based on the measured proximity to safety margins. The charge is only "vetoed" (stopped) as a last resort if no safe current can be found to maintain operation.

This adaptive approach allows the system to be robust to unobserved states. If the ambient temperature is higher than expected or the cooling system is failing, the controller senses the tightening thermal margin and reduces the current just enough to stay safe, rather than shutting down entirely or causing damage.

## RACL-B: A Margin-Aware Controller

The Repair-before-Veto Controller (RACL-B) operates by calculating normalized headrooms for three critical safety parameters: plating overpotential ($e_{pl}$), cell temperature ($e_T$), and terminal voltage ($e_V$). These headrooms represent the distance between the current state and the safety limit, including a small safety buffer.

At each control step, the controller identifies the "binding margin"—the safety limit that is currently closest to being violated. This is defined as $e_k = \min(e_{pl}, e_T, e_V)$. The charging current is then updated using a multiplicative rule:

$$I_{k+1} = I_k (1 + \lambda e_k)$$

In this equation, $I_k$ is the current at the current step, and $\lambda$ is a sensitivity factor. If there is ample headroom ($e_k > 0$), the current is allowed to increase. If a margin is tight or violated ($e_k \leq 0$), the current is reduced. This mechanism ensures that the controller "rides" the edge of the safest possible operating envelope without crossing it. 

To handle sudden transients or inadvertent limit crossings, RACL-B also includes an emergency repair mechanism that applies immediate, significant cuts to the current if a hard limit is breached. By starting from a conservative current and ramping up into available headroom, it prevents initial thermal or voltage overshoots that can occur with high-power charging.

## Evaluating Performance Under Uncertainty

To test the robustness of the RACL-B controller, it was evaluated against a suite of standard protocols across a "safety envelope" consisting of nine distinct conditions: three ambient temperatures (10, 25, and 40 °C) and three levels of cooling system health (100%, 60%, and 40%). A strict peak temperature limit of $T_{safe} = 45.0 \,^{\circ}\text{C}$ was enforced.

The results highlighted the vulnerabilities of existing methods:
*   **Standard Fixed Protocols:** A nominal 1.5C protocol failed (overheated) in 5 out of 9 conditions, reaching temperatures as high as 52 °C when cooling was degraded.
*   **Ambient-Scheduled Protocols:** Even industry-standard lookup tables that adjust for ambient temperature failed when faced with hidden cooling faults.
*   **Worst-Case Safe Protocols:** To remain safe across the entire envelope using a fixed current, the rate had to be lowered to 0.4C, resulting in an average charging time of over 100 minutes.

In contrast, RACL-B successfully completed the charge in all 9 conditions without a single safety violation. Because it adapts to the actual conditions, it achieved an average charging time of 62.9 minutes—**37.9% faster** than the worst-case safe fixed protocol.

## Detecting Hidden Faults: The CREST-B Monitor

A secondary contribution of this work is the development of a monitoring strategy called CREST-B (Transient-Credit fault readout). This monitor is designed to detect early-stage cooling faults from battery pack signals.

The challenge in fault detection is a phenomenon known as "shortcut learning." Machine learning models often latch onto smooth, non-decisive signals—like the absolute temperature of the pack—to predict a fault. However, absolute temperature is heavily influenced by the load and ambient conditions. A "hot-but-healthy" battery during a high-speed drive might look similar to a "failing-and-overheating" battery to a simple model.

CREST-B addresses this by focusing on sparse, electro-thermal transients. When a cooling fault occurs, the change in the thermal response is often brief and event-like compared to the steady heat generation of the load. CREST-B uses a low-pass transience residual ($R_{t,j} = F_{t,j} - (\text{LP}_\sigma F)_{t,j}$) to isolate these events.

![crest-b-fault-detection-performance-compared-to-standard-pooling-methods](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27781v1/x4.png)
*{fig:crest-b-fault-detection-performance-compared-to-standard-pooling-methods}: CREST-B focuses on sparse transient residuals rather than smoothed temperature data. This allows it to identify the true onset of a cooling fault (E) more accurately than standard attention-based or mean-pooling methods, which suffer from credit dilution.*

Theoretical analysis in the paper demonstrates that standard "pooling" methods (like taking the mean or using attention) quadratically dilute the evidence of sparse events. Specifically, the event-credit share vanishes as $\Theta(\epsilon^2)$ where $\epsilon$ is the fraction of time the fault signal is present. CREST-B "repairs" the readout by re-anchoring the prediction to these transient moments, significantly improving detection accuracy when operating conditions shift.

## Impact on Future Battery Management

The integration of the RACL-B controller and the CREST-B monitor forms a unified, margin-aware framework for battery management. This approach moves away from rigid, pre-calibrated schedules and toward a more intelligent system that understands its own safety boundaries.

The significance of this work lies in its practical deployability. By using a single-setting controller that is robust to unknown states, manufacturers can provide fast-charging capabilities that are safe in both freezing winters and sweltering summers, even as the vehicle's cooling hardware ages or degrades. This reliability is a key step toward making electric vehicles as convenient and dependable as their internal combustion counterparts.

Furthermore, the "repair-before-veto" principle provides a blueprint for managing other complex systems where safety is paramount but performance cannot be sacrificed. By prioritizing adaptive "repair" over binary "veto," we can create more resilient technology that performs optimally under the uncertainty of the real world.
