The 2026 FIFA World Cup represents a significant expansion in the history of international football, increasing the field from 32 to 48 teams. This change introduces a new Round of 32 and a total of 104 matches, creating a more complex landscape for statistical forecasting. Traditional prediction models often rely on a single, static measure of team strength—such as a current Elo rating—to estimate match outcomes. However, a team's current rating may not fully capture its momentum or recent performance trajectory. 

A recent study by researchers at Southern Illinois University Carbondale addresses this limitation by proposing a framework that incorporates the historical trajectory of team ratings. By applying Sufficient Dimension Reduction (SDR) to the recent history of Elo ratings, the authors extract informative patterns from high-dimensional temporal data. This approach allows for a more nuanced representation of team strength, which is then integrated into a probabilistic Poisson model to forecast match results and simulate the entire 2026 tournament.

## Quantifying Team Strength: The Elo Rating System

The foundation of many football forecasting models is the Elo rating system, which provides a continuous measure of relative team strength. In this system, a team's rating is updated after every match based on the difference between the observed result and the expected outcome. The expected outcome is a function of the rating difference between the two competing teams.

The specific implementation used in this research employs a modified Elo update formula:

$$
R'_h = R_h + \kappa \Gamma (S_h - E_h)
$$

In this equation, $R_h$ is the pre-match rating for the home team, $\kappa$ is a match-importance weight (e.g., higher for World Cup matches than friendlies), and $\Gamma$ is a goal-difference multiplier that accounts for the margin of victory. $S_h$ represents the actual match result (1 for a win, 0.5 for a draw, 0 for a loss), while $E_h$ is the expected score. To account for the advantage of playing at home, a home-field adjustment $\zeta = 100$ is added to the home team's rating in non-neutral matches, while $\zeta = 0$ for neutral-site games like those in the World Cup. All teams are initialized with a starting rating of $R_0 = 1,500$.

While the current Elo rating provides a snapshot of a team's standing, it ignores whether a team is on an upward or downward trend. For example, two teams might both have a rating of 1,800, but one might have climbed from 1,700 over the last six months, while the other might have dropped from 1,900. Intuitively, these trajectories suggest different levels of current form.

## The Challenge of Historical Data

Capturing these trajectories requires looking at a sequence of past ratings. However, directly adding lagged Elo ratings as predictors in a model introduces statistical challenges. If a researcher includes the last six months of Elo ratings, they face two primary issues:

1.  **High Dimensionality:** The number of predictors increases significantly, which can lead to overfitting, especially when the number of available matches for certain teams is limited.
2.  **Multicollinearity:** Consecutive monthly ratings are highly correlated. Including them simultaneously in a regression model makes the coefficient estimates unstable and difficult to interpret.

To overcome these hurdles, the research utilizes Sufficient Dimension Reduction (SDR). SDR aims to reduce the dimensionality of the predictor vector without losing the information necessary to predict the outcome. Instead of using all six lagged ratings, SDR finds a few "directions" or linear combinations of these ratings that contain the most predictive power.

## Sufficient Dimension Reduction Techniques

The study focuses on two specific SDR methods: Sliced Inverse Regression (SIR) and Sliced Average Variance Estimation (SAVE). These methods are applied to a $K=6$-dimensional vector of lagged monthly Elo differences, $x_m = (R_{h,t_m} - R_{a,t_m}, \dots, R_{h,t_m-K+1} - R_{a,t_m-K+1})^\top$.

Before applying these techniques, the predictor vector is "whitened" to ensure it has a zero mean and identity covariance, denoted as $\tilde{x}_m = \hat{\Sigma}_X^{-1/2} (x_m - \bar{x})$. 

**Sliced Inverse Regression (SIR)**
SIR identifies directions that maximize the separation between the means of the different outcome classes (Home Win, Draw, Away Win). It focuses on the kernel matrix:

$$
\hat{M}_{SIR} = \sum_{c \in \{H,D,A\}} \hat{\pi}_c \bar{x}_c \bar{x}_c^\top
$$

Here, $\bar{x}_c$ is the mean of the whitened predictors for matches resulting in outcome $c$, and $\hat{\pi}_c$ is the proportion of matches with that outcome. SIR effectively finds the directions along which the "average" team trajectory differs most between wins, draws, and losses.

**Sliced Average Variance Estimation (SAVE)**
While SIR looks at mean differences, SAVE also considers the variance within each outcome class. This allows it to capture more complex, non-linear relationships. Its kernel is defined as:

$$
\hat{M}_{SAVE} = \sum_{c \in \{H,D,A\}} \hat{\pi}_c (I_K - \hat{\Sigma}_c)^2
$$

By taking the top $d$ eigenvectors of these kernels, the researchers create a projection matrix $B$ that transforms the 6D history into a $d$-dimensional score $z_m = B^\top \tilde{x}_m$. In this study, $d$ is set to either 1 or 2, drastically reducing the complexity of the input data.

## Probabilistic Goal Modeling

The extracted SDR scores are then integrated into a Poisson double-regression model. This model treats the number of goals scored by the home team ($G_H$) and the away team ($G_A$) as independent Poisson random variables. The expected number of goals for each team, $\lambda_H$ and $\lambda_A$, is modeled using a log-linear relationship:

$$
\log \lambda_{H_m} = \mu_H + \sum_{j=1}^d \xi_j z_{m,j} + \delta_H N_m + \eta_1 \bar{G}^+_{h} + \eta_2 \bar{G}^-_{a}
$$
$$
\log \lambda_{A_m} = \mu_A - \sum_{j=1}^d \xi_j z_{m,j} + \delta_A N_m + \eta_3 \bar{G}^+_{a} + \eta_4 \bar{G}^-_{h}
$$

In these equations, $z_{m,j}$ represents the $j$-th SDR score (for $j=1, \dots, d$), $N_m$ is an indicator for neutral venues, and variables like $\bar{G}^+_{h}$ represent recent scoring and conceding averages to capture short-term offensive and defensive form. By using a Poisson distribution, the model can calculate the probability of every possible scoreline (e.g., 2-1, 0-0, 1-3). The probability of a specific match outcome (win, draw, or loss) is then found by summing the probabilities of all scorelines that result in that outcome.

## Evaluation and 2026 Forecast

The researchers validated their approach by "backtesting" it on the 2018 and 2022 FIFA World Cups. They compared the SDR-based models against several baselines, including standard logistic regression, XGBoost (a popular machine learning algorithm), and time-series models like ARIMA. 

The performance was measured using the Ranked Probability Score (RPS), where a lower score indicates better probabilistic accuracy. The SDR models significantly outperformed the baselines. While standard models achieved RPS values around 0.21, the SIR and SAVE models (with $d=2$) achieved scores near 0.127. This suggests that the historical trajectory captured by SDR contains vital information that current Elo ratings and standard machine learning features miss.

For the 2026 World Cup, the researchers ran 5,000 Monte Carlo simulations using the best-performing SDR models. The simulation followed the official FIFA tournament structure, including the new group stage format and the Round of 32 bracket assignments. 

Key findings from the 2026 forecast include:
- **Top Contenders:** Spain emerged as the favorite to win the title, followed by Argentina and France. 
- **Consistency:** Both SIR and SAVE models were highly consistent in their predictions for the group stage and the later knockout rounds.
- **Tournament Path:** In a representative simulation, Spain and France were projected to meet in a high-stakes semi-final, with Spain ultimately advancing to face Argentina in the final.

## Conclusion

The research demonstrates that predicting tournament outcomes benefits significantly from analyzing how team strengths evolve over time, rather than just looking at their current status. By using Sufficient Dimension Reduction, the authors successfully distilled complex, correlated histories of Elo ratings into stable, predictive scores. 

The resulting framework not only provides a high level of accuracy in historical backtests but also offers a detailed, probabilistic roadmap for the upcoming 48-team 2026 World Cup. As the sport continues to evolve and data becomes more prevalent, techniques like SDR offer a robust way to handle the increasing dimensionality of performance metrics in sports analytics.
