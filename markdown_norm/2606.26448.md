## The Theory-Building Bottleneck in Cognitive Science

Scientific discovery has historically been a labor-intensive process, relying on the intuition and creative synthesis of human researchers. While automation has successfully accelerated fields like chemistry and materials science, the behavioral sciences—specifically psychology and cognitive science—have faced a persistent "theory-building bottleneck." While we can now automate large-scale data collection via online platforms and use machine learning to fit complex models to that data, the act of generating a novel, interpretable theory from empirical observations has remained a human domain.

A new framework called AUTOCOG (AUTOmated COGnitive Scientist) aims to change this by closing the loop of scientific discovery. AUTOCOG is an agentic AI system that manages the entire scientific cycle: it generates psychological theories, designs adversarial experiments to test them, collects behavioral data from human participants, analyzes the results, and iteratively refines its theories. By expressing theories as both natural language descriptions and executable Python code, the system ensures that its discoveries are both human-interpretable and programmatically testable.

## The Architecture of an Automated Scientist

AUTOCOG operates through a collaborative multi-agent architecture powered by large language models (LLMs). The system is initialized by a human researcher who defines the task domain, the experimental design space (e.g., the range of possible stimuli), and a set of initial "seed" theories. From there, the system takes over in a four-stage iterative process.

### 1. Adversarial Experimental Design
The system maintains two competing theories. To differentiate them, two LLM agents—each acting as an advocate for one of the theories—collaborate to design an experiment that will maximize the discrepancy between their predictions. This adversarial approach ensures that the collected data is highly informative for model selection. For instance, if one theory predicts that humans only look at the most important piece of information while another predicts they average everything, the system will search for a specific set of stimuli where those two strategies lead to opposite choices.

### 2. Behavioral Data Collection
Once an experiment is designed, it is automatically deployed. AUTOCOG is flexible regarding its data source: it can collect data from human participants via online recruitment platforms (using tools like SweetBean and AutoRA), from foundation models acting as "in-silico" subjects, or from existing cognitive models. In studies with humans, the system typically recruits a sample size of $N=25$ per cycle to provide sufficient statistical power for its proposed metrics.

### 3. Analysis and Arbitration
After data collection, an LLM agent serves as an "arbitrator." It receives the experimental results and compares the observed behavior against the generative predictions of the incumbent theories. Rather than just fitting parameters, the system evaluates how well the theories *generate* the observed distribution of choices. The arbitrator diagnoses why a theory succeeded or failed and recommends specific directions for revision.

### 4. Theory Revision
In the final stage, the system updates its theories. This can involve a "new model" update (keeping the linguistic concept but changing the code) or a "new theory" update (generating an entirely new concept and implementation). Crucially, AUTOCOG includes an "inner critique loop": a candidate theory is only accepted if it improves the aggregate fit across *all* previously collected experimental data, creating a cumulative pressure for generalizable and parsimonious explanations.

## Closing the Loop: Recovering Known Heuristics

To validate that AUTOCOG can actually navigate the space of psychological theories, the researchers first tested its ability to recover "ground-truth" strategies from simulated data. In the domain of multi-attribute decision-making, psychologists have long debated between compensatory strategies like the Weighted-Additive (WADD) model—which sums up all available information—and non-compensatory heuristics like "Take-the-Best" (TTB), which ignores all but the most valid cue.

In these "recovery" simulations, AUTOCOG was seeded with incorrect theories and tasked with discovering the true underlying strategy. The system successfully recovered canonical heuristics like TTB and WADD even in the presence of "action noise" ($\epsilon$), where participants occasionally make random choices. As the noise parameter $\epsilon$ increased from 0 to $0.5$ and $0.75$, the system's performance naturally degraded, but it remained capable of capturing the core qualitative mechanisms of the target strategies.

Beyond standard models, AUTOCOG also demonstrated the ability to recover highly unconventional or "non-canonical" strategies, such as "Take-the-Worst" (choosing based on the least valid cue) or "Perseveration" (simply repeating the previous choice). This flexibility proves that the system is not merely biased toward known psychological findings but is genuinely driven by the evidence in the behavioral data.

## Discovering New Theories from Human Behavior

The most significant test for AUTOCOG was its application to real human behavior in multi-attribute decision-making tasks. These tasks involved participants choosing between two products based on expert ratings with varying validities (accuracies).

### Binary Cues: Unifying Heuristics
In experiments where cue ratings were binary (e.g., a "1" or "0"), AUTOCOG's theories improved significantly over five cycles. The mean squared error (MSE) between the best surfaced theory and human behavior dropped from 0.093 to 0.0097, with a statistically significant trend over cycles ($\beta = -0.014$). 

The "winning" theory produced by the system was a "Non-linear Subjective Weighting Model." This theory proposes that humans do not use objective validities directly but instead apply a power-law transformation to them. This single mathematical framework is capable of unifying TTB, Tallying, and WADD as limiting cases depending on the exponent, providing a more flexible and accurate account of how humans integrate information.

### Cardinal Cues: The "Diminishing Returns" Discovery
When the experimental space was expanded to include cardinal ratings (integers from 1 to 5), AUTOCOG discovered a novel regularity that had not been explicitly programmed into it. After five cycles of human data collection, the system converged on a theory it named "Diminishing Returns WADD."

This theory suggests that human decision-makers apply a concave utility function to cardinal ratings before weighting them. In other words, the subjective difference between a rating of 1 and 2 is perceived as larger than the difference between a rating of 4 and 5. This mirrors the concept of "diminishing sensitivity" found in other areas of psychology, such as prospect theory, but surfaced here autonomously in the context of multi-attribute choice.

## Prospective Validation of the Discovery

To ensure that the "Diminishing Returns" finding was a genuine scientific discovery rather than an artifact of the LLM's internal biases, the researchers conducted a preregistered follow-up study with new participants. They tested three specific hypotheses derived from the theory:

1.  **$H_1$ (Model Discrimination):** Participants' choices were significantly better predicted by the Diminishing Returns WADD model than by standard WADD or TTB ($p < 0.001$).
2.  **$H_2$ (Steep-vs-Flat):** Participants showed a significant preference for options that had an advantage in the "steep" (low-value) region of the rating scale compared to an objectively equal advantage in the "flat" (high-value) region ($M = 0.582$, $p < 0.001$).
3.  **$H_3$ (Level-Shift):** The same cue-value difference had a stronger effect on choice probability when presented in a low-value range than in a high-value range ($M_{\Delta} = 0.029$, $p = 0.036$).

The confirmation of these hypotheses provides strong empirical support for a novel regularity in human decision-making that was discovered entirely through the closed-loop autonomous system.

## A New Frontier for Scientific Discovery

AUTOCOG represents a shift in the role of the human scientist. Instead of manually designing every trial and agonizing over model specifications, the researcher now acts as a high-level architect, defining the "sandbox" in which the automated scientist operates. 

The significance of this work lies in three areas:
*   **Auditability:** Every step of the discovery—the failed theories, the adversarial designs, the arbitrator's reasoning—is logged as a machine-readable artifact. This creates a transparent and reproducible "trace" of how a theory was developed.
*   **Cumulative Science:** The system's requirement that new theories must explain all previous data prevents "one-off" findings and encourages the development of unifying principles.
*   **Open-Ended Search:** By treating theory discovery as an exploration of program space rather than just parameter optimization, AUTOCOG can surface qualitative insights that human researchers might overlook.

As AI systems become more capable of reasoning and synthesis, tools like AUTOCOG suggest a future where the pace of scientific progress in understanding human nature is no longer limited by the speed of manual theory-building, but by the creativity of the questions we ask the machine to solve.
