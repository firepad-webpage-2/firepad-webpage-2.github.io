## Foundations of Image Alignment and Similarity Transformations

Image alignment, often referred to as image registration, is a fundamental process in computer vision that involves overlaying two or more images of the same scene taken at different times, from different viewpoints, or by different sensors. The primary goal is to determine a geometric transformation that maps coordinates from one image (the "sensed" image) to the coordinate system of another (the "reference" image). This process is vital for applications requiring high-fidelity spatial correspondence, such as medical diagnostics, where doctors compare MRI scans over several months, or panorama stitching, where multiple photos are merged into a single wide-angle view.

*{fig:overview-of-the-alignment-framework}: The general flow of the similarity transformation alignment process, showing the sequential estimation of scale/rotation followed by translation.*

In real-world scenarios, the relationship between two images is rarely just a simple shift in position. It often involves a similarity transformation, which includes translation, rotation, and scaling. For instance, if a photographer moves closer to an object or rotates the camera slightly, the resulting image will be scaled (zoomed) and rotated relative to the first. Mathematically, if $x$ is the reference image and $y$ is the transformed image, the relationship can be expressed as:

$$y[p] = x[s R(\theta) p + \Delta p]$$

where $s$ represents the scale factor, $\theta$ is the rotation angle, $R(\theta)$ is the rotation matrix, and $\Delta p$ is the translation vector. Modern systems, particularly those running on mobile hardware, require alignment algorithms that are not only accurate but also computationally efficient. While feature-based methods like SIFT or SURF are robust, they often require significant processing power to extract keypoints and perform outlier rejection. Intensity-based methods, specifically those using cross-correlation, offer a faster alternative by working directly with pixel values in the frequency domain.

## Decoupling Geometry with the Fourier–Mellin Transform

One of the most effective techniques for handling similarity transformations is the Fourier–Mellin Transform (FMT). The FMT relies on two elegant properties of the Fourier magnitude spectrum. First, the magnitude of the Fourier transform of an image is invariant to spatial translation. This means that if an image is shifted, its frequency magnitude remains the same. This property allows researchers to "decouple" translation from rotation and scaling.

Second, the FMT utilizes the fact that rotation and scaling in the spatial domain are converted into simple translations in a log-polar coordinate system. By taking the Fourier transform of the images, calculating the magnitude, and then resampling that magnitude onto a log-polar grid, the scaling factor $s$ becomes a horizontal shift and the rotation $\theta$ becomes a vertical shift. This transformation simplifies a complex geometric problem into a linear shift estimation problem, which can then be solved using cross-correlation.

However, standard FMT implementations often suffer from accuracy limitations. They typically find the best alignment by looking for the peak of a discrete cross-correlation function. This restricts the estimated parameters to integer pixel values or requires heavy interpolation, which can be inaccurate due to the resampling artifacts introduced during the log-polar transformation.

## Subpixel Precision via the Auxiliary Function Method

To overcome the limitations of discrete correlation, this research integrates a high-precision maximization algorithm based on the auxiliary function method (also known as the Majorization–Minimization algorithm). Instead of searching for a peak on a fixed grid, this approach treats the correlation as a continuous function. The goal is to maximize the phase-only cross-correlation function $\breve{\Phi}_{xy}(p)$:

$$\breve{\Phi}_{xy}(p) = \frac{1}{NM} \sum_{k \in K} \sum_{l \in L} w_{kl} \hat{\Phi}_{xy}(\omega_{kl}) \exp(j \omega_{kl}^\top p)$$

where $w_{kl}$ are weights (set to the inverse magnitude for phase-only correlation) and $\hat{\Phi}_{xy}$ is the cross-spectrum. The objective is to find the subpixel translation $\tilde{\Delta} p$ that maximizes this value:

$$\tilde{\Delta} p = \arg \max_{p \in \mathbb{R}^2} \breve{\Phi}_{xy}(p)$$

The auxiliary function method solves this by iteratively constructing a surrogate function $Q(p, \theta)$ that "majorizes" (or in this case, lower-bounds) the objective function. By maximizing this easier-to-handle surrogate function, the algorithm progressively refines the estimate. Unlike standard gradient-based optimization, the auxiliary function method ensures that each step increases the correlation value, leading to a stable and highly accurate subpixel estimate for the translation vector.

## The Two-Stage Proposed Pipeline

The proposed method applies this subpixel maximization in two distinct stages. This ensures that every parameter—scale, rotation, and translation—is estimated with high precision.

### Stage 1: Scale and Rotation Estimation
The process begins by transforming the input images into their log-magnitude spectra. Using log-magnitude, defined as $S_x[r] = \log(|\hat{x}(\omega_r)| + 1)$, is crucial because it compresses the dynamic range of the frequency data, preventing low-frequency components from dominating the correlation and allowing finer high-frequency details to guide the alignment.

![scale-and-rotation-alignment-process](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11565v1/scale_and_rotation_alignment.png)
*{fig:scale-and-rotation-alignment-process}: The first stage of the pipeline transforms the frequency magnitude into log-polar coordinates, where the auxiliary function method estimates the shifts corresponding to scale and rotation.*

Once the log-polar spectra are created, the auxiliary function method estimates the subpixel shifts $\tilde{\rho}$ (log-scale) and $\tilde{\phi}$ (rotation). From these shifts, the actual physical parameters are derived:

$$\tilde{s} = \exp\left(\frac{\log(\min(N, M)) \tilde{\rho}}{N}\right)$$
$$\tilde{\theta} = \frac{2\pi \tilde{\phi}}{M}$$

The transformed image $y$ is then "un-warped" to compensate for this estimated scale and rotation, resulting in an intermediate image $y'$:

$$y'[p] = y\left[ \frac{1}{\tilde{s}} R(-\tilde{\theta}) p \right]$$

### Stage 2: Translation Alignment
With the rotation and scale corrected, the remaining task is to find the spatial translation $\Delta p$ between the reference image $x$ and the intermediate image $y'$.

![translation-alignment-stage](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11565v1/translation_alignment.png)
*{fig:translation-alignment-stage}: The second stage performs high-precision spatial cross-correlation to align the images once geometric warping has been removed.*

The auxiliary function method is applied once more in the spatial domain to find the subpixel peak of the cross-correlation between $x$ and $y'$. This final shift is used to produce the fully aligned output image $y''$:

$$y''[p] = y'[p - \tilde{\Delta} p]$$

## Experimental Validation and Performance

The researchers tested their approach using 2K-resolution images from high-definition standard sets, including "MusicBox", "Moss", and "Ship". They generated misaligned pairs by applying random scale factors between 0.8 and 1.2, rotations between -30 and 30 degrees, and translations within 5 pixels.

| Image Set | Parameter | Baseline Error | Proposed Error |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **MusicBox** | Translation (px) | 0.340 | 0.056 |
| | Angle (deg) | 0.509 | 0.101 |
| | Scale | 0.005 | 0.001 |
| **Moss** | Translation (px) | 0.174 | 0.043 |
| | Angle (deg) | 0.233 | 0.113 |
| | Scale | 0.003 | 0.001 |

The results showed a significant reduction in error across all categories. For the "MusicBox" dataset, the average absolute error for horizontal translation dropped from 0.340 pixels to just 0.056 pixels—a nearly sixfold improvement in accuracy. Similar gains were seen in rotation and scale estimation.

![comparison-of-alignment-for-musicbox](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11565v1/u02_MusicBox_2K_dcc_aligned_image_3.png)
*{fig:comparison-of-alignment-for-musicbox}: Aligned result for the MusicBox image using the proposed method, showing high visual fidelity to the original reference.*

However, this precision comes with a computational trade-off. Because the auxiliary function method is iterative, it takes longer to converge than a simple discrete peak-finding algorithm. The experiments indicated that the proposed method is approximately 2.7 to 3.1 times slower than the baseline FMT. For a "MusicBox" image, the baseline took about 7.9 seconds, while the proposed method took roughly 24.5 seconds on a standard CPU. This suggests that while the method is highly effective for tasks requiring extreme precision, it may need hardware acceleration (like GPU implementation) for real-time mobile applications.

## Significance and Broader Impact

The ability to achieve subpixel registration for similarity transformations without the heavy computational burden of complex feature matching is a significant step forward. This method is particularly relevant in domains where subpixel shifts can lead to incorrect data interpretation.

In **medical imaging**, subpixel accuracy allows for the precise tracking of tumor growth or the alignment of multi-modal scans (like PET and CT), where even a half-pixel error might translate to millimeters of spatial discrepancy in a patient's body. In **high dynamic range (HDR) photography**, multiple exposures are merged to capture more detail in shadows and highlights. If these exposures are not perfectly aligned at the subpixel level, the final image will appear blurry or exhibit "ghosting" artifacts around edges.

By integrating the auxiliary function method into the Fourier–Mellin framework, this work provides a robust mathematical foundation for achieving this level of precision. Future research may focus on optimizing the iterative process or improving the resampling techniques (such as using higher-order interpolation) to further reduce the errors introduced during the log-polar transformation.
