## Thermodynamic Computing for Sustainable Scientific Workloads

The rapid expansion of artificial intelligence (AI) and data-intensive scientific research has created an escalating demand for computational power, leading to significant concerns regarding energy sustainability. Projections suggest that data centers will consume an increasingly large share of global electricity, driven by workloads that current hardware architectures were not originally optimized for. This discrepancy is often described through the concept of the "hardware lottery," which posits that the success of specific algorithms is often tied more to the availability of compatible hardware than to the inherent superiority of the algorithm itself.

Most contemporary computing relies on Graphics Processing Units (GPUs) or Central Processing Units (CPUs) that function through deterministic logic. In these systems, energy is expended to suppress thermal noise and maintain a clear distinction between binary states. However, many modern computational challenges—ranging from generative AI to molecular modeling—are fundamentally probabilistic or combinatorial. For these tasks, researchers often employ algorithms that simulate natural stochastic processes, such as Simulated Annealing or Langevin Monte Carlo.

Thermodynamic computing represents an alternative paradigm that, instead of fighting thermal fluctuations, treats them as a computational resource. By utilizing the intrinsic physics of a system at room temperature, thermodynamic hardware can perform sampling and optimization tasks with significantly less energy than traditional digital architectures. This paper explores the application of this technology to mRNA codon optimization, providing a concrete demonstration of how thermodynamic sampling can address complex pharmaceutical challenges while drastically reducing energy consumption.

## The Biological Context: mRNA Codon Optimization

The development of mRNA-based therapeutics, such as vaccines and protein replacement therapies, requires the careful design of genetic sequences. Proteins are composed of chains of amino acids, and each amino acid is encoded by a sequence of three nucleotides known as a codon. Because multiple different codons can encode the same amino acid (a property called redundancy), a single protein sequence can be represented by a vast number of synonymous mRNA sequences.

Selecting the "best" sequence from this astronomical number of possibilities is the goal of codon optimization. The choice of codons significantly impacts how efficiently the host organism (such as a human cell or a bacterial factory like *E. coli*) translates the mRNA into a protein. Key factors in this optimization include:
*   **Codon Usage Bias:** Host organisms prefer certain codons over others. Using preferred codons generally leads to higher protein expression.
*   **GC Content:** The proportion of guanine (G) and cytosine (C) bases affects the stability and secondary structure of the mRNA molecule. Deviations from an optimal range can hinder translation.
*   **Nucleotide Repeats:** Long runs of the same nucleotide can cause "slippage" during translation or lead to manufacturing difficulties.

For a protein like the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, which consists of $1,273$ amino acids, the number of possible mRNA sequences is far too large to search exhaustively. This makes codon optimization a complex combinatorial optimization problem, typically addressed using heuristic methods like Genetic Algorithms (GAs).

## Problem Formulation and Mathematical Mapping

To solve the codon optimization problem on thermodynamic hardware, the biological constraints must be translated into a mathematical energy function. The objective is to find a sequence of codons that minimizes the total energy $E$:

$$E = w_f \sum_{i=1}^L u(c_i) + w_{GC} (f_{GC} - 0.5)^2 + w_R \sum_{i=1}^{L-1} r(c_i, c_{i+1})$$

In this expression, $L$ represents the length of the protein sequence, and $c_i$ is the codon chosen at position $i$. The term $u(c_i)$ represents the penalty for using a codon that is not preferred by the host. The global constraint $f_{GC}$ represents the fraction of GC bases in the entire sequence, and $w_{GC}$ scales the penalty for deviating from the target fraction (0.5). The term $r(c_i, c_{i+1})$ penalizes repeating sequences at the boundaries between adjacent codons. The weights $w_f$, $w_{GC}$, and $w_R$ determine the relative importance of each constraint.

The researchers mapped this problem onto two physical models:
1.  **Potts Model:** This model uses categorical variables to represent the synonymous codons at each position. If an amino acid can be represented by 6 different codons, the corresponding variable in the Potts model has $K = 6$ states.
2.  **Ising Model:** To run the problem on binary thermodynamic hardware (which uses probabilistic bits or "p-bits"), the Potts model is converted into an Ising model using Domain-Wall Encoding (DWE). In this encoding, a $K$-state variable is represented by $K-1$ binary spins. This mapping allows the problem to be solved on hardware that only understands $+1$ and $-1$ states.

## Hardware Principles: The Thermodynamic Sampling Unit (TSU)

The core of this approach is the Thermodynamic Sampling Unit (TSU), a programmable chip fabricated using standard CMOS technology. Unlike traditional processors, the TSU operates by allowing its internal units (p-bits for the Ising model or p-dits for the Potts model) to fluctuate according to their thermal environment. 

The TSU follows a "flash-sample-read" cycle. First, the host computer "flashes" the weights and biases (representing the biological constraints) onto the chip. The chip then performs rapid, analog sampling. Because the system is physical, it naturally tends toward states with lower energy—the Boltzmann distribution. Thermal noise allows the system to "jump" out of local minima (suboptimal solutions) to explore the solution space more effectively, eventually settling near the global minimum.

One challenge in this mapping is the GC content term, which is a global constraint that couples every codon position to every other position. Standard TSU hardware is designed for sparse, local connections. To overcome this, the researchers introduced an adaptive coefficient $\lambda$. This coefficient acts as a linear bias that is updated by the host computer between sampling steps:

$$\lambda_{t+1} = \lambda_t + \alpha (f_{GC} - 0.5)$$

Where $\alpha$ is a small step size. This mechanism steers the system toward the target GC content without requiring the complex all-to-all physical wiring that would otherwise be necessary.

## Results and Comparative Energy Analysis

The researchers benchmarked the thermodynamic approach against a Genetic Algorithm (GA) running on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU. The results demonstrated that all methods—the Potts model, the Ising model, and the GA—achieved comparable optimization quality. However, the energy consumption profiles were vastly different.

The energy consumption for the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein optimization was measured and estimated as follows:
*   **Potts Model on TSU:** Approximately $0.084\text{ nJ}$ per run.
*   **Ising Model on TSU:** Approximately $82\text{ nJ}$ per run.
*   **GPU (Measured):** The GA required $21\text{ J}$ and the Ising sampler on GPU required $84\text{ J}$.

The TSU's efficiency stems from several factors. First, the energy per spin update ($E_{\text{cell}}$) is approximately $1.3\text{ fJ}$, which is orders of magnitude lower than the energy required for a floating-point operation on a GPU. Second, the thermodynamic hardware avoids the overhead associated with kernel launches and memory latency that often plagues sparse, probabilistic workloads on traditional architectures. Even when comparing against an "idealized" GPU (assuming 100% peak efficiency), the TSU remained $160$ to $2.4 \times 10^5$ times more efficient.

## Significance for Pharmaceutical Research

The findings of this study suggest that thermodynamic computing could significantly impact the pharmaceutical research pipeline. The ability to perform complex optimizations in approximately $8\text{ ms}$ while consuming nanojoules of energy makes it feasible to integrate these steps into high-throughput, real-time workflows.

The current SARS-CoV-2 spike protein formulation used $3,147$ spins, which is well within the capacity of near-term TSU designs capable of supporting up to $250,000$ spins. This provides ample room to optimize much larger protein complexes or to incorporate more sophisticated biological constraints, such as mRNA folding energy or more complex motif avoidance.

By providing a methodological template for mapping discrete biological optimization problems onto physical hardware, this work demonstrates that the transition to thermodynamic paradigms is not just a theoretical possibility but a practical path toward more sustainable and powerful scientific computing. The massive energy savings achieved—ranging from $10^5$ to $10^9$ times—address the environmental and economic challenges of modern drug discovery and set a precedent for the future of energy-efficient AI and scientific simulation.
