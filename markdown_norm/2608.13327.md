## The Adaptive Nature of Organized Crime

Traditional theories of criminal deterrence often treat criminal organizations as static targets. In these models, law enforcement agencies increase the probability of detection or the severity of punishment to make criminal acts "too expensive" for individuals. However, organized crime is fundamentally different from isolated individual crime because groups are adaptive. When a high-ranking member of a cartel is arrested, the organization does not simply vanish; it reroutes its supply chains, promotes junior members, or splits into smaller, more agile coalitions.

Recent research investigates how these organizations use internal "division norms"—the rules governing how profits are shared—to protect themselves. By treating the organization as a collection of potential coalitions that can re-form even after law enforcement strikes, we can better understand why some groups are more resilient than others. The core problem is not just how to catch a criminal, but how to dismantle an entire network when the network can reorganize itself.

## A Three-Stage Strategic Game

The relationship between law enforcement and a criminal organization can be modeled as a three-stage game involving an "authority" and $n$ potential criminal members, denoted by the set $N = \{1, \dots, n\}$.

1.  **Norm Formation:** The criminal organization establishes a "ledger" or division norm. This norm determines how the proceeds $v(S) > 0$ generated by any subset of members (a coalition $S$) are divided among them. Crucially, this norm is considered "rigid"—it is a slow-moving social or organizational contract that cannot be instantly rewritten the moment the police start making arrests.
2.  **Deterrence Assignment:** An authority, observing these division norms, commits its limited deterrence capacity. It assigns a cost $c_i$ to each member $i$. These costs represent the expected loss to the member from law enforcement actions, such as the probability of imprisonment or asset forfeiture.
3.  **Coalition Formation:** Given the deterrence costs, the members decide whether to form a coalition. A member $i$ will participate in a coalition $S$ only if their assigned rent $b_i(S)$ exceeds the deterrence cost $c_i$. The authority's goal is to set the costs $c_i$ such that no coalition $S$ is profitable for all its members.

In this framework, the organization is "dismantled" if the authority manages to block every possible non-empty coalition. The proceeds function $v$ is assumed to be monotone, meaning larger groups generate at least as much profit as smaller ones: $S \subseteq T \implies v(S) \leq v(T) \text{ for all } S, T \subseteq N$.

## The Dismantling Theorem: Finding the Weakest Path

A central challenge for law enforcement is that they don't just have to stop the "grand coalition" of all members; they have to stop every possible subgroup. If the police only target the leader, the remaining members might form a smaller, still-profitable coalition.

The researchers propose a **Dismantling Theorem** to calculate the minimum budget required to achieve complete deterrence. If we assume "population monotonicity"—meaning a member's rent doesn't decrease when the group grows ($b_i(S) \leq b_i(T)$ if $S \subseteq T$ and $i \in S$)—then the minimum budget $E(b)$ can be found using a recursive formula:

$$
V_b(R) = \min_{i \in R} [b_i(R) + V_b(R \setminus \{i\})]
$$

where $V_b(\emptyset) = 0$. This formula represents a "shortest path" problem on a lattice of subsets. To dismantle a group $R$, the authority picks one member $i$ to "remove" by assigning a deterrence cost equal to their rent $b_i(R)$, then moves on to dismantle the remaining group $R \setminus \{i\}$. The total cost $E(b)$ is the sum of these costs along the cheapest possible removal sequence.

## Rethinking Target Priority: The Hub Effect

Common intuition suggests that law enforcement should always target the "weakest link"—the member who requires the least amount of deterrence to be removed. However, this research shows that a "greedy" strategy is often suboptimal.

Consider a "hub-and-spoke" model where one central figure (the hub) enables everyone else to make a profit. While the hub might command a very high individual rent, removing them might cause the rents of all other members to collapse. In this case, it might be cheaper to pay a high "upfront" cost to remove the hub rather than trying to deter dozens of smaller, highly profitable sub-groups that would persist if the hub remained.

This is formalized through the concept of the **enabling effect**. The effect of member $i$ on member $j$ in a group $R$ is defined as:

$$
e_{i \to j}(R) = b_j(R) - b_j(R \setminus \{i\})
$$

If member $i$ has a massive enabling effect on others, they become a high-priority target because their removal "cheapens" the deterrence required for everyone else. Strategic targeting requires looking past current rents and calculating how a member's removal impacts the continuation rents of the survivors.

## The Shapley Ledger: The Ultimate Defense

If an organization knows the authority will try to find the cheapest dismantling path, what is the best way to share profits to prevent this? The research identifies the **Shapley Ledger** as the uniquely most resilient division norm.

The Shapley value is a classic concept in cooperative game theory that assigns each member a share of the total value based on their average marginal contribution across all possible ordering sequences. In this context, the researchers show that if an organization uses the Shapley value to divide its proceeds, it makes every possible dismantling path exactly as expensive as every other path.

The total cost to the authority under a Shapley ledger is equal to the "average bill" $\Pi_v(N)$, which is the average cost across all possible removal orders:

$$
\Pi_v(R) = \frac{1}{|R|!} \sum_{\pi \in S(R)} C_{\pi}(b; R)
$$

By equalizing the cost of all paths, the organization ensures there are no "weak spots" for the authority to exploit. The Shapley ledger satisfies the property that a member's rent is exactly the difference in the average potential of the group:

$$
b_i(S) = \Pi_v(S) - \Pi_v(S \setminus \{i\})
$$

This strategy forces the authority to pay the maximum possible price to dismantle the group, assuming the organization must maintain internal stability (population monotonicity).

## Liquidity and the Power of Transfers

The model also considers "liquidity"—the ability of an organization to move money around to help members who are being targeted by the police. In a "rigid" ledger, if the police increase the pressure on member $A$, the organization cannot easily take money from member $B$ to compensate $A$.

However, in reality, many criminal groups are highly liquid. They use "solidarity funds" or internal insurance to shield targeted members. The researchers introduce a liquidity parameter $\rho$ (where $0 \leq \rho \leq 1$). As $\rho$ increases, the organization can reallocate more of its proceeds to counter specific deterrence costs. The blocking condition for a coalition $S$ becomes more difficult for the authority:

$$
\sum_{i \in S} [c_i - (1-\rho)b_i(S)]_+ \geq \rho v(S)
$$

The result is intuitive: the more liquid an organization is, the more expensive it is to dismantle. When an organization is perfectly liquid ($\rho = 1$), the authority loses the ability to target individuals strategically and must effectively counter the total profit of the entire group. This underscores the importance of anti-money laundering (AML) and financial disruption policies. By reducing the "liquidity" of a criminal group, law enforcement forces the group into a more rigid structure where individual-based targeting becomes effective again.

## Conclusion and Policy Insights

This research shifts the focus of deterrence from individual actors to organizational structures. It provides a formal mathematical basis for several key observations in modern policing:

*   **Targeting Successors:** Effective deterrence requires predicting how a group will look *after* a strike. The most valuable target is the one whose removal causes the largest drop in the profitability of the remaining members.
*   **The Cost of Fairness:** Organizations that use "fair" division rules like the Shapley value are actually more resilient to law enforcement because they eliminate exploitable asymmetries in their cost structure.
*   **Financial Disruption:** Measures that impede internal transfers are not just about "following the money"; they are fundamental to the success of targeted deterrence. Without liquidity, an organization cannot defend its members from concentrated law enforcement pressure.

By understanding the division norms that hold these groups together, authorities can move beyond simple "whack-a-mole" tactics and develop strategies that systematically increase the cost of maintaining a criminal enterprise.
