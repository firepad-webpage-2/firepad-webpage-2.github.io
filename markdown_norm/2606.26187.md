Indoor localization systems have reached a point where tracking a user's position with centimeter-level accuracy is standard practice, particularly when using Ultra-Wideband (UWB) technology. However, knowing where a person is located is only half of the story for many context-aware applications. For an interactive museum guide, a smart home interface, or a secure access system, knowing the direction the user is facing—their orientation—is just as vital as knowing their coordinates. 

Most existing systems solve this by adding more hardware, such as Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) containing gyroscopes and accelerometers, or by requiring the user to wear multiple UWB tags. These solutions increase the cost, power consumption, and physical footprint of wearable devices. This research introduces a method to estimate human body yaw orientation using only the data already provided by a single UWB tag: the Channel Impulse Response (CIR). By processing this data through a specialized deep learning architecture based on State Space Models (Mamba), the system achieves accurate orientation tracking without auxiliary sensors.

## The Role of Channel Impulse Response in Orientation

UWB technology operates by sending very short radio pulses. When these pulses travel from a wearable tag to a fixed anchor, they don't just take a single path. They reflect off walls, floors, and—most importantly—the user’s own body. The anchor records a "Channel Impulse Response" (CIR), which is essentially a timestamped log of all these arriving signal components.

The human body acts as a significant obstacle to UWB signals, a phenomenon known as body shadowing. When a user turns, their body blocks or reflects the signal in different ways, changing the "shape" of the CIR. For instance, if the tag is on the user's chest and they face away from an anchor, the signal must travel through or around the torso, attenuating the direct path and enhancing certain multipath reflections. This research leverages these subtle, orientation-dependent patterns within the CIR to infer the user's yaw angle $\theta$ without needing a compass or gyroscope.

## Architecture: Multi-Scale Embeddings and Conditioning

The proposed system does not look at a single anchor in isolation. Instead, it aggregates observations from a network of $L$ fixed anchors. Because different anchors might be visible at different times, the model uses a binary mask to handle missing data.

To extract features from the raw, 150-sample CIR vectors, the researchers designed a Multi-Scale Convolutional CIR Embedding module. UWB signals contain features at different temporal scales: sharp peaks representing direct paths and broader "smears" representing complex environment reflections. The module uses four parallel convolutional branches with different kernel sizes to capture these features.

A critical challenge in wearable tech is that users might place a tag in different locations—a pocket, an arm, or the chest. Each placement results in a different shadowing pattern. To make the model versatile, the researchers included a "body-part conditioning" module. This module takes an indicator of the tag's location and maps it to a $d$-dimensional embedding that is added to the CIR features. This allows the model to "re-interpret" the signal patterns based on whether the tag is on an arm or in a pocket.

## Mamba: Linear Complexity for Sequential Signals

At the heart of the model is the Mamba architecture, a specific type of State Space Model (SSM). While Transformers have become the standard for sequence modeling due to their self-attention mechanism, they suffer from quadratic computational complexity $O(N^2_{\max})$ relative to the sequence length. In a localization system with many anchors or high-frequency updates, this can become a bottleneck.

Mamba provides an alternative by using a "selective scan" mechanism. It processes sequences with linear complexity $O(N_{\max})$ while maintaining the ability to remember long-range dependencies. The researchers employed a bidirectional Mamba model, which processes the anchor sequence in both forward and backward directions. This ensures that the spatial relationships between anchors are fully captured regardless of their numerical ID or the order in which they are processed.

The core of the Mamba block relies on learning input-dependent parameters $\Delta, B, C$. These parameters allow the model to decide which parts of the CIR data to "remember" in its internal state and which to "ignore," effectively filtering out noise and focusing on the multipath components that most accurately signal the user's orientation.

## Post-Processing with Kalman Filters

Deep learning models, while powerful at pattern recognition, can sometimes produce jittery outputs or predictions that ignore physical reality (e.g., a person's head suddenly spinning $360^\circ$ in a fraction of a second). To ensure temporal consistency, the researchers integrated two variants of Kalman Filters (KFs):

1.  **Orientation-based KF:** This filter uses only the neural network's yaw predictions. It assumes a constant yaw-rate model and smooths the output over time, which is helpful when no position data is available.
2.  **Location-based KF:** This more advanced variant incorporates the user's movement. If the system knows the user's position, it can calculate their heading (the direction of travel). While a person's body orientation isn't always the same as their walking direction (e.g., walking sideways), they are often correlated. This filter fuses the Mamba-predicted orientation with the position-derived heading to refine the final estimate.

## Experimental Results and Performance

The system was evaluated in a lab environment using five UWB anchors and a high-precision motion capture (MoCap) system for ground truth. Three subjects performed various walking trajectories with the UWB tag placed on their chest, in their pocket, and on their arm.

The results showed that the Mamba-based approach significantly outperforms simpler rule-based methods. A rule-based baseline that only looks at signal strength achieved a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of $49.5^\circ$. In contrast, the raw Mamba model achieved an MAE of $38.6^\circ$ using only UWB data.

When post-processing was added, the accuracy improved further:
*   **Mamba + Orientation KF:** $33.4^\circ$ MAE.
*   **Mamba + Location KF:** $18.9^\circ$ MAE.

The study also compared Mamba to a Transformer-based model. While both achieved similar accuracy, the Mamba model was much more efficient. The bidirectional Mamba model used only 409 K parameters and occupied 1.6 MB of storage, compared to the Transformer's 1.14 M parameters and 4.4 MB. This makes Mamba a more suitable candidate for deployment on mobile devices or embedded hardware.

## Analysis of Tag Placement

The research found that the physical location of the tag significantly affects performance. When the tag was worn on the arm, the raw deep learning models performed best ($35.8^\circ$ MAE for Mamba). This is likely because the movement of the arm during walking creates more dynamic and distinguishable CIR patterns.

Interestingly, for the pocket placement, the location-based Kalman filter yielded the best overall results, reaching an MAE of $13.0^\circ$. This suggests that for tags placed in pockets, the correlation between walking direction and body orientation is particularly strong, allowing the KF to provide highly effective corrections.

## Practical Significance

This research demonstrates that human orientation can be "sensed" through the air using standard UWB signals. By moving the complexity from the hardware (additional sensors) to the software (efficient Mamba models), the study provides a path toward simpler, cheaper, and more durable wearable devices.

The ability to extract orientation from a single tag at a low update rate (around $2.2\text{ Hz}$) suggests that this method could be integrated into existing UWB infrastructure without requiring significant upgrades. Applications ranging from hands-free "follow-me" robots to intuitive museum exhibits that know exactly which painting a visitor is admiring could benefit from this sensor-lite approach. 

Ultimately, by leveraging the efficiency of State Space Models and the rich information hidden within multipath signal reflections, this work bridges the gap between simple positioning and full context-aware human tracking.
