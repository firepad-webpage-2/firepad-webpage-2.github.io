## Introduction to Bermudan Swaption Pricing

A Bermudan swaption is a financial derivative that provides the holder the right to enter into an interest rate swap on any one of a predetermined set of exercise dates. Unlike a European swaption, which has only a single exercise date at maturity, or an American swaption, which can be exercised at any time, the Bermudan variety offers discrete windows of opportunity. This structure is frequently used in interest rate markets to provide flexibility against shifting yield curves, but it introduces significant mathematical complexity.

The primary challenge in pricing these instruments lies in the "early exercise" feature. At each exercise date, the holder must decide whether to exercise the option immediately or wait for a potentially more favorable opportunity later. This decision depends not only on the current state of interest rates but also on the expected future value of the remaining exercise opportunities. Traditionally, this problem is solved using numerical methods such as Monte Carlo simulations with least-squares regressions (the Longstaff-Schwarz approach) or by solving partial differential equations (PDEs) on a grid. While accurate, these methods are often computationally intensive and can act as "black boxes," making it difficult for risk managers to understand the sensitivities (the "Greeks") of the option's price to market changes.

This research develops an analytic framework for pricing Bermudan swaptions that have a limited number of exercise dates—often referred to as "few exercise dates" or "Canary swaptions." By decomposing the complex Bermudan payoff into a series of simpler European swaptions and correction terms, the paper provides a transparent and efficient alternative to numerical grids. This approach is grounded in the Hull-White short-rate model, a standard framework in quantitative finance that describes the evolution of the instantaneous interest rate $r(u)$.

## The Core Framework: Hull-White and Price Decomposition

The methodology relies on the single-factor Hull-White model, where the dynamics of the short rate allow for analytic expressions of bond prices and European options. In this model, the value of the bank account numeraire at time $t$ is given by:

$$
B(t) = \exp\left\{\int_0^t r(u)du\right\}
$$

To simplify the pricing of swaps and swaptions, the paper utilizes $T$-forward measures. These measures are chosen such that the prices of assets denominated in units of a zero-coupon bond maturing at time $T$ are martingales.

The central concept of the paper is the decomposition of the Bermudan price. For a Bermudan swaption with two exercise dates, $T_0$ and $T_1$, the value $V_0$ at time zero can be expressed as an expectation of the maximum of two values at the first date $T_0$: the value of exercising the swap immediately or the value of continuing to the next exercise date. Mathematically, this is represented as:

$$
V_0 = \text{Swaption}_1(0) + B(0, T_0) E_{T_0}[\max\{\text{Swap}_2(T_0) - \text{Swaption}_1(T_0), 0\} | \mathcal{F}_0]
$$

In this expression, $\text{Swaption}_1(0)$ represents the price of a European swaption that matures at $T_0$, while the second term—a "spread option"—captures the additional value provided by the subsequent exercise date $T_1$. By identifying this spread option as a forward-starting receiver swaption, denoted as $\tilde{\text{Swaption}}_{[T_1,T_2]}(T_0)$, the problem is reduced to evaluating the integral of this payoff over the distribution of the interest rate at $T_0$.

## The Linear Exercise Boundary

A critical finding in the paper is the behavior of the optimal exercise boundary. In most Bermudan pricing problems, the boundary that separates the "exercise" region from the "hold" region is a complex, non-linear function of the underlying state variables. However, the author identifies that within the Hull-White model, particularly for swaps with relatively short tenors, this boundary exhibits near-linear characteristics.

The exercise boundary, denoted $x^*(y)$, defines the critical level of the state variable at which immediate exercise becomes optimal. The research demonstrates that this boundary can be approximated as:

$$
x^*(y) = d^- + by
$$

Here, $y$ represents a Gaussian state variable associated with the first exercise date, and $x$ is the variable for the second. The coefficients $d^\pm$ and $b$ are determined by the strikes and the variance distribution of the interest rate model. Crucially, the coefficient $b$ is found to be negative ($b < 0$), which acts as a "damping factor." This negative slope implies that as the uncertainty at the first date increases, the impact on the subsequent exercise boundary is mitigated. This linearity is the "secret sauce" that allows the integration of the option payoff to be performed analytically using specialized mathematical functions.

## Evaluating the Correction with TT-Functions

Once the exercise boundary is established as linear, the integral for the spread option can be evaluated in closed form. The author employs Owen's T-functions, referred to in this context as TT-functions, to solve these integrals. These functions are essentially integrals of the bivariate normal distribution and are well-suited for capturing the interaction between two correlated Gaussian events—in this case, the decision to exercise at $T_0$ versus $T_1$.

The analytic price for a two-exercise Bermudan swaption is thus composed of two European swaptions and two TT-function correction terms. This explicit formula reveals that the magnitude of the early exercise premium is highly sensitive to how variance is distributed over time. If the interest rate variance is "front-loaded" (meaning most of the uncertainty occurs early in the option's life), the correction term is smaller, and the Bermudan price is closer to the sum of its constituent European swaptions. Conversely, "back-loaded" variance increases the value of the later exercise dates, resulting in a larger correction term.

## Extension to Three Exercise Dates and Beyond

The research extends this methodology to three exercise dates ($T_{-1}, T_0, T_1$) through the process of backward induction. In this scenario, the price is viewed as the two-exercise Bermudan price plus an additional spread option term that accounts for the earliest exercise date $T_{-1}$:

$$
V(0) = \text{Swaption}_1(0) + \text{SpreadOpt}^{(2-1)}(0) + B(0, T_{-1})E_{T_{-1}}[\max\{\text{Swap}_3(T_{-1}) - \text{Swaption}_1(T_{-1}) - \text{SpreadOpt}^{(2-1)}(T_{-1}), 0\} | \mathcal{F}_0]
$$

This extension introduces more complexity, as the payoff now involves double integrals over Gaussian variables $y$ and $z$. However, the author observes a significant pattern: the exercise boundaries $x^*(y,z)$ and $Y^*(z)$ remain linear. Furthermore, the negative coefficients in these linear boundaries ($B_2 < 0$) cause the higher-order integrals to decay extremely rapidly. 

This observation leads to a practical approximation for the general "few exercise dates" case. Because the double and triple integrals represent increasingly marginal corrections to the total price, they can be neglected without sacrificing material accuracy. The resulting general pricing formula becomes a recursive sum of short-tenor swaptions and forward-starting receiver swaptions.

## The Recursive Approximation Strategy

For a general Bermudan swaption with $n$ exercise dates, the paper proposes that the price can be approximated by a structured sequence of additions and subtractions. At each potential exercise date $T_m$:
1. Add a short-dated European swaption with a strike that accounts for the exercise boundary.
2. Subtract the forward-starting receiver swaptions that would have commenced at that date if the option had not been exercised earlier.

This recursive logic provides a clear financial intuition for the Bermudan price: it is an upper bound formed by a string of European options, tightened by "penalties" or corrections that account for the mutual exclusivity of the exercise dates. If you exercise at $T_0$, you lose the right to exercise at $T_1$; the correction terms analytically quantify the value of this lost opportunity.

## Numerical Validation and Practical Impact

The paper validates these analytic formulas by comparing them to numerical PDE solutions. The results, summarized in multiple tables for different volatility and strike scenarios, show that the analytic method is highly accurate. For the two-exercise case, the solution is exact within the model's assumptions. For the three-exercise and general cases, the approximation involving the neglect of higher-order integrals remains robust, with errors typically being negligible for practical trading and risk management purposes.

The significance of this work lies in three areas:
*   **Speed:** Analytic formulas can be evaluated orders of magnitude faster than PDE grids or Monte Carlo simulations. This is vital for banks managing large portfolios of Bermudan-style products.
*   **Transparency:** By breaking the price down into constituent swaptions and TT-function terms, quants and traders can see exactly which exercise dates are driving the value of the instrument.
*   **Risk Management:** Having a closed-form expression makes it much easier to calculate "Greeks" (delta, gamma, vega). Instead of re-running a simulation with a bumped parameter, one can simply differentiate the analytic formula.

By focusing on the subset of Bermudan swaptions with a limited number of exercise dates, this research provides a tractable and mathematically elegant solution to a problem that is usually left to brute-force computation. The identification of linear exercise boundaries and the application of damping factors within the Hull-White model offer a new perspective on how optionality accumulates in interest rate derivatives.
