## Introduction to Quasar Redshift Distributions

Quasars are among the most energetic and distant objects in the known universe, powered by supermassive black holes at the centers of galaxies. These active galactic nuclei (AGN) emit vast amounts of radiation across the electromagnetic spectrum, characterized by prominent broad emission lines. However, a closer look at a quasar's spectrum reveals a more complex structure: alongside these broad emission features are numerous narrow absorption lines.

![quasar-spectrum](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10945v1/qsospectrum-sargent1988.png)
*{fig:quasar-spectrum}: A typical quasar spectrum showing the characteristic emission peaks and the dense forest of narrow absorption lines.*

In standard astrophysical practice, the redshift of the emission lines, denoted as $z_{em}$, is treated as the cosmological redshift. This value indicates the distance to the quasar and is assumed to reflect the expansion of the universe at the time the light was emitted. The absorption lines, labeled $z_{abs}$, are generally observed at lower redshifts than the emission lines. Traditionally, these features are explained by the "intervening medium" hypothesis, which posits that the light from a distant quasar passes through independent gas clouds or foreground galaxies located along the line of sight. Each cloud absorbs light at specific wavelengths, shifted according to its own cosmological distance, resulting in the observed absorption lines.

This research paper by Subramanian et al. investigates systematic relationships between $z_{em}$ and $z_{abs}$ that suggest this traditional two-component model might be incomplete. The study is motivated by prior observations that the lowest detected values of $z_{abs}$ appear to correlate with $z_{em}$. If the absorbers were truly independent and intervening, one would expect the minimum detectable absorption redshift to remain constant for all quasars, representing the nearest structures in the foreground. The authors aim to model these trends mathematically to explore the possibility that absorption lines have a local origin within the quasar's environment.

## The Intervening Medium vs. Local Origin Hypothesis

The fundamental question addressed in this work is the spatial origin of quasar absorption features. Under the intervening medium hypothesis, a quasar acts as a background light source illuminating the universe. The absorption lines provide a "core sample" of the intergalactic and circumgalactic media between the observer and the quasar. In this framework, the properties of the absorber (its redshift $z_{abs}$) should be statistically independent of the source's properties ($z_{em}$).

However, if a systematic trend exists such that the minimum $z_{abs}$ increases as $z_{em}$ increases, the independence of these features is brought into question. Such a correlation implies that the lowest-redshift absorption systems "know" about the emission redshift of the quasar. Kantharia (2016) previously identified that the distribution of $z_{abs}$ relative to $z_{em}$ follows a monotonic upward trend at its lower boundary.

The authors argue that this dependency suggests a physical connection. If the absorption systems are located within the quasar host galaxy or are part of large-scale outflows originating from the central engine, their observed redshifts would be a combination of the cosmological redshift and local kinematics (such as expansion or outflow velocity). This "local origin" hypothesis challenges the standard cosmological interpretation of quasar spectra and suggests that $z_{em}$ might not be the sole indicator of the quasar's true distance.

## Data Selection and Processing Methodology

To quantify these trends, the researchers analyzed large-scale spectroscopic datasets from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS). The primary focus was on the singly ionized magnesium (MgII) doublet, a common feature in quasar spectra.

The MgII dataset was sourced from the Raghunathan et al. (2016) catalogue, which includes 36,981 features from over 250,000 quasars in the SDSS 12th data release (DR12). These data points cover a redshift range of $0.35 < z_{MgII} < 2.3$. For higher-redshift comparisons, the authors utilized triply ionized carbon (CIV) data from Cooksey et al. (2013) and Monadi et al. (2023). The Cooksey catalogue provides CIV redshifts in the range $1.5 < z_{CIV} < 4.5$.

The redshift for any given line was calculated using the standard Doppler convention:
$$
z = \frac{\lambda_{obs} - \lambda_{rest}}{\lambda_{rest}}
$$
where $\lambda_{obs}$ is the wavelength measured by the telescope and $\lambda_{rest}$ is the wavelength of the transition measured in a laboratory at rest.

To identify the systematic boundaries of the redshift distribution, the authors employed a data-driven approach using the `ConvexHull` algorithm from the `scipy.spatial` library. This algorithm identifies the set of points that form the outermost perimeter (the "hull") of the data distribution in the $z_{em}$ vs $z_{abs}$ plane. By isolating the points belonging to the lower diagonal edge of this hull, the authors could objectively define the "lower envelope"—the minimum observed absorption redshift for any given emission redshift.

## Modeling the Redshift Envelopes

The core contribution of this paper is the derivation of specific linear models that describe the lower envelope of the redshift distributions for different atomic species.

### MgII Model
For the MgII absorption lines, the lower envelope was found to follow a highly linear trend. The authors performed a linear regression on the hull points, resulting in the model:
$$
z_{MgIImodel} = (0.418 \pm 0.008)z_{em} - (0.482 \pm 0.02)
$$
This fit achieved a coefficient of determination ($R^2$) of 0.99, indicating that the linear model captures nearly all the variance in the lower boundary. This result suggests that for any quasar, the observed MgII absorption lines are bounded such that $z_{MgIImodel} < z_{MgII} < z_{em}$.

![mgii-redshift-model](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10945v1/zMgII-Raghunathan2016-model.png)
*{fig:mgii-redshift-model}: Scatter plot of $z_{em}$ vs $z_{MgII}$ with the derived linear model (red line) representing the lower boundary of the distribution.*

### CIV Model
A similar analysis was conducted for the CIV absorption lines using the Cooksey et al. (2013) data. The resulting linear fit for the lower envelope was:
$$
z_{CIVmodel} = 0.845(\pm0.0002)z_{em} - 0.153(\pm0.0006)$
$$
Again, the fit showed a high $R^2$ of 0.99. Interestingly, the slope for the CIV model (0.845) is significantly steeper than that for MgII (0.418). This indicates that CIV absorption lines generally occur at redshifts much closer to $z_{em}$ than MgII lines do, suggesting a stratified distribution of different ions within the quasar's environment.

## Validation and Cross-Species Comparison

The researchers validated the MgII model by testing it against independent datasets of very high-redshift quasars. Data from Matejek and Simcoe (2012) and Chen et al. (2017) included quasars with $z_{em}$ values up to approximately 7.5. Despite being outside the range of the initial SDSS fitting data, these points consistently fell above the $z_{MgIImodel}$ line. For a quasar at $z_{em} = 7$, the model predicts a minimum absorption redshift of 2.446; for $z_{em} = 10$, it predicts 3.7.

The study also compared these trends across other species, including singly ionized iron (FeII) and triply ionized silicon (SiIV), using data from Sargent et al. (1988). The analysis revealed a consistent hierarchy: for a fixed $z_{em}$, MgII typically represents the lowest observed absorption redshift, followed by FeII in an intermediate region, and SiIV/CIV closest to the emission redshift.

![multi-species-comparison](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10945v1/zmgzfezsi-zem-Sargent1988.png)
*{fig:multi-species-comparison}: Comparison of different absorption species (MgII, FeII, SiIV) showing their relative positions within the $z_{em}$ vs $z_{abs}$ space.*

All observed absorption redshifts $z_{X}$ for a given species $X$ were found to satisfy the general condition:
$$
z_{Xmodel} < z_{X} < z_{em}
$$
where $z_{Xmodel}$ is the species-specific linear lower bound. The consistency of these bounds across different datasets and species strengthens the argument for a non-random, physical origin for these distributions.

## The Difference Redshift $\Delta z$

To further investigate the relationship between the emitter and the absorber, the authors calculated the "difference redshift" ($\Delta z$). This value represents the intrinsic shift between the quasar and the absorption feature in the quasar's own frame of reference. It is calculated using the relativistic formula for sequential redshifts:
$$
(1 + z_{em}) = (1 + \Delta z)(1 + z_{abs})
$$
Solving for $\Delta z$ gives:
$$
\Delta z = \frac{1+z_{em}}{1+z_{abs}} - 1
$$
When calculating $\Delta z$ between $z_{em}$ and the modeled minimum redshift $z_{MgIImodel}$, the authors found that the value remains remarkably consistent across a wide range of $z_{em}$. For example, at $z_{em} = 7$, the predicted $\Delta z \approx 1.32$, while at $z_{em} = 4$, $\Delta z \approx 1.28$. This near-constancy suggests that the physical mechanism producing the lowest-redshift absorption features maintains a similar relative velocity or spatial structure regardless of the total cosmological redshift of the quasar.

## Significance and Implications for Cosmology

The findings of this paper have significant implications for how astrophysicists interpret quasar data. By quantifying the linear relationship between $z_{em}$ and the lowest $z_{abs}$, the authors provide strong evidence that these absorption lines are not merely random foreground objects but are intrinsic to the quasar's environment.

### Re-evaluating Redshift Components
If absorption lines have a local origin, the interpretation of $z_{em}$ as the true cosmological distance indicator may need refinement. The authors suggest a two-component redshift model where the observed redshift is the sum of a constant cosmological component and a local, kinematics-based component. They propose that the lowest observed absorption redshift, such as the value predicted by the $z_{MgIImodel}$, might actually be a more accurate representation of the quasar's true cosmological redshift than the emission line redshift itself.

### Predictive Tools for Spectroscopy
The linear models derived in this study ($z_{MgIImodel}$ and $z_{CIVmodel}$) serve as predictive tools. For any newly discovered quasar, these equations can predict the range within which absorption lines are expected to appear. This helps observers distinguish between intrinsic lines and potential rare cases of genuine intervening matter.

### Understanding Quasar Structure
The distinct slopes and boundaries for different ions (MgII vs. CIV) offer clues about the physical conditions within quasars. The observation that MgII lines are consistently more shifted from $z_{em}$ than CIV lines suggests a stratified environment. This could indicate a scenario where different ions exist in different zones of an outflow, with MgII potentially representing faster-moving or more distant components of the local gas.

By moving from a descriptive observation of these trends to a quantitative mathematical framework, this research provides a basis for future studies to investigate the specific physical mechanisms—such as radiation-driven winds or host galaxy dynamics—that drive these systematic redshift distributions.
