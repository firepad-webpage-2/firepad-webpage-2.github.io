## Bridging Choice Modeling and Game Theory

In marketing science, understanding how consumers make choices between competing products is fundamental to strategic planning. Discrete choice analysis, particularly choice-based conjoint (CBC) experiments, has long served as the gold standard for eliciting these preferences. By observing which products individuals select from sets of alternatives, researchers can estimate the "part-worth utilities" of various product features, such as brand, price, or specific technical attributes.

However, a significant challenge arises when firms move from analyzing consumer preferences to making actual market decisions. In a realistic market, any change a firm makes to its product line—such as launching a new product or adjusting a price—is likely to trigger a reaction from competitors. Traditional product optimization often treats the competitive environment as static, ignoring these strategic responses. To address this, researchers integrate discrete choice models with non-cooperative game theory to simulate competitive dynamics and identify stable market outcomes, known as Nash equilibria.

The R package `cash` (conjoint + Nash) provides an integrated, end-to-end simulation pipeline to model these interactions. It bridges the gap between preference elicitation and game-theoretic equilibrium computation, offering a structured environment for researchers to simulate market scenarios where firms compete on both price and non-price product features.

## The Logic of Discrete Choice and Random Utility

The foundation of the `cash` package lies in random utility theory. This theory posits that a consumer $i$ derives a latent utility from a product alternative $k$ in a choice set $j$. This utility is composed of a deterministic component, which depends on product attributes and consumer preferences, and a stochastic error term representing unobserved factors. The utility is expressed as:

$$
U_{ijk} = \beta_i x_{jk}^T + \epsilon_{ijk}
$$

In this equation, $x_{jk}^T$ represents the vector of product attributes, $\beta_i$ is the vector of individual $i$'s preferences (part-worths), and $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is a random error term, typically assumed to follow a Gumbel distribution. Under the "first choice rule," the consumer is assumed to choose the product that maximizes their total utility:

$$
y_{ijk} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } U_{ijk} = \max_{k' \in \{1, \dots, K\}} U_{ijk'} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

When estimating these preferences from data, `cash` utilizes Hierarchical Bayesian mixed logit (HB-MXL) models. These models are particularly effective because they account for heterogeneity—the fact that different consumers have different tastes. Instead of estimating a single set of preferences for the entire population, the model assumes that individual $\beta_i$ values are drawn from a population-level distribution (usually multivariate normal) with mean $\bar{\beta}$ and covariance $\Sigma$. The probability of a consumer choosing alternative $j$ is then modeled via the conditional logit formula:

$$
P(y_{ij} = 1) = \frac{\exp(\beta_i x_j^T)}{\sum_{k=1}^K \exp(\beta_i x_k^T)}
$$

## Simulating Competitive Reactions

The primary innovation of the `cash` package is its ability to simulate how firms interact strategically. In the simulation, each firm $f$ aims to maximize its total contribution margin $\pi_f$, which is the sum of the profits from all products in its product line $\mathcal{K}_f$. The profit for a single product $k$ depends on its price $p_k$, its variable cost $c_k$, a base cost $\delta$, and the demand $D_k$ (market share) it captures:

$$
\pi_f = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}_f} (p_k - c_k - \delta) \cdot D_k(p, x)
$$

The simulation uses a "myopic best response" approach to find a Nash equilibrium. The process begins with an initial set of products for all firms. Firms then take turns optimizing their product lines, assuming the current configurations of their competitors remain fixed. A Nash equilibrium is reached when no firm can unilaterally change its product features or prices to increase its profit.

Because product attributes (like color, size, or material) are often discrete, the optimization problem is combinatorial and non-linear. To ensure global optimality and avoid the pitfalls of heuristic search methods, `cash` employs complete enumeration of all possible product configurations for the firms.

## The End-to-End Simulation Pipeline

The `cash` package organizes the complex process of competitive simulation into a series of modular steps, labeled alphabetically to guide the user through the pipeline.

### 1. Preference Generation and Choice Design
Before simulating a market, one must define the consumers. The function `B_prefgen` creates synthetic consumer preferences by sampling from multivariate normal distributions. This allows researchers to specify how much consumers care about different features and how much they vary in their opinions. Following this, `C_choicedes` generates statistically efficient choice designs. These designs are carefully constructed to ensure that the data collected (or simulated) will be informative enough to accurately recover the underlying preferences.

### 2. Error Control and Response Simulation
To simulate realistic consumer choices, random noise must be added to the deterministic utilities. `cash` introduces a novel metric called the Median Relative Gumbel Error (MRGE). This metric allows researchers to control the "signal-to-noise" ratio in the simulation. By setting a specific MRGE, the user ensures that the random errors ($\epsilon_{ijk}$) have a controlled magnitude relative to the part-worth utilities ($\beta_i$). The `D_responsesim` function then applies these errors and the first choice rule to generate simulated choice data.

### 3. Model Estimation and Validation
Once responses are generated, `E_hbmxlest` estimates the preferences using a hybrid Gibbs sampler. This Bayesian approach allows for the estimation of individual-level part-worths even with a relatively small number of choices per respondent. The package includes comprehensive evaluation tools in `G_modeleval` to check for model convergence and accuracy. It measures parameter recovery using metrics like the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and the average Pearson correlation ($\rho$) between true and estimated preferences:

$$
\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N \cdot O} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{o=1}^O (\hat{\beta}_{io} - \beta_{io})^2}
$$

$$
\rho = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \text{corr}(\hat{\beta}_i, \beta_i)
$$

### 4. Equilibrium Computation
The final stage involves the actual game-theoretic simulation. Due to the computational intensity of evaluating every possible market scenario, `cash` splits this into two steps. First, `H_precomputeM` calculates and stores the contribution margins for all possible competitive configurations. This step is parallelized and implemented in C++ to maximize efficiency. Finally, `I_preoptandnashgame` uses these pre-computed values to rapidly simulate the best-response sequences and identify Nash equilibria.

## Handling Complexity and Computational Efficiency

Simulating competitive games in markets with many product features and multiple firms leads to a "combinatorial explosion." For example, if there are multiple discrete features and price levels, the number of possible product configurations grows exponentially. If multiple firms each offer multiple products, the number of possible market states becomes massive.

`cash` addresses this through several technical strategies:
*   **Parallel Computing:** The pre-computation of market scenarios is distributed across multiple CPU cores.
*   **C++ Integration:** Performance-critical functions for demand calculation and utility summing are written in C++ and integrated via `Rcpp`.
*   **Efficient Look-ups:** By pre-calculating the payoffs and storing them in an optimized matrix (the $M$ matrix), the actual game simulation (finding the best response) becomes a simple look-up operation rather than a repeated optimization task.

## Significance for Marketing Strategy

The introduction of the `cash` package moves the field of marketing analytics closer to a more realistic representation of competitive markets. By providing an open-source, end-to-end framework, it allows both practitioners and researchers to:

1.  **Anticipate Competitor Reactions:** Instead of assuming competitors will do nothing, firms can predict how rivals will likely reposition their products or adjust prices in response to a new launch.
2.  **Evaluate Long-term Stability:** A product that seems profitable in a static analysis might lead to a price war or a crowded market segment once competitors react. `cash` helps identify which product configurations lead to stable, profitable Nash equilibria.
3.  **Conduct Methodological Research:** The package serves as a sandbox for researchers to test how different modeling assumptions (e.g., choice rules, error levels, or prior distributions in Bayesian models) affect the predicted market outcomes and the stability of identified equilibria.

By integrating preference elicitation, Bayesian econometrics, and non-cooperative game theory into a single computational tool, the authors provide a rigorous foundation for strategic product policy and competitive analysis.
