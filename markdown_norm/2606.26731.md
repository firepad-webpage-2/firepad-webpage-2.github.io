In the practice of financial engineering, the Black–Scholes–Merton framework provides a foundational approach to derivative pricing through the concept of dynamic replication. However, the theoretical assumption of frictionless markets—where assets can be traded continuously without cost—does not hold in reality. Market participants face bid–ask spreads, market impact, and varying levels of liquidity, all of which generate transaction costs. Hedging Valuation Adjustment (HVA) is a metric designed to quantify these expected costs.

A significant challenge in managing HVA is that transaction costs are not static; they are highly sensitive to market stress and liquidity demand. When market volatility increases, the demand for rebalancing hedges often spikes simultaneously with a widening of spreads, creating a correlated risk between hedging activity and transaction costs. This paper develops a robust framework for HVA that explicitly accounts for this "liquidity–demand stress." By employing entropy-based robust risk measurement, the author provides a method to evaluate different hedging policies under a consistent economic interpretation of stress.

## The Mechanics of Hedging Valuation Adjustment

At its core, HVA represents the value adjustment needed to cover the lifetime transaction costs of a hedging strategy. Consider a European call option with payoff $H(S_T) = (S_T - K)^+$. To hedge this option, a trader follows a delta-hedging strategy where the target position at time $t$ is the Black–Scholes delta, $\hat{\Delta}_t = \partial_S V(t, S_t)$.

In practice, trading occurs at discrete intervals $t_0, ..., t_n$. To manage costs, traders often use a "no-trade-band" rule. Under this rule, the hedge position $\Delta_{t_i}(b)$ is only updated if the difference between the target delta $\hat{\Delta}_{t_i}$ and the current position $\Delta_{t_{i-1}}(b)$ exceeds a specified bandwidth $b$. If it does not, the trader maintains the previous position. At maturity $t_n$, the entire position is liquidated such that $\Delta_{t_n}(b) = 0$.

The rebalancing trade at any step is given by $\Delta q_i(b) = \Delta_{t_i}(b) - \Delta_{t_{i-1}}(b)$, and the resulting trading volume is $X_i(b) = S_{t_i}|\Delta q_i(b)|$. The transaction costs typically consist of a linear component (the spread) and a convex component (market impact). When accounting for a pathwise illiquidity multiplier $m_{t_i}$, the discounted total loss $L(b)$ for a single path is:

$$L(b) = \sum_{i=1}^n e^{-rt_i} m_{t_i} (sX_i(b) + \kappa X_i(b)^2)$$

Where $s$ is the baseline half-spread and $\kappa$ is the quadratic impact coefficient. The baseline $HVA_0(b)$ is simply the expected value of this loss, $HVA_0(b) = HVA^{ind}(b) = E_{P^{ind}}[L(b)]$, under the assumption that liquidity fluctuations are independent of trading demand.

## Entropy-Based Robustness and its Limitations

The "robust" aspect of this research involves preparing for "worst-case" scenarios where trading demand and illiquidity are positively correlated. This is achieved using Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence, a measure from information theory that quantifies the difference between two probability distributions.

The robust HVA, $HVA_\varepsilon(b)$, is defined as the maximum expected loss over a set of alternative probability distributions $Q$ that are within a relative-entropy "distance" $\varepsilon$ from the baseline distribution:

$$HVA_\varepsilon(b) = \sup_{Q \in U_\varepsilon} E_Q[L(b)]$$

While this framework is mathematically rigorous, the paper identifies a practical problem when comparing different hedging policies. Every no-trade band $b$ produces a different empirical distribution of losses $L(b)$. If a risk manager applies a fixed radius $\varepsilon$ to all bands (the "fixed-radius convention"), they might inadvertently apply different levels of "economic stress" to different policies. A policy that naturally reduces turnover (like a very wide band) might appear robust simply because its loss distribution is less sensitive to the KL "tilt," rather than because it is a superior strategy. This creates an "apples-to-oranges" comparison when trying to select an optimal hedging rule.

## The Gaussian Benchmark-Stress Convention

To resolve this inconsistency, the paper introduces a "fixed benchmark-stress convention." This approach anchors the robust valuation to a tangible economic scenario: a specific level of rank correlation between trading volume and market illiquidity.

The author uses a Gaussian copula benchmark to define this stress. By re-ranking simulated paths so that high-turnover paths coincide with high-illiquidity paths, the author creates a benchmark increment $\Delta_{G_b}(\rho_G)$. The parameter $\rho_G$ acts as an economic stress label. For example, $\rho_G = 0$ implies no correlation (baseline), while $\rho_G = 0.4$ represents a moderately stressed environment where high demand and poor liquidity are likely to occur together.

Instead of fixing $\varepsilon$, the risk manager fixes $\rho_G = \rho_{G_0}$ and then calculates the required KL radius $\varepsilon_{req}(b; \rho_{G_0})$ that matches the benchmark's cost increment. This ensures that every hedging policy is evaluated under an equivalent degree of demand–liquidity stress.

## Empirical Results: Policy Comparison across Liquidity States

The paper evaluates these concepts through Monte Carlo simulations involving a Merton jump-diffusion model. The analysis covers three distinct liquidity environments: High, Medium, and Low liquidity.

In high-liquidity environments, the choice of convention (fixed-radius vs. fixed benchmark-stress) matters less because transaction costs are small. The optimal no-trade band $b$ is typically narrow (e.g., $b = 0.02$), prioritizing tight tracking of the delta.

However, in low-liquidity environments, the costs of rebalancing are so high that they dominate the risk profile. The framework shows that as liquidity deteriorates:
1.  **Optimal bands widen:** In the low-liquidity scenario, the optimal band increases significantly to $b = 0.30$.
2.  **Convention divergence increases:** The fixed-radius convention significantly underestimates the required "tilt" for wide bands. To maintain a consistent stress level of $\rho_{G_0}=0.4$, the required radius $\varepsilon_{req}$ must increase by a factor of three as the band widens.

The "optimal" policy is found by minimizing a joint objective function $J_v(b) = HVA_v(b) + \lambda TE(b)$, which balances the robust transaction costs against the tracking error $TE(b)$. The tracking error is measured as the 95% CVaR of the replication shortfall.

## State-Dependent Hedging and Gamma Sensitivity

The research further explores whether adjusting the no-trade band dynamically—based on the option's gamma—can improve outcomes. Gamma measures the sensitivity of the delta to changes in the underlying asset price. High-gamma regions are where the delta changes most rapidly, necessitating more frequent rebalancing.

The author tests "gamma-tightened" rules (where $b$ is smaller when gamma is high) and "gamma-widened" rules. The data shows that high-gamma observations (the top 10% of gamma values) account for a disproportionate share of total turnover, often exceeding 20-30% of the total trading volume.

While gamma-tightened rules reduce the tracking error, they significantly increase the HVA because they force trades exactly when the delta is most volatile. The robust framework allows for a clear visualization of this trade-off, helping traders decide whether the reduction in tracking error justifies the "stressed" cost of the additional trades.

## Conclusion

This work provides a systematic bridge between statistical robust risk measures and economic market stress. By introducing the fixed benchmark-stress convention, the author provides a solution to the "policy comparison problem" inherent in entropy-based valuation. 

The findings emphasize that in illiquid markets, the most "robust" strategy is often to accept larger tracking errors in exchange for significantly reduced trading turnover. This research provides the quantitative tools necessary for derivative desks to set appropriate reserves (via HVA) and to calibrate their hedging parameters in a way that is resilient to joint shocks in trading demand and market liquidity. The methodology is extensible to other valuation adjustments, such as XVA, and offers a robust path forward for risk management in complex, non-frictionless markets.
