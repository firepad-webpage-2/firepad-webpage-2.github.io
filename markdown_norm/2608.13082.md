## Introduction to Synthetic Market Data Evaluation

The ability to simulate realistic financial markets is a cornerstone of modern quantitative finance. Synthetic market data allows researchers and practitioners to backtest trading strategies, measure risk, and study market microstructure without the high costs or privacy constraints associated with historical datasets. At the heart of these simulations is the Limit Order Book (LOB), a record of all outstanding buy and sell orders for a specific financial instrument. Because the LOB evolves through a continuous stream of messages, synthetic models must do more than just predict price movements; they must generate a realistic flow of orders that preserves the complex dependencies of real-world markets.

While generative models for LOB data have become increasingly sophisticated—utilizing everything from Hawkes processes to deep diffusion models—the tools used to evaluate them have lagged behind. Traditional evaluation often relies on "stylized facts," such as volatility clustering or heavy-tailed returns, and specific market statistics like bid-ask spreads or order imbalances. However, these metrics are often "marginal," meaning they look at individual properties in isolation. They frequently fail to capture the joint structure of the LOB: how the liquidity at the best price relates to deeper levels, how the bid and ask sides interact, and how these relationships evolve over time.

This paper introduces **LOB-ID**, an embedding-based evaluation framework designed to address these limitations. By adapting metrics from the field of computer vision—specifically the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) and Monge Inception Distance (MIND)—to the financial domain, the authors provide a method for assessing the structural realism of synthetic LOB trajectories. LOB-ID uses a pre-trained deep learning model to "see" the LOB, capturing the intricate temporal and cross-level dependencies that traditional statistics might miss.

## The Limit Order Book and the Challenge of Realism

A Limit Order Book is a multi-dimensional data structure. At any given moment, it contains the prices and volumes of the top $n$ levels of buy orders (bids) and sell orders (asks). As new orders arrive, are cancelled, or are executed, the state of the book changes. Evaluating a synthetic LOB model is essentially a task of measuring **distributional similarity**. We do not expect a generative model to perfectly recreate a specific historical day; rather, we want it to produce "plausible alternatives" that look and behave like real market data.

Capturing "realism" is difficult because the LOB contains deep structural dependencies:
1.  **Temporal Dependence**: A book state at time $t$ is highly dependent on the state at $t-1$.
2.  **Cross-Level Dependence**: The volume at the best bid price is related to the volume at the second-best bid price and so on.
3.  **Bid-Ask Interaction**: Changes on the buy side often signal or trigger changes on the sell side.

Existing benchmarks like **LOB-Bench** use a collection of market-microstructure statistics to compare real and synthetic data. While helpful, these methods can be "blind" to certain types of errors. For example, a model might correctly generate the average spread and average volume at each level, but fail to capture the way those volumes move together over time. If the joint structure is broken, the synthetic data may lead to misleading results when used for testing trading algorithms.

## The LOB-ID Framework

The LOB-ID framework moves away from hand-designed statistics toward learned representations. The core idea is to pass LOB data through a "feature extractor"—a neural network that has been trained to understand LOB dynamics—and then compare the distributions of the resulting features (embeddings).

### The Inception Network: DeepLOB
The authors use the **DeepLOB** architecture as their feature extractor. DeepLOB is a deep convolutional neural network (CNN) combined with Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) units. It is designed to take a window of $T=100$ successive LOB states as input and predict short-term price movements. Each state includes the price and size for the ten best bid and ask levels, resulting in an input $x \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times 40}$.

To create the LOB-ID embedding, the authors remove the final classification layers of DeepLOB. They take the output of the "Inception" module—a part of the network that looks at features at different scales—and average it over time. This results in a 96-dimensional feature vector $\psi_w(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{96}$ that represents the essential characteristics of the LOB window.

### Measuring Distance: FID vs. MIND
Once we have these embeddings for both real and synthetic data, we need a way to measure the distance between their distributions. The paper explores two metrics:

**1. Fréchet Inception Distance (FID):** This is the standard metric in image generation. It assumes that the embeddings follow a Gaussian (normal) distribution. It calculates the distance based on the means ($\mu, \tilde{\mu}$) and covariance matrices ($\Sigma, \tilde{\Sigma}$) of the two sets:

$$d^2((\mu, \Sigma), (\tilde{\mu}, \tilde{\Sigma})) = \|\mu - \tilde{\mu}\|_2^2 + \text{Tr}(\Sigma + \tilde{\Sigma} - 2(\Sigma\tilde{\Sigma})^{1/2})$$

While popular, FID is limited because it only considers the first two moments (mean and variance). It might miss differences in the "shape" of the distributions that aren't captured by a Gaussian approximation.

**2. Monge Inception Distance (MIND):** To overcome the limitations of FID, the authors propose using MIND. Instead of assuming a Gaussian shape, MIND uses a "random projection" approach. It projects the high-dimensional embeddings onto many 1D lines and calculates the 2-Wasserstein distance (a common measure of distance between probability distributions) on those lines. This allows MIND to capture higher-order differences in the data distribution. For LOB-ID, the authors use $\alpha = 3d = 288$ as a scaling factor where $d = 96$ is the embedding dimension.

## Stability, Sensitivity, and Adversarial Resilience

For an evaluation metric to be useful, it must be stable, sensitive to changes, and difficult to "fool." The authors conducted several experiments to test these properties.

### Stability and Sensitivity
The LOB-ID metric (specifically using MIND) proved to be consistent across different trading days for the same stock but showed distinct differences between different stocks (see {fig:mind-heatmap}). This confirms that the metric captures instrument-specific market characteristics. 

Furthermore, when the authors intentionally corrupted real market data with controlled perturbations—such as adding a random walk to prices or jumping the price at a specific point—MIND responded monotonically. As the perturbation strength $\epsilon$ increased, the MIND score also increased, showing that the metric is sensitive to distortions in the underlying data.

### Resilience to Adversarial Attacks
A key contribution of the paper is demonstrating that LOB-ID can detect issues that other metrics miss. 
*   **Moment-Matching Attack**: The authors created a synthetic dataset designed to match the mean and covariance of real data embeddings. While this "fooled" the FID metric ($R_{FID} = 3.5$), MIND remained highly sensitive ($R_{MIND} = 74.5$), detecting the distributional differences that FID ignored.
*   **Joint-Structure Attack**: The authors shuffled the data in the deep levels of the LOB (levels 2-10) to break the temporal and cross-level dependencies while keeping the top level (level 1) and all individual statistics the same. Standard LOB-Bench metrics failed to detect this, essentially rating the corrupted data as "realistic." In contrast, LOB-ID consistently flagged the data as anomalous, showing a $75\% \pm 17\%$ increase over the baseline noise floor.

## Evaluating Generative Models

The authors applied LOB-ID to five different types of synthetic data generators, ranking them by their realism.

1.  **Zero-Intelligence (ZI)**: A basic stochastic model that samples every event (order type, price, volume) independently. It has no memory and no connection between variables.
2.  **Compound Hawkes Process (CH)**: A model that captures some temporal dependence (the "self-exciting" nature of trades) but still samples volumes and prices from fixed distributions.
3.  **LOBGAN**: A deep learning model (Generative Adversarial Network) that tries to learn the LOB structure by competing against a discriminator.
4.  **LOBS5**: A sequence model that treats LOB messages like text tokens, predicting the next message based on the previous ones.
5.  **DiffLOB**: A diffusion model that jointly generates future trajectories of prices and volumes. This was tested in an "unconditional" (null) mode and an "oracle" mode (where it was given future market regimes).

The results showed that LOB-ID (MIND) produced a clear and intuitive ranking:
**DiffLOB > LOBS5 > LOBGAN > Hawkes > ZI**

This ranking matches the theoretical complexity of the models. The models that were specifically designed to capture joint temporal and cross-level structures (like DiffLOB and LOBS5) achieved much better (lower) MIND scores than the stochastic baselines. Interestingly, while some models performed well on specific stylized facts or $L_1$ distances for individual statistics, LOB-ID provided a more holistic view of their overall structural fidelity.

## Significance of the Work

LOB-ID represents a move toward more rigorous validation of synthetic financial data. By leveraging deep learning to extract meaningful features from the Limit Order Book, the framework can detect subtle failures in joint structure that traditional statistical benchmarks miss. 

The primary advantages of LOB-ID are:
*   **Comprehensive Assessment**: It captures the relationships across time and price levels simultaneously.
*   **Robustness**: It is harder to "game" or fool with simple moment-matching or marginal statistic matching.
*   **Ease of Use**: Once the DeepLOB network is trained, it provides a consistent distance metric that can be used to compare any two LOB datasets without needing to retrain a discriminator.

For researchers in quantitative finance, LOB-ID offers a reliable tool to ensure that synthetic data is fit for purpose. Whether training a trade execution agent or testing a risk model, having a metric that truly reflects the "realism" of the simulated environment is essential for translating laboratory results into real-market success. The paper suggests that future work could extend this framework to higher-resolution message-level data and a broader variety of global equity markets.
