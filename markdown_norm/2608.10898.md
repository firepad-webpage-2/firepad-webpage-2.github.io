## Evolution of 6G: Integrating Sensing and Semantic Communication

The transition toward sixth-generation ($6\text{G}$) wireless systems marks a shift from purely communicative networks to multi-functional platforms that integrate sensing and high-level data interpretation. Two pillars of this evolution are Integrated Sensing and Communication (ISAC) and Semantic Communication (SemCom). ISAC enables hardware and spectrum sharing between radar-like sensing and data transmission, which is vital for applications like autonomous driving and smart factories. Meanwhile, SemCom focuses on transmitting the "meaning" or task-relevant features of data rather than raw bits, significantly reducing bandwidth requirements.

Traditional ISAC research has focused heavily on monostatic configurations where the transmitter and receiver are co-located. However, these setups suffer from significant self-interference. Bi-static ISAC, where the sensing receiver is physically separated from the transmitter, provides a solution to this interference but introduces a new challenge: time synchronization (TS) errors. In practical distributed networks, achieving perfect synchronization between two separate units is difficult. Furthermore, as we integrate SemCom into these systems, we face a complex resource allocation problem. Sensing accuracy and semantic data quality compete for the same limited power and bandwidth.

To address these challenges, researchers have proposed a Sensing-Aware Adaptive Source-Channel Coding (SA-ASCC) framework. This approach moves beyond simple data compression by jointly optimizing the way data is encoded and how wireless beams are formed, all while explicitly accounting for the performance degradation caused by imperfect time synchronization.

## The Framework for Semantic Communication

In the semantic communication component of this system, the goal is to transmit images while minimizing end-to-end (E2E) distortion. Unlike traditional systems that treat every bit with equal importance, this framework uses Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) to extract essential features.

The process begins with a source encoder, represented by a feature extraction function $f_\phi$. Given an input image, the encoder produces a $K$-dimensional feature vector $z$. This vector is then quantized into $\hat{z}$ and converted into a semantic bitstream $b$ using lossless arithmetic coding. The efficiency of this compression is defined by the source coding rate $R_s$. To allow the system to adapt to different channel conditions, the framework utilizes a set of $G$ pre-trained DNN models, each corresponding to a specific discrete source rate $R_s = \{R_{s,1}, \dots, R_{s,G}\}$.

Once the semantic bits are generated, they undergo channel encoding at a rate $R_c$ to protect against noise and interference. These bits are then modulated into SemCom symbols $s_c$ and transmitted. At the communication receiver (CRE), the received signal $y_c(t)$ is processed to recover the image. The quality of this recovery depends on the signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio (SINR), denoted by $\gamma$.

![semantic-distortion-curves](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10898v1/x2.png)
*{fig:semantic-distortion-curves}: End-to-end semantic distortion $D_o$ as a function of bit error rate $\rho_b$ for different source rates $R_s$. The solid lines represent the regression-based fitting used in the optimization.*

A critical contribution of this work is the mathematical modeling of semantic distortion $D_o$. Because the relationship between the bit error rate (BER) $\rho_b$ and the final image quality in a DNN-based system is highly non-linear, the authors employ a regression-based fitting method. The E2E distortion is approximated using a logistic function:

$$\log_{10} D_o(R_s, \rho_b) = \frac{A_1(R_s) - A_2(R_s)}{1 + ( \log_{10} \rho_b / x_0(R_s) )^p} + A_2(R_s)$$

Here, $A_1$, $A_2$, $x_0$, and $p$ are parameters determined by the properties of the semantic encoder-decoder pair and the specific source rate $R_s$. This model allows the system to predict how changes in the channel rate or transmit power will affect the final perceived quality of the received data.

## Bistatic Sensing with Imperfect Synchronization

Parallel to the semantic task, the system performs sensing to estimate the position of a target. The transmitter sends out sensing probing symbols $s_0$, which are superimposed with the SemCom symbols $s_c$. The sensing receiver (SRE) listens for the echo reflected from the target.

In a bi-static setup, the estimation of the target position $p_o$ is sensitive to the transmission delay $\tau$. In an ideal world, the SRE knows exactly when the TR sent the signal. In reality, there is a time synchronization error $\Delta\tau_{t,r}$, which is modeled as a random variable following a Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_T^2)$. This error acts as a "nuisance parameter" that can skew the position estimation.

To quantify sensing performance in this uncertain environment, the research utilizes the Hybrid Cramér-Rao Bound (HCRB). The HCRB provides a lower bound on the variance of any unbiased estimator of the target position. It is calculated by constructing a hybrid Fisher Information Matrix (FIM), $\mathbf{J}(\chi)$, which combines the information derived from the observed data and the prior knowledge of the synchronization error distribution:

$$\mathbf{J}(\chi) = \mathbf{J}_D(\chi) + \mathbf{J}_B(\chi)$$

where $\mathbf{J}_D(\chi)$ is the observed FIM and $\mathbf{J}_B(\chi)$ is the prior FIM. The resulting bound for the target position is:

$$\text{HCRB}(p_o) = [\mathbf{J}^{-1}(\chi)]_{1:2, 1:2}$$

This mathematical framework ensures that the system design is robust against the practical timing jitters found in distributed wireless deployments.

## Sensing-Aware Adaptive Source-Channel Coding

The core innovation of the paper is the joint optimization of both tasks under the SA-ASCC framework. The objective is to minimize the semantic distortion $D_o$ while satisfying several constraints:

1.  **Sensing Accuracy:** The trace of the position HCRB must not exceed a predefined threshold $\Pi$, ensuring $\text{Tr}(\text{HCRB}(p_o)) \leq \Pi$.
2.  **Resource Constraints:** The total channel uses must be within the available bandwidth, expressed as $R_s/R_c \leq E_{\max}^a$.
3.  **Communication Reliability:** The channel rate $R_c$ must not exceed the channel capacity $C(\gamma)$.
4.  **Power Budget:** The total transmit power across all antennas, $\text{Tr}(\Sigma)$, must stay below $P_T$.

This creates a complex "mixed-integer" problem. The source rate $R_s$ is a discrete choice from a set of pre-trained models, while the channel rate $R_c$ and the beamforming vectors $w_c, w_0$ are continuous variables. Because these variables are coupled—for example, increasing power to the sensing beam improves sensing accuracy but leaves less power for the semantic beam—the problem is non-convex and difficult to solve directly.

## Solving the Non-Convex Problem: Alternating Optimization

To tackle the optimization, the authors developed an Alternating Optimization (AO) algorithm. This approach breaks the complex problem into smaller, solvable pieces that are updated iteratively until the system converges.

*   **Model Selection:** First, the algorithm fixes the beamforming and channel rate to find the best discrete source coding rate $R_s$ from the available DNN models.
*   **Rate Optimization:** With $R_s$ fixed, the algorithm optimizes the channel rate $R_c$. Since the distortion function and BER approximations are non-convex, the authors use Successive Convex Approximation (SCA). This technique approximates non-convex functions with simpler convex versions at each step.
*   **Beamforming Optimization:** Finally, the algorithm optimizes the beamforming vectors $w_0$ and $w_c$. This sub-problem is particularly challenging because of the HCRB constraint. The authors use semi-definite relaxation (SDR) and introduce an auxiliary matrix $\Omega$ to transform the sensing constraints into a manageable form. A penalty method is then used to handle the remaining non-convex terms.

This iterative process ensures that the transmitter constantly balances the needs of the sensing task and the semantic communication task, adapting its strategy based on the current noise levels and channel quality.

## Performance Analysis and Trade-offs

The effectiveness of the SA-ASCC scheme is demonstrated through comprehensive simulations. The researchers used the CUB-200-2011 dataset (images of birds) to evaluate semantic performance using the Multi-Scale Structural Similarity (MS-SSIM) index.

A key finding is the characterization of the achievable performance region between MS-SSIM and HCRB. As the sensing requirement becomes less stringent (larger HCRB threshold), the system can allocate more power to semantic transmission, leading to higher MS-SSIM. However, the presence of time synchronization errors significantly shrinks this region. For example, a TS error of $100 \text{ ns}$ requires much more transmit power to achieve the same sensing accuracy as a system with only $1 \text{ ns}$ of error.

The SA-ASCC scheme also solves the "cliff effect" common in traditional systems. In conventional separation-based coding, the system quality drops abruptly when channel conditions fall below a certain threshold. By adaptively choosing $R_s$ and $R_c$, SA-ASCC maintains a smoother degradation, performing significantly better in low-power or high-noise scenarios.

![power-allocation-ratios](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10898v1/x8.png)
*{fig:power-allocation-ratios}: Adaptive power allocation between SemCom and sensing as a function of the HCRB threshold. SA-ASCC dynamically shifts resources, whereas benchmark schemes maintain fixed ratios.*

As shown in {fig:power-allocation-ratios}, the proposed scheme is uniquely capable of reallocating power dynamically. When the sensing accuracy requirement is high (a low HCRB threshold), the system prioritizes the sensing beam. As the requirement relaxes, it seamlessly shifts power back to the semantic beam to maximize communication quality. This adaptability is missing in standard benchmarks like Deep Joint Source-Channel Coding (DJSCC) or traditional BPG compression, which rely on static power allocation strategies.

## Implications for Distributed 6G Networks

The significance of this work lies in its practical approach to the imperfections of real-world $6\text{G}$ hardware. By integrating semantic efficiency with robust sensing derivations that account for timing errors, the framework provides a template for future distributed intelligent systems.

The research demonstrates that semantic communication is not just a way to save bandwidth, but a tool that provides the flexibility needed to support secondary tasks like sensing. The ability to maintain high image reconstruction quality while meeting strict target-positioning requirements, even with imperfect synchronization, suggests that bi-static ISAC can be effectively deployed in large-scale autonomous networks. Future research may build on this by exploring multi-target environments or co-designing the DNN models specifically to be robust against sensing-induced interference.
