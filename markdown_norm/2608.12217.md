## High-Energy Neutrinos in Dense Environments

The quest for the origins of high-energy astrophysical neutrinos is a central pillar of multi-messenger astronomy. While relativistic jets associated with gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) have long been considered primary candidates, finding a definitive spatial and temporal correlation between neutrinos and the prompt electromagnetic emission of ordinary GRBs has remained elusive. A growing body of research suggests that a significant portion of the cosmic neutrino flux might originate from "hidden" sources—astrophysical transients where the jet is contained or "choked" within a dense surrounding medium, preventing the escape of gamma rays while allowing neutrinos to pass through relatively unimpeded.

One such environment is the accretion disk surrounding a supermassive black hole (SMBH) in an active galactic nucleus (AGN). These disks are not merely swirling gas; they are nurseries for stars and compact objects. Interactions, stellar collapses, or mergers within these dense environments can launch relativistic jets. However, unlike standard GRBs that propagate through the relatively thin envelope of a star or the vacuum of space, these embedded jets must navigate the high-density material of the AGN disk. This paper presents a dynamic, time-dependent framework to model the propagation of these jets and the resulting neutrino signatures, moving beyond previous simplified models to provide a more accurate picture of their contribution to the high-energy neutrino sky.

## The Mechanics of Embedded Jet Propagation

A jet launched within an AGN disk faces significant resistance. The density of the disk material determines whether a jet will successfully "break out" of the disk surface or become "choked" and stall. The researchers characterize these jets using several parameters: the isotropic-equivalent kinetic energy $E_{iso}$, the duration of the central engine $t_{dur}$, the initial Lorentz factor $\Gamma_j = 300$, and a half-opening angle $\theta_0 = 5^\circ$.

The propagation is governed by the jet-head velocity $\beta_h$, which depends on the balance between the jet's power and the ram pressure of the ambient disk gas. This is expressed through a dimensionless luminosity parameter $\tilde{L}$:

$$\tilde{L} = \frac{L_j}{\Sigma_h \rho_d c^3}$$

where $L_j$ is the jet luminosity, $\Sigma_h$ is the cross-sectional area of the jet head, and $\rho_d$ is the local disk density. The resulting velocity is:

$$\beta_h = \frac{\tilde{L}^{1/2}}{1 + \tilde{L}^{1/2}}$$

The outcome of the jet—whether it breaks out or chokes—is defined by the engine-clock ratio $R_{dur} \equiv t_{req}/t_{dur}$, where $t_{req}$ is the time required for the jet to reach the disk surface. If $R_{dur} \leq 1$, the jet breaks out; if $R_{dur} > 1$, it is choked. This study distinguishes itself by tracking the full evolution of these parameters as the jet traverses different layers of the disk, rather than assuming a single average density.

## Moving Beyond the Single-State Approximation

A common simplification in previous studies is the "single-state approximation." For choked jets, this usually involves calculating neutrino emission based on the physical conditions at the point where the jet stalls. For breakout jets, it might involve conditions just before the jet exits the disk. This paper demonstrates that such approximations, while useful for rough estimates, can be inaccurate because they ignore the time-integrated history of the jet.

The researchers tested their dynamic framework against these approximations using two different AGN disk models: the Sirko & Goodman (SG) model and the Thompson, Quataert, & Murray (TQM) model. The SG model typically features steeper density gradients compared to the TQM model. In the case of breakout jets in SG disks, the rapid acceleration of the jet head as it reaches lower-density regions significantly reduces the power processed by the reverse shock. The researchers found that single-state approximations could overpredict the total neutrino yield by a factor of 2.6 in these scenarios. Conversely, for choked jets, the stalling-state approximation was found to be relatively accurate (within 14%), as the majority of the emission occurs near the stalling radius where the dissipation power peaks.

## Microphysics of Neutrino Production

The production of high-energy neutrinos occurs at the reverse shock within the jet head, but only when the shock is "collisionless." In the very dense inner regions of the disk, the shock is radiation-mediated, which suppresses the acceleration of high-energy particles. As the jet moves to thinner regions, the Thomson optical depth drops, and the shock becomes collisionless, allowing for first-order Fermi acceleration of protons.

These accelerated protons, which follow a power-law distribution $dn'_p/d\epsilon'_p \propto \epsilon'^{-s}_p$ with $s=2$, interact with both the ambient gas ($pp$ interactions) and the radiation field ($p\gamma$ interactions). These interactions produce secondary mesons (pions and kaons) and muons, which then decay into neutrinos. The maximum energy a proton can reach, $\epsilon'_{p,max}$, is determined by a balance between the acceleration rate and various energy-loss processes.

At the highest energies, $p\gamma$ interactions are the primary limit on proton energy. Interestingly, the model identifies a "spectral depression" in the neutrino fluence caused by Bethe–Heitler pair production ($p + \gamma \to p + e^+ + e^-$), which bleeds energy from protons without producing neutrinos, creating a characteristic dip between the low-energy $pp$ component and the high-energy $p\gamma$ component.

## Spectral Characteristics and Ultra-High-Energy Tails

One of the most significant findings of this dynamic modeling is the prediction of ultra-high-energy (UHE) neutrino tails. In certain environments—specifically the outer, lower-density regions of TQM-style disks—the secondary particles (pions and muons) experience significantly less cooling before they decay. This allows the neutrino spectrum to extend to much higher energies, reaching the 10–100 PeV range.

While these high-energy tails are theoretically impressive, they present a unique challenge for detection. Current observatories like IceCube are most efficient in the TeV to low-PeV range. When the spectrum hardens significantly, a large portion of the neutrino flux shifts into the UHE regime, which may actually result in a lower event count for existing detectors, even if the total energy released is high. For example, a deeply choked jet in a dilute environment (case TQM-3) was found to have a predicted detector yield $N_{det} \approx 0.72$, whereas a more moderately choked jet (TQM-2) yielded $N_{det} \approx 1.38$, despite TQM-3 having higher-energy individual neutrinos.

## Implications for Multi-Messenger Astronomy

This time-dependent framework establishes that the neutrino signature of an embedded jet is highly sensitive to the specific density profile of the AGN disk and the exact timing of the jet's propagation. The researchers conclude that for a single source located at a benchmark distance of 100 Mpc, the expected number of muon-track events in IceCube ranges from 0.1 to 1.4.

The study has several key implications:
1.  **Detector Guidance**: The prediction of 10–100 PeV neutrinos from outer-disk jets provides a strong scientific motivation for next-generation UHE neutrino telescopes like IceCube-Gen2 or GRAND.
2.  **Model Selection**: The differences in neutrino light curves and spectra between the SG and TQM disk models suggest that neutrino observations could eventually be used to probe the structure of AGN disks themselves.
3.  **Accuracy in Population Studies**: By showing where single-state approximations fail (particularly in breakout scenarios), this work provides a more reliable foundation for calculating the cumulative diffuse neutrino background from all such "hidden" jets in the universe.

Ultimately, by accounting for the complex, evolving environment within an AGN disk, this research brings us closer to identifying the hidden engines that drive the most energetic particles in our universe. While these jets may remain invisible to traditional telescopes, their neutrino "ghosts" carry vital information about the physics of black hole accretion and relativistic outflows.
