## Bridging the Gap in Neural Video Compression

Neural video compression has reached a point where its coding efficiency often exceeds established traditional standards like H.265 (HEVC). Modern learned codecs frequently demonstrate bitrate savings of 60% to 70% over traditional benchmarks. However, these advancements have primarily remained within the realm of academic research, evaluated on high-performance datacenter GPUs that do not reflect the hardware constraints of the average consumer. For a video codec to be useful in real-world applications—such as video conferencing on a laptop or smartphone—it must overcome two significant hurdles: computational efficiency on low-power hardware and cross-platform compatibility.

In cross-platform scenarios, existing neural codecs like DCVC-RT suffer from catastrophic decoding failures, whereas MLVC maintains visual consistency.*

Most existing neural codecs are non-deterministic across different hardware. A stream encoded on an Apple device might fail to decode on an Intel or Qualcomm processor due to minute differences in floating-point arithmetic or compiler-specific rounding. These discrepancies, while small, lead to "catastrophic failures" in entropy decoding where the entire video stream becomes unreadable noise. MLVC (Multi-platform Learned Video Codec) addresses these issues by introducing a deterministic scale-sharing mechanism and a hardware-compatible architecture designed to run in real-time on commodity Neural Processing Units (NPUs).

## The Barrier of Numerical Divergence

The primary reason neural codecs fail when moving between different hardware platforms is numerical divergence. In a typical neural codec, the encoder and decoder must both calculate the exact same parameters for the entropy model to interpret the compressed bitstream. These parameters, often representing the scale $\sigma_t$ and mean $\mu_t$ of a probability distribution, are generated by neural network layers.

In an environment using floating-point 16 (FP16) precision—which is standard for power-efficient NPUs—different hardware vendors implement rounding and mathematical approximations differently. A difference as small as $10^{-7}$ in the calculated scale can cause the entropy decoder to select the wrong index from a lookup table. Because the decoding process is sequential and relies on previous states, this tiny error propagates and magnifies, leading to a complete collapse of the reconstructed image within a few frames.

Previous research attempted to solve this using network quantization or by transmitting "calibration" data to nudge the decoder back into alignment. However, quantization often struggles with platform-specific compiler behaviors, and calibration offers only probabilistic consistency rather than the absolute guarantee required for commercial deployment. MLVC shifts the paradigm by changing how these parameters are communicated.

## The Scale-Sending Mechanism for Guaranteed Consistency

To eliminate catastrophic entropy decoding failures, MLVC introduces a mechanism that explicitly transmits scale parameters within the hyperprior. Instead of asking the decoder to calculate the scales from scratch, the encoder derives them deterministically from the quantized hyperlatent representation $\hat{z}$.

The process follows a structured three-step approach:
1.  **Slicing and Absolute Value:** A portion of the quantized hyperlatent $\hat{z}$ is extracted, and its absolute value is computed to form a base index map $I_{base} \in \mathbb{N}^{C_y/r \times H_z \times W_z}$.
2.  **Expansion:** This map is expanded spatially by a factor and replicated across channels. This structured sharing reduces the amount of extra data that needs to be sent by a factor of $128\times$, ensuring the bitrate remains manageable.
3.  **Lookup:** The resulting indices $I_{c,h,w}$ are used to pull quantized scale values $\sigma_{c,h,w}$ from a pre-defined codebook that is identical on all devices.

Because the hyperlatent $\hat{z}$ is itself transmitted using a fixed, factorized entropy model that is platform-independent, both the encoder and decoder are guaranteed to arrive at the exact same scale parameters. This design also improves decoding speed because all scales $\sigma_t$ are known before the main latent groups are decoded, allowing for more efficient parallel processing.

## Hardware-Compatible Architectural Design

Beyond entropy consistency, a deployable codec must run efficiently on the NPUs of consumer devices. Many modern neural architectures use complex activation functions like Swish or SiLU, which are often approximated differently by hardware vendors, leading to further divergence.

MLVC utilizes a hardware-friendly architecture by restricting activations to ReLU and LeakyReLU, which have consistent implementations across Apple, Intel, and Qualcomm hardware. To recover the performance lost by using simpler activations, the researchers introduced a channel-split variant of ReGLU in the depth-wise convolution blocks. This module replaces simple addition with multiplicative gating, using a capped activation function $\text{ReLU1}(x) = \min(\text{ReLU}(x), 1)$ to stabilize training and minimize divergence.

To handle temporal information, the decoder is equipped with a gated memory module. This module maintains an explicit long-term state $m_t$, governed by forget gates $g_f$ and output gates $g_o$. This allows the model to "remember" information about occluded objects or static backgrounds over long periods without significantly increasing the computational load. The entire model is also unified; rather than having separate models for I-frames (standalone images) and P-frames (inter-frame predictions), an I-frame is simply treated as a P-frame that references a uniform gray image.

## Managing Long-Term Robustness and Drift

Even with deterministic entropy decoding, small floating-point differences can still cause "gradual divergence" in the reconstructed video frames over long prediction chains. If a video sequence runs for thousands of frames, these tiny errors in pixel values can eventually accumulate.

MLVC manages this through two primary methods:
*   **Long-Term Reference (LTR) Recovery:** Periodic LTR frames are introduced to bound the length of prediction chains. By referencing a high-quality frame from the recent past rather than just the immediately preceding frame, the model can "reset" some of the accumulated errors.
*   **Periodic I-frames:** To fully resynchronize the encoder and decoder states, the codec uses regular I-frame intervals. While this adds some bitrate overhead, it is a practical necessity for robustness in real-world streaming where packet loss or hardware drift might occur.

## Performance and Deployment Results

The efficacy of MLVC was tested across a heterogeneous hardware suite, including the Apple M3 Pro, Intel Lunar Lake NPU, and Qualcomm Snapdragon X Elite. The results confirm that MLVC is the first neural codec to achieve real-time performance and cross-platform robustness simultaneously.

### Speed and Efficiency
On commodity NPUs, MLVC achieves an average encoding speed of 103 FPS and a decoding speed of 99 FPS at 360p resolution. Even at 540p, it maintains speeds above 30 FPS, making it suitable for high-quality video conferencing. A lighter variant, MLVC-S, can reach 30 FPS at 1080p resolution on Apple silicon.

### Compression Performance
In objective tests (PSNR-based), MLVC achieves an average bitrate saving of -52.0% relative to a hardware-accelerated HEVC implementation (HEVC-QSV). While this is a slight reduction in efficiency compared to experimental models like DCVC-RT (which achieves -69.6% but fails cross-platform), it represents a significant leap over other cross-platform compatible neural methods.

### Subjective Quality
Subjective tests, which measure how humans perceive video quality (Mean Opinion Score or MOS), show even more impressive results. Fine-tuned with perceptual losses and region-of-interest (ROI) masking to prioritize facial details, MLVC achieves a MOS-based BD-rate of -75.5% at 360p. This means it provides the same perceived quality as HEVC-QSV while using only a quarter of the bits.

The cross-platform BD-rate matrix confirms the reliability of the system. Across different combinations of Apple, Intel, and Qualcomm encoders and decoders, the variation in performance was consistently within 2%, proving that the scale-sending mechanism successfully prevented the catastrophic failures seen in previous models.

## Conclusion

MLVC represents a shift in neural video compression research from theoretical efficiency to practical deployability. By identifying and solving the "catastrophic failure" problem caused by numerical divergence, the researchers have created a codec that can actually be used in the diverse ecosystem of consumer electronics. 

The combination of a deterministic scale-sending mechanism, hardware-compatible activation functions, and efficient temporal modeling allows MLVC to deliver superior video quality at lower bitrates than traditional hardware encoders, all while maintaining the real-time speeds necessary for modern communication. This work establishes a viable path for the integration of learned codecs into mass-market applications, bridging the gap between state-of-the-art research and real-world utility.
