## Integrating Nonprobability Samples into Small Area Estimation

Small Area Estimation (SAE) is a critical field in survey statistics used to produce reliable estimates for geographic areas or demographic groups where the sample size is too small for standard direct estimation. Traditionally, these estimates rely on probability samples (PS), where every unit in the population has a known, non-zero probability of selection. However, the landscape of data collection is shifting. Declining response rates and the high costs of maintaining rigorous probability-based surveys have led researchers to explore nonprobability samples (NPS).

Nonprobability samples, such as web-based opt-in surveys, administrative records, or satellite imagery, offer significant advantages, including larger sample sizes and lower costs. Despite these benefits, NPS data are often biased because the selection mechanism is unknown and typically correlated with the variables of interest. This research evaluates how these biased datasets can be effectively integrated with traditional survey methods to improve SAE performance without sacrificing accuracy.

The primary challenge in using NPS is the lack of a design-based framework. In a probability sample, the known inclusion probabilities allow for unbiased estimation of population totals. In an NPS, individuals select themselves into the sample, creating a "selection bias" that can lead to the "Big Data Paradox": a larger sample size that provides more precise but highly inaccurate (biased) results. This study systematically compares various statistical methods designed to mitigate this bias in the context of SAE.

## Measuring Data Quality: The Data Defect Correlation (DDC)

To evaluate the impact of bias, the researchers use the Data Defect Correlation (DDC) framework. Proposed by Meng (2018), the DDC ($\rho_{r,y}$) quantifies the correlation between an individual's inclusion in a sample and their response value. In a perfect simple random sample, the DDC is zero. In a biased sample, the DDC is non-zero, and its impact on the error of the sample mean is magnified by the population size $N$.

The relationship between the sample size $n$, the population size $N$, and the bias is captured by the bias-aware effective sample size ($n_{eff}$):

$$ n_{eff} \approx \frac{n}{1 + \rho_{r,y}^2 (N-1)} $$

This equation demonstrates that if the correlation $\rho_{r,y}$ is even slightly positive or negative, the effective information provided by millions of NPS records can be reduced to a handful of unbiased records. In the simulation study, the authors vary the DDC across three settings—Favorable, Typical, and Extreme—to mimic different levels of survey quality. In the extreme case, despite having a large sample size, the effective sample size $n_{eff}$ drops to approximately 7, rendering the data nearly useless for standard estimation.

## Methodological Frameworks for NPS Integration

The study reviews and adapts four main families of methodologies for combining PS and NPS data. Each family addresses the unknown selection mechanism of the NPS differently.

### 1. Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW)
IPW methods attempt to estimate the "propensity" of an individual to be included in the nonprobability sample. By treating the PS as a reference that represents the true population, researchers can fit a logistic regression model to estimate these inclusion probabilities. The inverse of these probabilities is then used to weight the NPS data.

A key variation explored is the inclusion of the response variable $y_i$ in the propensity model (IPW+Y). If the outcome of interest is available in both the PS and NPS, using it to predict inclusion can significantly reduce bias, as it directly accounts for the relationship between the outcome and the selection mechanism.

### 2. Superpopulation Models and MRP
Superpopulation models, such as Multilevel Regression and Poststratification (MRP), assume the outcome follows a specific statistical model (e.g., a binomial model for binary data). This model is fit to the NPS data and then used to predict outcomes for the entire population. These predictions are then aggregated (poststratified) to the small area level using known population counts. The study also introduces Cell-Level Inclusion Probabilities (CLIP), which estimate inclusion probabilities based only on NPS counts and known population totals, removing the absolute requirement for a PS.

### 3. NPS-Informed Priors (NIP)
Working within a Bayesian framework, NIP methods use the NPS data to inform the prior distributions of a model fit to the PS data. This is achieved through "power priors," where the likelihood of the NPS data is raised to a power $a \in [0, 1]$ before being combined with the PS data:

$$ \pi(\theta | y_{NPS}, a) \propto [L(\theta | y_{NPS})]^a \pi(\theta) $$

The weight $a$ controls how much influence the NPS data have. The authors test an "exp-link" variant where $a$ is determined by a test statistic $T^2$ comparing the PS and NPS coefficients:

$$ a = \exp(-c T^2) $$

If the NPS data are highly inconsistent with the PS data (high $T^2$), the weight $a$ approaches zero, effectively ignoring the biased NPS data.

### 4. Aggregate-level Combination (VSW)
Unlike unit-level models that require individual records, aggregate-level methods combine the summary statistics from the PS and NPS for each small area. The VSW method models the bias of the NPS at the area level, allowing for the integration of data when individual-level records are unavailable or restricted by privacy laws.

## The Simulation Study: California Health Coverage

To test these methods, the researchers used a finite population based on the 2023 American Community Survey (ACS) Public Use Microdata Sample (PUMS) for California. This "oracle population" contains $N = 1,475,631$ individuals partitioned into $D = 281$ Public Use Microdata Areas (PUMAs).

The objective was to estimate the prevalence of `pubcov` (means-tested public health coverage, such as Medicaid). The population prevalence is 60.3%. The simulation setup included:
*   **Probability Sample (PS)**: A Poisson sample with a sampling fraction $f = 0.005$, yielding about 7,264 records. It is designed to be high quality with a DDC $\rho_{r,y} \approx N^{-1/2} \approx 0.0008$.
*   **Nonprobability Sample (NPS)**: A larger sample with a fraction $f^* = 0.05$ (74,000 records). Its inclusion probabilities were manipulated to create three DDC scenarios:
    *   **Favorable**: Low bias (-3.0 percentage points).
    *   **Typical**: Moderate bias (-6.1 percentage points).
    *   **Extreme**: High bias (-19.0 percentage points).

All Bayesian models used weakly informative priors, such as $\beta \sim N(0, 9I)$ for fixed effects and $\sigma_\eta \sim \text{Cauchy}^+(0, 5)$ for the standard deviation of area-level random effects.

## Comparative Results and the Big Data Paradox

The results highlights the trade-offs between precision (low variance) and accuracy (low bias).

In the **Favorable DDC** setting, almost all NPS-integrated methods outperformed the PS-only direct estimator. The IPW-BULM+Y method (a Bayesian unit-level model with IPW weights including the response) achieved the lowest Mean Squared Error (MSE), demonstrating that even slightly biased NPS data can provide a massive boost in precision if the selection mechanism is correctly modeled.

However, in the **Extreme DDC** setting, the "Big Data Paradox" became evident. Methods that relied too heavily on the NPS without adequate bias correction produced estimates with near-zero interval coverage. This means that while the estimates were very precise (the confidence intervals were narrow), they were centered far away from the true population value, leading to false conclusions.

The **NPS-Informed Prior (exp-link)** method proved to be the most robust. In favorable conditions, it borrowed strength from the NPS to improve upon the PS-only benchmark. In extreme conditions, the adaptive weighting mechanism recognized the discrepancy and effectively defaulted to the PS-only model, preserving nominal 95% coverage rates. This "do no harm" property is highly desirable for practitioners who may not know the true quality of their NPS data.

Another significant finding was the performance of **IPW-DE-CLIP**. This method does not require a probability sample at all, relying instead on population-level auxiliary information (like census counts). It performed comparably to PS-informed methods in low-bias scenarios, suggesting that SAE can be conducted using only NPS and census data if the selection bias is not extreme.

## Practical Guidance for Survey Statistics

This research provides several key takeaways for practitioners looking to use nonprobability samples for small area estimation:

1.  **Prioritize the Response Variable**: If the outcome variable is available in the probability sample, it should be used in the propensity model for the NPS. This "IPW+Y" approach is the most effective way to reduce MSE across different data quality levels.
2.  **Use Adaptive Bayesian Methods**: If the quality of the NPS is unknown, adaptive methods like the NIP (exp-link) are recommended. They provide a safeguard against extreme bias by automatically downweighting the NPS data when it contradicts the high-quality PS data.
3.  **The Value of Auxiliary Information**: Known population totals for demographic groups are powerful tools. Methods like MRP and CLIP can produce viable estimates even without a concurrent probability sample, provided the DDC is not excessively high.
4.  **Caution with "Big Data"**: A large sample size is not a substitute for a representative sampling design. As DDC increases, the performance of NPS-only estimators degrades rapidly, leading to overconfident and incorrect results.

By categorizing and comparing these methods under a unified framework, the study clarifies the conditions under which nonprobability samples can be safely and effectively integrated into official statistics. This is a vital step toward making SAE more cost-effective and accessible in an era of declining survey participation.
