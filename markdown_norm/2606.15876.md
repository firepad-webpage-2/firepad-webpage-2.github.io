## Introduction to Visualizing Financial Interdependencies

The European sovereign bond market is a complex network of interdependencies where the economic health and policy decisions of one nation can significantly influence its neighbors. Analyzing these relationships is challenging because they are often asymmetric—meaning the influence of country A on country B is not necessarily equal to the influence of country B on country A—and they evolve over time across different economic regimes. 

This research applies a recently developed statistical framework that integrates a generalized h-plot with archetypoid analysis to study 10-year government bond yields across 23 European countries. Unlike traditional clustering methods that seek to group similar countries based on average behavior, this approach identifies "archetypes"—extreme yet representative profiles that define the boundaries of the market's behavior. By representing every country as a mixture of these archetypes, the methodology provides a nuanced view of how national economies relate to one another and how these roles have shifted through the European debt crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic.

## The Challenge of Asymmetric Three-Way Data

In financial markets, the concept of "closeness" or "similarity" between two variables is often more complex than a simple symmetric distance. For instance, a shock in a major economy like Germany might have a profound effect on a smaller economy like Latvia, but the reverse is rarely true. This directional relationship is known as asymmetry.

Furthermore, these relationships are "three-way" because they involve three dimensions: countries (the first mode), the countries they influence or are influenced by (the second mode), and time or economic conditions (the third mode). Standard statistical techniques like Classical Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) often struggle with such data because they typically assume symmetry (where the distance from $i$ to $j$ is the same as $j$ to $i$) and are often designed for two-way data. 

The methodology used in this study overcomes these limitations by using a generalized h-plot. This tool allows for the visualization of non-metric, asymmetric dissimilarity matrices in a unified Euclidean space, making it possible to see how the directional influences between countries change across different time intervals.

## Data Acquisition and Directional Dissimilarity

The researchers analyzed monthly 10-year government bond yields from 2001 to 2025, sourced from Eurostat. To handle the temporal shifts in the market, the data was partitioned into three distinct subintervals:
1.  **January 2001 to March 2011**: The period leading up to the European debt crisis.
2.  **April 2011 to February 2020**: Encompassing the debt crisis and the subsequent recovery phase.
3.  **March 2020 to April 2025**: The COVID-19 crisis and its economic aftermath.

To quantify the relationship between countries, the study uses a measure called *oriented wavelet squared coherence*. This technique is particularly effective for financial time series because it can capture local correlations in both time and frequency while incorporating phase differences. If the movements in country $i$'s bond yields consistently precede those in country $j$, the dissimilarity from $i$ to $j$ will be lower than from $j$ to $i$, indicating that $i$ acts as a leading indicator or influencer for $j$.

## The Generalized h-plot Framework

The core of the visualization process involves transforming the asymmetric dissimilarity matrices into a format suitable for eigendecomposition. For $L$ time periods (where $L=3$), the researchers construct a large concatenated matrix $D$ of size $n \times 2Ln$, where $n$ is the number of countries (23). This matrix includes both the "influencing" profiles (how a country affects others) and the "influenced" profiles (how a country is affected by others) for each period.

The study then computes the variance-covariance matrix $S$ of $D$. By extracting the two largest eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ and their corresponding eigenvectors $q_1, q_2$, a two-dimensional projection $H_2$ is created:

$$
H_2 = [\sqrt{\lambda_1} q_1, \sqrt{\lambda_2} q_2]
$$

In this space, the Euclidean distance between points approximates the standard deviation of the differences between the original variables. This allows the researchers to plot each country multiple times—once for its "influencing" role ($d_{j \cdot l}$) and once for its "influenced" role ($d_{\cdot jl}$) for each of the three periods—providing a map of the market's structure.

## Identifying Extremes: Archetypoid Analysis (ADA)

While the h-plot provides the map, Archetypoid Analysis (ADA) identifies the "landmarks." Most statistical methods, such as K-means clustering, focus on the "average" member of a group. However, in complex systems, understanding the extremes can be more informative. 

Archetypal analysis identifies extreme patterns (archetypes) such that each data point can be represented as a convex combination of these extremes. Because archetypes are often synthetic points, this study uses *archetypoids*, which are required to be actual observations (specific countries in specific states) from the dataset. 

Mathematically, for a data matrix $Y$ of size $n \times 4L$, ADA seeks to find $k$ archetypoids $z_j$ such that each country profile $y_i$ is approximated as:

$$
\hat{y}_i = \sum_{j=1}^k \alpha_{ij} z_j
$$

where the coefficients $\alpha_{ij}$ represent the mixture weight of each archetype, subject to:

$$
\alpha_{ij} \geq 0 \text{ and } \sum_{j=1}^k \alpha_{ij} = 1
$$

The archetypoids themselves are selected from the original data points using a binary selection matrix $\beta$:

$$
z_j = \sum_{l=1}^n \beta_{jl} x_l
$$

By minimizing the residual sum of squares (RSS), the algorithm identifies the set of countries that best represent the diversity of the entire European bond market.

## Results: The Three Pillars of the European Market

Using the "elbow criterion" on a plot of RSS versus the number of archetypoids, the researchers determined that $k=3$ archetypoids were optimal for describing the European market. The identified archetypal countries were **Greece (EL)**, **Hungary (HU)**, and the **Netherlands (NL)**.

The market structure revealed by the h-plot (see {fig:generalized-h-plot-showing-bond-yield-relationships}) shows three distinct branches:
*   **The Netherlands Archetype (Core EU)**: This archetype dominates countries with stable, low-risk bond yields, including Germany, Austria, Finland, France, and Luxembourg. These countries are clustered tightly, indicating high synchronization and similar influenced/influencing profiles.
*   **The Greece Archetype (Southern Peripheral)**: This profile represents countries more heavily impacted by the debt crisis, such as Portugal, Spain, and Italy. Interestingly, Ireland initially belonged to this group but transitioned toward the core EU group in later periods.
*   **The Hungary Archetype (Central and Eastern Europe)**: This branch captures the behavior of emerging and non-Eurozone Baltic and Central European countries like Poland, the Czech Republic, and Lithuania.

The mixture coefficients ($\alpha$) provide a detailed look at countries that sit between these archetypes. For example, Cyprus was found to be a mixture of 36% Greece-like behavior and 64% Hungary-like behavior. Ireland's profile was split between Greece (54%) and the Netherlands (46%), illustrating its unique position as a country that successfully navigated a return from the periphery toward the core.

## Dynamic Evolution and Asymmetry

One of the most significant findings involves how the degree of asymmetry in the market changed over time. Asymmetry measures the extent to which relationships are directional.

The study found that the second period (2011–2020), which covered the European debt crisis, exhibited the **highest degree of asymmetry**. This suggests that during times of financial stress, influence becomes more unidirectional, with certain countries leading the market's movements while others follow. In contrast, the third period (2020–2025), following the COVID-19 pandemic, showed the lowest asymmetry. This indicates a more synchronized, collective response across Europe, likely driven by common external shocks and unified policy responses like the NextGenerationEU recovery fund.

At the individual country level, the analysis revealed:
*   **Greece** had the lowest asymmetry, suggesting it evolved somewhat independently or its directional influences were balanced.
*   **The Baltic countries (Lithuania, Latvia)** and **Luxembourg** showed the highest asymmetry, indicating they were more prone to being part of strong cause-effect relationships in the bond market.

## Significance and Impact

This research demonstrates that the combination of generalized h-plots and archetypoid analysis is a powerful tool for macroeconomic analysis. By focusing on archetypes rather than averages, the method captures the full spectrum of market behavior, identifying the "boundary" countries that define the economic landscape.

For policymakers and investors, this approach offers several benefits:
1.  **Risk Management**: By identifying which archetype a country's bond yield most closely resembles, investors can better assess the type of risk (e.g., core vs. peripheral) they are exposed to.
2.  **Tracking Contagion**: The visualization of asymmetric influences helps in understanding how shocks might propagate from one specific economy to another.
3.  **Policy Evaluation**: Observing how a country's mixture coefficients change over time (like Ireland's shift toward the core) provides a quantitative way to measure the success of economic reforms or the impact of regional integration.

Finally, the study promotes transparency by making the underlying R code and datasets publicly available, allowing other researchers to apply these advanced visualization and dimensionality reduction techniques to other financial domains, such as equity markets or exchange rate dynamics.
