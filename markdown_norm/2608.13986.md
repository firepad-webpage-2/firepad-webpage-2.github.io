## Re-evaluating the Foundations of Statistical Modeling

In statistical science, particularly within the Bayesian framework, researchers often rely on a series of fundamental distinctions: the frequentist versus the Bayesian approach, objective versus subjective probability, and the separation of a theoretical model from the empirical data it describes. While these distinctions provide a structured way to conduct inference, they have long been the subject of philosophical debate. Some critics argue that Bayesian updating is less about genuine learning from the world and more about the internal re-expression of a pre-existing model.

David J. T. Sumpter's work addresses these foundational issues by applying the philosophy of Donald Davidson to statistical practice. The paper argues that many of the standard "dualisms"—specifically the idea that there is a conceptual "scheme" (the model) that is distinct from uninterpreted "content" (the data)—are fundamentally problematic. By drawing on Davidson’s "radical interpretation" program, the paper suggests that statistical modeling should not be viewed as a way to bridge a gap between a subjective mind and an objective world. Instead, it is a process of building coherent belief systems that are grounded through social interaction and shared experience.

## Dissolving the Dualism of Objective and Subjective Probability

A central point of contention in statistics is the nature of probability. Is it a "subjective" degree of belief held by an individual, or an "objective" property of the physical world? Traditionally, the variable $P$ is used to represent a person's degree of belief, while $\mathbb{P}$ might represent a hypothetical "true" or objective probability.

Sumpter follows the tradition of Ramsey, de Finetti, and Savage, arguing that we only ever have access to $P$. However, he goes a step further by using Davidson's critique of the "third dogma of empiricism"—the idea that there is a world of "given" data independent of our conceptual schemes. If we discard this dualism, the distinction between subjective and objective probability begins to dissolve. 

The paper posits that a degree of belief is inherently objective because it is always about a shared, objective world. To ask whether our beliefs $P$ converge to some external $\mathbb{P}$ is, from this perspective, to presuppose an unintelligible gap between our thoughts and reality. Instead, objectivity is found in the way our belief systems are constrained by coherence and their usefulness in navigating a shared environment.

## The Interdependence of Models and Data

In standard practice, we treat the model parameters $\Theta$ and the data $D$ as distinct entities. We imagine the model as a structure that exists prior to the data, which we then "fit" or "update" when observations arrive. Sumpter challenges this separation, suggesting that models and data are interdependent parts of a single system.

Using the example of estimating the scoring rate of a football team, the paper illustrates that a parameter like the goal rate $\lambda$ can be seen as a summary statistic derived from the data itself. For instance, in a sufficiently long sequence of games, the parameter $\lambda$ might be defined as the limiting frequency of goals:

$$\lambda = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N x_i$$

where $x_i$ represents the number of goals in game $i$. In this view, the model parameter is not a hidden "truth" separate from the data, but a specific way of representing the data sequence. Furthermore, the act of collecting data is "model-laden." What a researcher chooses to record as "data" is already influenced by their underlying model of what is relevant. There is no such thing as "pure" data that exists outside of a conceptual framework.

## Re-interpreting Bayes' Theorem as Internal Coherence

Perhaps the most significant re-evaluation in the paper concerns Bayes' theorem. Typically, Bayes' theorem is viewed as a mechanism for updating: you start with a prior $P(\Theta)$, observe data $y$, and compute a posterior $P(\Theta | D = y)$. 

$$P(\Theta = \theta | D = y) = \frac{P(D = y | \Theta = \theta) P(\Theta = \theta)}{P(D = y)}$$

Sumpter argues that, mathematically, all terms in this equation—the prior, the likelihood, and the posterior—are variables within a single, pre-defined probability space. When we "observe" data, we are not changing the underlying belief system $P$; we are simply selecting a specific conditional probability that was already defined within that system.

This suggests that what is often called "learning" in Bayesian inference is actually a process of internal redistribution within a fixed scheme. Genuine learning, according to the paper, happens at a higher level: when we realize our entire belief system $P$ is inadequate and we decide to build a new one. This aligns with the "hypothetico-deductive" view of statistics, where we propose a model, check its implications against the world, and revise the whole structure if it fails.

## Davidson’s Constraints: Charity and Triangulation in Statistics

To provide a rational basis for building and revising these belief systems, Sumpter introduces three constraints derived from Davidson’s work:

1.  **Coherence:** The belief system must be logically consistent and follow the axioms of probability. This is the standard requirement for Bayesian models.
2.  **Charity:** To interpret any belief system (including our own or a colleague's), we must assume it is largely rational and captures true features of the world. In statistics, "charity" translates to the assumption that priors and model structures are sensible and grounded in some level of understanding. Without this assumption, communication and interpretation between researchers become impossible.
3.  **Triangulation:** This is the process by which the content of a belief is fixed. It requires at least two agents interacting with each other and with a common object in the world. In a statistical context, this means that a model is not just a private mental state; it is disciplined by its answerability to other researchers and to the shared empirical reality they are all observing.

Triangulation provides the "third point" that anchors a model. If Joe and Jen are both modeling a football team's performance, their shared observations of the actual games (the common cause) and their ability to discuss their models (social interaction) are what make their individual beliefs objective.

## Connecting Philosophical Principles to Statistical Practice

The paper argues that these philosophical constraints are not just abstract ideas but are already present in best statistical practices. 

One example is **prior elicitation**. When statisticians use frameworks like the "Sheffield method" to gather expert opinions for a prior, they are engaging in a social process of triangulation. They are ensuring that the prior is not just a random guess but a charitable interpretation of collective knowledge, grounded in shared experience. Similarly, the use of "weakly informative priors" can be seen as a way to ensure that a model remains charitable—that it does not make absurdly strong claims that would make it unintelligible to others.

Another example is the **iterative model-checking workflow** advocated by statisticians like Andrew Gelman and Cosma Shalizi. This workflow involves:
*   Building a model based on current understanding (Coherence and Charity).
*   Checking the model's predictions against real-world data.
*   Critically evaluating the model’s failures and rebuilding the system if necessary (Triangulation).

This process acknowledges that the "updating" happens not just inside the math of Bayes' theorem, but in the human decision to change the model itself when it no longer aligns with the triangulated reality.

## Redefining Objectivity and the Utility of Models

The paper concludes by reconsidering the famous maxim by George Box: "All models are wrong, but some of them are useful." From a Davidsonian perspective, Sumpter suggests this might be misleading. If a model is consistently useful and allows us to successfully navigate the world and communicate with others, it is because the model is capturing something "correct" about our shared reality. 

Instead of seeing models as failed attempts to copy an external "master reality," we should see them as tools for interpretation. Objectivity is not a "view from nowhere" but a social achievement. It is the result of multiple people, using different models, reaching a consensus because their belief systems are charitable to one another and triangulated against the same world.

By shifting the focus from "truth as correspondence to an external world" to "truth as coherence, charity, and triangulation," Sumpter provides a philosophical grounding for the entire lifecycle of statistical modeling—from the first elicitation of a prior to the final check of a complex model's validity. This perspective encourages statisticians to view their work not as the discovery of hidden parameters, but as the construction of robust, shared, and interpretable systems of belief.
