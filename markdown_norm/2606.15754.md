## Challenges in Ordinal Causal Inference

In many fields, such as public health, medicine, and social science, researchers often encounter variables that are ordered but not truly numerical. Examples include cancer staging (Stage I to IV), Likert scales (Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree), or physical disability scores. These are known as ordinal outcomes. Estimating the causal effect of a treatment on these outcomes is inherently difficult because the "distance" between categories is not defined. For instance, the improvement from Stage I to Stage II of a disease is not necessarily equivalent to the improvement from Stage III to Stage IV.

Standard causal estimands like the average treatment effect (ATE) rely on calculating mean differences, which requires assigning arbitrary numbers to these categories. This practice can lead to results that change depending on the chosen scale. To avoid this, researchers focus on probability-based estimands that respect the ordering of the data. Two primary quantities of interest are the probability that the treatment outcome is strictly better than the control outcome, denoted by $\eta = P\{Y(1) > Y(0)\}$, and the probability that the treatment outcome is at least as good as the control outcome, denoted by $\tau = P\{Y(1) \geq Y(0)\}$.

The fundamental problem of causal inference is that for any single individual, we can observe only one potential outcome: either the result under treatment $Y(1)$ or the result under control $Y(0)$, but never both. Consequently, the joint distribution of $(Y(0), Y(1))$—which is necessary to calculate $\eta$ and $\tau$—is unobservable. We can only identify the marginal distributions (the probability of each outcome level within the treatment group and the control group separately). This leaves $\eta$ and $\tau$ unidentifiable, meaning they cannot be uniquely determined from the data without further assumptions. Instead, researchers derive "sharp bounds"—the tightest possible intervals—within which these causal effects must lie.

## Visualizing Causal Estimands: The Colored Matrix Approach

To make the problem of unidentifiability more intuitive, this work utilizes a graphical tool called "colored matrices." Imagine a grid where the horizontal axis represents the possible values of the control outcome $Y(0)$ and the vertical axis represents the possible values of the treatment outcome $Y(1)$. Each cell in the grid represents the joint probability of a specific pair of outcomes.

For an ordinal variable with levels $\{1, \dots, J\}$, the estimand $\eta$ corresponds to the sum of the probabilities in the cells above the main diagonal (where $Y(1) > Y(0)$). The estimand $\tau$ includes these cells plus the diagonal itself (where $Y(1) = Y(0)$). 

The marginal distributions provide the sums of the rows and columns of this matrix. The bounding problem then becomes a mathematical exercise: how can we distribute the probability mass within the cells to maximize or minimize the sum of the "favorable" cells (those defining $\eta$ or $\tau$) while still respecting the row and column sums provided by the data? As shown in {fig:visual-representation-of-the-joint-probability-distribution-for-potential-outcomes-highlighting-the-cells-that-contribute-to-the-estimand-eta}, the visualization helps identify which parts of the joint distribution are constrained by the data and which are entirely free to vary, contributing to the width of the bounds.

## The Limits of Model-Free Bounds

When no assumptions are made about the relationship between $Y(0)$ and $Y(1)$, we use model-free sharp bounds. Let $\Delta_j = P\{Y(1) \geq j\} - P\{Y(0) \geq j\}$ represent the difference in the marginal tail probabilities at level $j$. The bounds for $\tau$ and $\eta$ are defined as:

$$
\tau_L = \max_{1 \leq j \leq J} \{P\{Y(0) = j\} + \Delta_j\}
$$

$$
\tau_U = \min_{1 \leq j \leq J} \{1 + \Delta_j\}
$$

$$
\eta_L = \max_{1 \leq j \leq J} \{\Delta_j\}
$$

$$
\eta_U = \min_{1 \leq j \leq J} \{1 + \Delta_j - P\{Y(1) = j\}\}
$$

In practice, these bounds are often frustratingly wide. For example, in a clinical trial, the model-free bounds for $\eta$ might range from 0.15 to 0.79. This tells us only that somewhere between 15% and 79% of patients benefit from the treatment—a range so large it provides little guidance for clinical decision-making. To obtain more useful results, researchers often look for ways to tighten these intervals by introducing assumptions about the dependence between potential outcomes.

## Why Standard Positive Dependence is Insufficient

A common intuition in causal inference is that potential outcomes should be positively dependent. That is, an individual who performs well under control is likely to also perform well under treatment. This has led some researchers to suggest using "independence-based" estimands ($\eta_I$ and $\tau_I$), calculated as if $Y(0)$ and $Y(1)$ were independent, as lower bounds for the true causal effects. The logic is that if the outcomes are actually positively correlated, the true $\eta$ and $\tau$ should be higher than the values calculated under independence.

However, this research demonstrates that standard, well-known notions of positive dependence are insufficient to guarantee this. Specifically, even strong conditions like Positive Regression Dependence (PRD)—where the probability of a good outcome under treatment increases as the outcome under control improves—do not mathematically ensure that $\tau_I \leq \tau$ or $\eta_I \leq \eta$. Through numerical counterexamples, the authors show that one can construct joint distributions that satisfy PRD but where the independence-based estimate actually overestimates the causal effect. This finding is significant because it highlights a potential flaw in how researchers have previously justified using independence as a conservative lower bound.

## Establishing Diagonal Tail Dominance (DTD)

To solve the problem of invalid independence-based bounds, the paper introduces a new set of conditions called Diagonal Tail Dominance (DTD). These conditions are specifically designed to be sufficient for the independence-based estimates to serve as valid lower bounds.

1.  **Open-tail DTD:** This condition requires that $P\{Y(1) > j | Y(0) = j\} \geq P\{Y(1) > j\}$ for all $j$. In simpler terms, it means that for individuals who would have had outcome level $j$ under control, the probability of doing strictly better under treatment is at least as high as the average probability of doing better than level $j$ in the whole population. If this holds, then $\eta_I \leq \eta$.
2.  **Closed-tail DTD:** This condition requires that $P\{Y(1) \geq j | Y(0) = j\} \geq P\{Y(1) \geq j\}$ for all $j$. This ensures that individuals at level $j$ under control are not less likely to achieve at least level $j$ under treatment than the general population. If this holds, then $\tau_I \leq \tau$.

These DTD conditions are weaker than PRD in some ways but more targeted toward the diagonal of the joint probability matrix. Crucially, like all assumptions about joint potential outcomes, DTD is untestable. One cannot prove it holds using the observed data alone; it must be justified by domain knowledge or theory about the treatment mechanism.

## Local DTD and Refined Bounding Strategies

While global DTD (assuming it holds for all outcome levels) is a powerful tool, it might be too strong an assumption for many real-world scenarios. The authors propose a more flexible approach called "local DTD." Under this framework, a researcher might assume that the dependence condition holds only for certain levels of the outcome—for example, only for the most severe stages of a disease where the treatment is expected to have the most predictable effect.

By assuming DTD holds locally for a subset of levels $D_{ot}$ (for $\eta$) or $D_{ct}$ (for $\tau$), the researchers derived new, improved lower bounds:

$$
\tilde{\eta}_L = \max_{1 \leq j \leq J} \{ \Delta_j + \sum_{k \geq j, k \in D_{ot}} P\{Y(1) > k\} P\{Y(0) = k\} \}
$$

$$
\tilde{\tau}_L = \max_{1 \leq j \leq J} \{ P\{Y(0) = j\} + \Delta_j + \sum_{k > j, k \in D_{ct}} P\{Y(1) \geq k\} P\{Y(0) = k\} \}
$$

These improved bounds allow for a "middle ground." They are tighter and more informative than model-free bounds, but they rely on more nuanced and potentially more plausible assumptions than global independence or global DTD.

## Empirical Application: Acute Ischemic Stroke Trial

The paper illustrates these methods using data from a trial on acute ischemic stroke, where the outcome is the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), a 6-level ordinal scale measuring disability. 

The analysis reveals the stark difference between the various bounding approaches. For the probability of a strictly better outcome ($\eta$):
*   **Model-free bounds:** $[0.155, 0.790]$. This range is very wide.
*   **Independence-based estimate:** $\eta_I = 0.486$. If global DTD is assumed, this becomes the new lower bound, significantly raising the floor from 0.155.
*   **Local DTD bounds:** By assuming DTD only for the more favorable recovery levels (e.g., levels 3-6), the researchers found an improved lower bound of $\tilde{\eta}_L = 0.224$. 

This application demonstrates that even a conservative "local" assumption can meaningfully improve the precision of causal claims compared to making no assumptions at all. It provides a roadmap for how researchers can incorporate clinical intuition to strengthen their statistical findings.

## Broader Implications for Statistical Research

This work provides a rigorous framework for navigating the "identifiability gap" in ordinal data analysis. By identifying that common dependence measures like PRD are insufficient, the study warns against over-reliance on intuitive but mathematically incomplete assumptions. 

The introduction of DTD and Local DTD provides researchers with a new toolkit for sensitivity analysis. Instead of simply reporting wide model-free bounds or a single number based on a strong assumption, researchers can now report a range of bounds based on different levels of dependence assumptions. Furthermore, the principles developed here for ordinal data—using tail probabilities and diagonal dominance—are extendable to discrete numerical and even continuous outcomes, suggesting a broad impact on the methodology of causal inference across various scientific domains.
