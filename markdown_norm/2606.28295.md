## Moving Beyond Static Equilibrium

For nearly a century, economic theory has centered on the concept of general equilibrium. Formalized by Kenneth Arrow and Gerard Debreu, this framework treats an economy as a collection of simultaneous equations where prices adjust to ensure that every agent's plan is consistent with every other's. While this "competitive canon" successfully proved that such equilibria exist under specific conditions, it remained largely silent on how an economy actually reaches that state or how it adapts to crises and growth. 

Crucially, mathematical results known as the Sonnenschein–Mantel–Debreu (SMD) theorems revealed a fundamental problem: simply knowing an equilibrium exists does not tell us if it is unique, stable, or even reachable. This research proposes a fundamental shift. Instead of starting with equilibrium as a fixed point, it treats equilibrium as a specific, limiting state of a much larger, dynamic, and adaptive system. By modeling the economy as an information-processing source and agents as learning entities, the paper demonstrates that the classical competitive model is "nested" within this broader framework, emerging only when specific informational and adaptive frictions vanish.

## The Economy as an Information Source

The first core principle of this new framework is the "Adaptive Primitive." Rather than a set of supply and demand curves, the economy is defined as a stochastic process $X = (X_t)_{t \in \mathbb{Z}}$ on a probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$. This process is assumed to be "asymptotically mean stationary" (AMS), meaning that while the economy might change over time, it eventually settles into a statistical regularity.

Two vital concepts emerge from this definition:

1.  **Entropy Rate ($\bar{H}$):** This measures the amount of "novelty" or unforeseen information the economy generates per period. In a traditional equilibrium model, there is no novelty—everything is predetermined by the state of the world. In the adaptive economy, a positive entropy rate represents genuine uncertainty and the ongoing generation of new events.
2.  **The Dependence Operator ($\hat{T}$):** This is a mathematical object acting on a Hilbert space $L_0^2(\bar{\mu})$ that captures how variables in the economy move together. It represents the underlying "structure" of the economy, such as the network of interactions between sectors or agents.

In this view, the economy is not a puzzle to be solved for a fixed price vector. It is an evolving source of information where the structure $\hat{T}$ and the novelty $\bar{H}$ are the primary objects of study.

## The Bounded Agent and the Gibbs Law

The second pillar of the theory concerns the agents within the economy. Traditional models assume "perfectly rational" agents with unlimited information-processing power. This paper replaces them with "bounded agents" who act as information channels with finite capacity $C$.

An agent's decision-making is modeled as a rate-distortion problem: they try to maximize their utility while constrained by how much information they can process about the state of the economy. The constraint is expressed as $I(\xi; A) \leq C$, where $I$ is the mutual information between the environment $\xi$ and the agent's action $A$.

The result of this constrained optimization is that agents follow a **Gibbs Law** for their choices:

$$
q^*(a) = \frac{p_0(a) \exp(\beta U(a))}{\sum_{a'} p_0(a') \exp(\beta U(a'))}
$$

In this equation:
- $p_0(a)$ is the agent's "prior" or baseline behavior.
- $U(a)$ is the utility of action $a$.
- $\beta$ is the "inverse temperature," which acts as the shadow price of information.

The parameter $\beta$ is critical. If $\beta \to \infty$ (infinite information capacity), the agent becomes the traditional "rational maximizer," always picking the best possible action. If $\beta \to 0$, the agent ignores all rewards and simply follows their prior habits. For any finite $\beta$, the agent’s behavior is stochastic—they are more likely to pick better actions, but they occasionally make "errors" or choose sub-optimal paths due to limited processing capacity.

## The General Nesting Theorem: Five Steps to Equilibrium

The paper's most significant contribution is the **General Nesting Theorem**. It proves that the classical competitive equilibrium is recovered as a "joint limit" of the adaptive economy. For the economy to collapse into the traditional Walrasian state, five conditions must be met simultaneously:

1.  **Zero Entropy Rate ($\bar{H} \to 0$):** All novelty must vanish. The economy becomes perfectly predictable and deterministic.
2.  **Divergent Channel Capacity ($\beta \to \infty$):** Every agent must gain infinite information-processing power, turning their stochastic choices into deterministic utility maximization.
3.  **Infinitely Sharp Selection ($\sigma \to \infty$):** In the population dynamics, the "selection intensity" must become infinite, meaning only the single most "fit" economic structure survives.
4.  **Converged Adaptive Learning:** Agents must have finished learning the patterns of the economy, such that their expectations perfectly match the actual outcomes (Rational Expectations).
5.  **Structural Fixity:** The dependence operator $\hat{T}$ must stop evolving. The network of the economy becomes a static, fixed topology.

When all five conditions are met, the adaptive system exactly reproduces the results of the Arrow-Debreu model. This demonstrates that the "Competitive Canon" is not wrong, but it is a "frictionless corner" of a much larger space. It describes an economy that has stopped learning, stopped changing, and has no limits on information.

## Behavior-Freedom and the "Certification Gate"

A unique aspect of this research is the concept of **"Behaviour-Freedom."** In traditional economics, you cannot say anything about the market unless you assume agents have "rational" preferences (i.e., their preferences are complete and transitive).

This paper shows that the aggregate structure of the economy can be recovered *without* these assumptions. Even if agents use simple heuristics, "rules of thumb," or machine-learning policies that don't satisfy the axioms of rationality, the overall dependence operator $\hat{T}$ can still be identified from the data. 

However, there is a catch: the "Certification Gate." Because we observe the economy through data, we can only "partially identify" its structure. We can see the "self-adjoint" part of the structure, $\hat{T}_s = \frac{1}{2}(\hat{T} + \hat{T}^\top)$, which represents the mutual, symmetric connections. But the "skew" or directed parts (who specifically causes what) might be hidden behind a signal-to-noise threshold. This implies that different underlying structures can be "observationally equivalent," as seen in {fig:structural-identification} and {fig:observational-equivalence}.

## What is Lost in the Limit?

The paper argues that by focusing only on the equilibrium limit, economists inadvertently erase several essential features of reality. The "Erased Contents" include:

- **Novelty:** In equilibrium, there is no "new" information, only the unfolding of pre-known states.
- **Irreversibility:** The "arrow of time" disappears. Equilibrium models are often time-reversible, whereas real economies have a clear directionality driven by entropy.
- **The Structure of Crisis:** Crises are often characterized by a collapse in the recovery of the dependence operator $\hat{T}$. Because the limit assumes $\hat{T}$ is perfectly fixed and known, it cannot naturally represent the process of a systemic collapse.

## Redefining Welfare: From Efficiency to Viability

Finally, the paper proposes a shift in how we judge whether an economy is "doing well." The traditional standard is **Pareto Efficiency**—a state where no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off. 

In an adaptive economy, the new standard is **Viability**. An economic trajectory is viable if it stays within a region where the system can still generate information (finite entropy) and the structure is still recoverable. "Welfare" is no longer just about the efficiency of a single static allocation; it is about the resilience of the system's ability to adapt and learn over time. The classical notion of efficiency is recovered as the frictionless limit of this broader viability criterion.

## Conclusion

This research reframes the relationship between different schools of economic thought. Rather than seeing behavioral economics, information theory, and classical equilibrium as competing theories, it provides a single mathematical framework where they are all connected. The classical competitive model is shown to be the "limiting case" of a world with no informational or adaptive frictions. By starting from the more general adaptive primitive, economists gain the tools to study the dynamics of novelty, the risks of structural collapse, and the behavior of agents who—like real people—must operate with finite minds in an ever-changing world.
