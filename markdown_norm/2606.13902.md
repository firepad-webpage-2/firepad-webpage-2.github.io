## The Evolution of Synthetic Data: From Samples to Populations

The concept of synthetic data originated in the early 1990s as a solution for statistical agencies to share data while protecting individual privacy. Initially proposed by Rubin (1993), the technique was framed as an extension of multiple imputation—a method originally designed to handle missing values in survey data. In this traditional view, the confidential dataset $D_{conf}$ is treated as a sample of size $n$ drawn from a larger population $P$ of size $N$. By using the observed sample to estimate the underlying population distribution, researchers could generate "synthetic" records that maintained the statistical properties of the original sample without releasing the actual records.

For decades, this "sample-to-population" framework was the standard. Synthetic data were primarily used for survey datasets, where $n$ was significantly smaller than $N$. However, the landscape has shifted dramatically with the rise of high-capacity generative models, such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and Variational Autoencoders (VAEs). These models $G$ allow for the synthesis of much more complex data, including electronic health records, financial transactions, and full census-level administrative files.

This technological shift has enabled a new use case: synthesizing entire populations. In this scenario, $n$ effectively equals $N$. When $D_{conf}$ represents the entire population, the fundamental assumptions used to measure the privacy risk of synthetic data—which were built for samples—begin to break down. This paper identifies a critical gap in how researchers evaluate empirical risk when the boundary between the sample and the population disappears.

## The Core Challenge: The Sampling Assumption

The central argument of the work is that current empirical risk metrics implicitly rely on a "sampling assumption." When we generate synthetic data from a sample, the fact that many individuals in the population were not included in the training data provides a natural layer of protection. If an attacker finds a record in the synthetic data $D_{syn}$ that seems to match a real person, there is a high probability that this match is a "false positive" because the person might not even have been in the training set $D_{conf}$.

However, when $D_{conf}$ encompasses the entire population, this protection vanishes. Every person in the population is, by definition, a member of the training set. This changes the nature of three primary privacy risks:
1.  **Membership Inference:** Determining if an individual was used to train the model.
2.  **Singling Out:** Identifying a unique individual based on a combination of attributes.
3.  **Attribute Inference:** Learning new, sensitive information about a person.

Current methodologies for evaluating these risks often utilize hold-out sets—data from the same population that was not used for training—to serve as a baseline. When the training data is the population, there is no "out-of-sample" data left to act as this baseline, making it difficult to distinguish between the model learning general trends and the model memorizing specific individuals.

## Re-evaluating Membership Inference Attacks (MIA)

A Membership Inference Attack (MIA) attempts to determine whether a specific individual $x_i$ was included in $D_{conf}$ by analyzing $D_{syn}$ or the model $G$. The success of an MIA is often quantified by the probability $\text{Pr}(\text{Member} \mid D_{syn})$. In standard machine learning contexts, MIA is a critical metric because membership in a dataset (e.g., a clinical trial for a specific disease) can itself be sensitive information.

The author argues that in the context of population data, MIA may be less relevant for two reasons. First, in many population-level datasets, membership is already public knowledge. For example, if a dataset contains every resident of a specific city, knowing that a resident is "in the data" provides the attacker with zero new information. In such cases, a high MIA success rate does not necessarily indicate a privacy failure, as the "secret" being attacked was never a secret to begin with.

Second, the standard way to measure MIA success involves comparing the model’s performance on members versus non-members. If the dataset includes the entire population, there are no non-members to test against. Researchers often try to circumvent this by splitting the population into a training set and a test set, but this artificially recreates a sampling scenario. The resulting MIA metric then measures the risk of being in the "training sample," which may not reflect the actual risk of being in the "population dataset" that the end-user eventually receives.

## The Heightened Risk of Singling Out

"Singling out" occurs when an individual can be uniquely identified within a dataset. In the context of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), singling out is a primary concern for anonymization. The paper highlights that this risk is fundamentally different—and much higher—when synthesizing populations compared to samples.

Consider a person with a unique set of attributes (e.g., a specific age, occupation, and rare medical condition). If this person is unique in a sample $s \subset P$, we do not know if they are unique in the full population $P$. There might be dozens of people in the wider population with those same attributes who just weren't sampled. This uncertainty protects the individual; an attacker cannot be sure that the record they see in the synthetic data actually belongs to the specific person they have in mind.

In a population dataset, however, uniqueness in the data is equivalent to uniqueness in the world. If the synthetic model $G$ captures the tail of the distribution well enough to generate a unique record that matches a unique person in the population, that person has been singled out. There is no "sampling noise" to hide behind. The author points out that current empirical metrics, which focus heavily on MIA, often fail to capture this specific singling out risk, which is arguably the most significant threat for population-level data science.

## Attribute Inference and the Missing Baseline

Attribute inference occurs when an attacker uses $D_{syn}$ to learn the value of a sensitive attribute $Y$ for an individual, given some known attributes $X$. The risk is usually defined as the gain in knowledge:
$$\text{Pr}(Y \mid X, D_{syn}) - \text{Pr}(Y \mid X)$$
To measure this, researchers typically compare the inference accuracy on a "target" individual who was in the training data against a "baseline" accuracy on individuals who were not.

The "population problem" here is the absence of a baseline. If everyone is in the data, how do we distinguish between "general statistical knowledge" and "private individual knowledge"? For example, if a synthetic population dataset allows an attacker to correctly guess that a specific person has a certain disease, is that because the model memorized that person's record, or because the model correctly learned that 90% of people with that person's demographic profile have that disease?

The paper suggests that in the absence of a natural out-of-sample baseline, the threshold for what constitutes an "acceptable" inference must be a policy decision rather than a purely technical one. Data stewards must decide if certain attributes are so sensitive that even group-level inferences (e.g., "most people in this neighborhood have low income") are prohibited, or if the risk assessment should only focus on inferences that are significantly more accurate for individuals in the population than for a hypothetical person outside of it.

## The Dilemma of Subsampling

A common reaction to these challenges is to simply subsample the population before synthesis. By taking a sample $n < N$, researchers can revert to the traditional framework where MIA and AIA have established baselines. 

However, the author argues that this is not a universal solution. The very purpose of population-level data science is often to study small sub-groups or rare events that would be lost in a subsample. If a researcher needs a full synthetic population to perform a granular policy simulation, subsampling the data just to make the privacy metrics easier to calculate defeats the utility of the dataset.

Furthermore, subsampling provides a specific type of protection (the "sampling's protection") that might be illusory if the sampling rate is high. If we sample 95% of a population, the protection is minimal, yet the metrics might appear more favorable than a 100% synthesis. The paper advocates for being honest about the population-level nature of the data and developing metrics that can handle it, rather than using subsampling as a "metric-fix."

## Conclusions and Future Directions

The commentary concludes by calling for a more context-aware approach to empirical risk measurement. The field currently lacks a standardized toolkit for evaluating synthetic data when the training set is the population. The author identifies several paths forward:

*   **Defining Membership Sensitivity:** Before applying MIA, practitioners should determine if membership is actually a secret.
*   **Developing Singling Out Metrics:** New metrics are needed that specifically measure the probability of generating "unique" synthetic records that correspond to "unique" population records.
*   **Policy-Based Thresholds for AIA:** Since a statistical baseline is missing in population data, we must establish social and legal thresholds for what types of attribute inferences are permissible.
*   **Contextual Evaluation:** Risk should be measured based on the intended use case. A dataset meant for broad public release requires different metrics than one intended for a restricted group of researchers.

By moving away from the assumption that all synthetic data is "sample-based," the research community can develop more robust and honest ways to evaluate the privacy of the large-scale population datasets that are increasingly common in modern data science.
