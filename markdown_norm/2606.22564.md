## The Regulatory Landscape of Sunday Trading

Historically, regulations on store opening hours, often known as "blue laws," have been a fixture of the retail landscape in many countries and U.S. states. These laws typically restrict or prohibit commercial activities on Sundays, originally rooted in religious observance but evolving to serve various social and economic purposes, such as ensuring a collective day of rest for workers and protecting small independent retailers from the competition of larger chains. While the potential social benefits of these restrictions have been widely discussed in economic literature, the direct costs they impose on consumers—specifically the loss of convenience and flexibility—have remained difficult to quantify due to data limitations and the inherent complexity of consumer shopping patterns.

The central challenge in studying retail hours is the problem of endogeneity: stores generally choose their hours based on expected customer demand, and customers choose when to shop based on when stores are open. To disentangle this relationship, researchers require a "natural experiment"—an external event that changes store hours regardless of immediate consumer demand. This study utilizes the repeal of North Dakota’s Sunday closing law on August 1, 2019. Prior to this date, North Dakota maintained one of the strictest blue laws in the United States, requiring most non-grocery retailers to remain closed between midnight and noon on Sundays. The sudden removal of this restriction provides a clear environment to observe how consumers react when previously unavailable shopping windows are opened.

## Overcoming the Endogeneity Challenge

In typical market conditions, observing a correlation between store hours and foot traffic does not reveal which caused the other. A store might stay open late because it expects late-night shoppers, or shoppers might visit late because that is the only store open. This "simultaneity" makes it difficult to estimate the true value consumers place on specific opening hours.

The 2019 North Dakota repeal serves as an exogenous shock. Because the change was a state-level policy shift, it affected all non-exempt retailers simultaneously, regardless of their individual business strategies. By comparing the shopping behavior in North Dakota before and after the repeal to the behavior in neighboring states (Minnesota, South Dakota, and Montana) that did not change their laws, the authors employ a Difference-in-Differences (DiD) framework. This method effectively isolates the impact of the policy change from general seasonal trends or regional economic shifts.

## Data Granularity through Mobile Geolocation

A unique aspect of this research is the use of high-frequency GPS data from mobile devices to track consumer movements. Traditional retail data, such as scanner data or consumer surveys, often lack the temporal and spatial precision needed to see *exactly when* a person visited a store or *how far* they traveled.

The dataset, provided by Advan Research, tracks anonymized pings from mobile phone applications to identify visits to specific retail points of interest with a precision of approximately 10 meters. This allows the researchers to observe hourly visit patterns across thousands of stores. The analysis focuses primarily on two categories of retailers:
1.  **Walmart Stores:** As large general merchandise retailers, these were strictly affected by the Sunday morning ban and typically opened their doors immediately following the repeal.
2.  **Grocery Stores:** These were largely exempt from the original law and could open on Sunday mornings even before 2019. They serve as a crucial comparison group to see if consumers shifted away from grocery stores once the more diverse Walmart locations became available.

## Empirical Evidence of Behavioral Shifts

The study identifies several distinct margins of consumer adjustment following the deregulation. When the restriction was lifted, Sunday morning visits to Walmart stores in North Dakota increased by over 200%. This massive surge confirms that the law was a highly binding constraint on consumer choice.

![event-study-of-sunday-morning-visits-to-walmart](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.22564v1/img-5.jpeg)
*{fig:event-study-of-sunday-morning-visits-to-walmart}: The event study shows a sharp, immediate increase in Sunday morning visits to North Dakota Walmarts following the August 1 repeal (indicated by the dashed line), with no evidence of pre-existing trends.*

Beyond the simple increase in morning visits, the researchers identified three primary sources of this new traffic:
1.  **Temporal Substitution:** Some consumers who previously shopped on Sunday afternoons moved their trips to Sunday mornings. The data shows a statistically significant decrease in Sunday afternoon visits to Walmarts (roughly 8% of the morning increase).
2.  **Store-Type Substitution:** Because grocery stores were always open, they acted as a substitute for closed Walmarts. After the repeal, Sunday morning visits to grocery stores declined as consumers moved back to Walmart for their morning shopping needs.
3.  **Spatial (Cross-Border) Substitution:** Before the repeal, residents in border cities like Fargo often drove across state lines into Minnesota to shop at Walmarts that were legally allowed to open. After the repeal, Sunday morning visits to Minnesota Walmarts near the border dropped, indicating that North Dakotans were now shopping closer to home.

To quantify the causal effect, the authors use a DiD regression of the form:
$$\ln(\text{Visits}_{it}) = \beta (\text{NorthDakota}_i \times \text{AfterRepeal}_t) + \delta_t + \eta_i + \epsilon_{it}$$
In this equation, $\ln(\text{Visits}_{it})$ represents the log of visits to store $i$ at time $t$. The coefficient $\beta$ captures the policy effect, while $\delta_t$ and $\eta_i$ represent time and store fixed effects, and $\epsilon_{it}$ is the error term.

## Quantifying Consumer Welfare

While counting visits provides evidence of behavior, it does not directly tell us how much consumers *value* the new hours. To address this, the authors develop a discrete choice model. In this model, a consumer's utility (satisfaction) from a shopping trip depends on the distance they must travel, the type of store, and the time of day.

The utility $U_{iwt}$ for consumer $i$ choosing store type $w$ at time $t$ is expressed as:
$$U_{iwt} = \alpha D_{iw} + \beta t + \gamma w + \epsilon_{iwt}$$
Where $D_{iw}$ is the distance to the store, $\alpha$ represents how much consumers dislike traveling (distance sensitivity), $\beta$ captures the preference for shopping on Sunday morning, $\gamma$ captures the inherent preference for Walmart over other stores, and $\epsilon_{iwt}$ represents individual random factors.

By observing how far consumers were willing to drive to reach an open store (like a cross-border Walmart or a local grocery store) versus how many stayed home when their preferred store was closed, the researchers can calculate a "compensating distance." This metric translates the inconvenience of the Sunday morning ban into miles. 

The analysis concludes that the Sunday morning closing law was welfare-equivalent to increasing the distance to the nearest Walmart by approximately 1.4 miles for every consumer. For a person who lives 5 miles from a store, the ban made the shopping experience feel as though the store were 6.4 miles away due to the restricted timing.

## Broad Implications for Policy and Retail

The findings of this paper offer a nuanced perspective on the trade-offs inherent in retail regulation. While the total number of unique monthly visitors to stores did not change significantly, the *efficiency* and *convenience* of shopping improved. The study demonstrates that consumers have a strong preference for choosing their own shopping times, and removing restrictions reduces the need for costly workarounds like cross-border travel or substituting to less-preferred store types.

For policymakers, the "1.4-mile" welfare metric provides a concrete way to weigh the consumer costs of blue laws against their intended social benefits. If the goal of a Sunday morning ban is to protect worker leisure, the policy must provide social value greater than the 1.4-mile "inconvenience tax" it imposes on every household.

For retailers, the study highlights the competitive importance of operating hours. The substitution effects observed between Walmarts and grocery stores suggest that hours are a key dimension of competition, similar to price or product selection. When one retailer is forced to close, it creates a temporary local monopoly for exempt businesses, which disappears immediately upon deregulation. As mobile location data becomes more prevalent, this methodology provides a framework for businesses and regulators to analyze the spatial and temporal dynamics of retail markets with unprecedented precision.
