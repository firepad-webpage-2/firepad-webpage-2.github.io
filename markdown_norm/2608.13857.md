## Quantifying Imperial Disintegration through Topology

The study of how empires collapse has historically relied on qualitative narratives, often categorizing the end of a civilization through metaphors like "erosion," "destruction," or "decapitation." This research transitions these historical concepts into a mathematically rigorous framework by applying Topological Data Analysis (TDA), specifically persistent homology, to the Inca Empire (Tawantinsuyu) between 1400 and 1572 CE. 

By modeling the imperial system as a multi-layer network, the authors demonstrate that the Inca collapse follows a unique structural trajectory. Unlike the gradual "erosion" observed in the Roman-Byzantine transition, the Inca case represents a "Substitution Topology." In this mode, the political hierarchy is rapidly decapitated and replaced, while the underlying physical infrastructure—the $12,000$ km road network known as the Qhapaq Ñan—remains topologically intact and is subsequently inherited by the successor state.

## Persistent Homology as a Historical Metric

To analyze the complexity of the Inca network, the study employs persistent homology. This tool allows researchers to quantify the "shape" of data by tracking the birth and death of topological features across different scales. In the context of a network, these features are represented by Betti numbers: $\beta_0$ counts the number of connected components (islands of connectivity), and $\beta_1$ counts the number of fundamental cycles (redundant loops or circuits in the network).

The primary scalar metric used is persistent entropy $H_k(t)$. This value measures the diversity and stability of the "life spans" of these cycles. A high entropy indicates a network with many well-distributed redundant paths, which is often a sign of structural resilience. The formula for entropy in this context is:

$$H_k(t) = -\sum_{i} \frac{p_i}{\sum p_j} \ln \left( \frac{p_i}{\sum p_j} \right) \text{ where } p_i = \text{death}_i - \text{birth}_i$$

The persistence of these cycles represents their "importance" within the network's structure. By calculating the Wasserstein distance $W_p(D_1, D_2)$, the researchers can measure the "topological velocity" of the empire—how fast the network's configuration is changing over time.

## Multi-Layer Network Architecture

The researchers constructed a high-resolution model consisting of $4,492$ nodes and $4,358$ route segments. To capture the full scope of imperial life, they organized this data into six distinct temporal layers:

1.  **$N_{infra}$ (Infrastructure):** The physical road segments of the Qhapaq Ñan. This layer is treated as nearly static, providing a baseline for structural continuity.
2.  **$N_{inca}$ (Inca Political Control):** This layer tracks the territorial extent, administrative legitimacy, and succession stability of the Inca state.
3.  **$N_{alliance}$ (Spanish Alliances):** A model of the growing Spanish dominion through ethnic coalitions and military expansion.
4.  **$N_{trade}$ (Trade and Tribute):** This layer integrates radial tribute flows toward the capital (Cusco) and lateral commercial exchanges.
5.  **$N_{sp,legal}$ (Spanish Legal Administration):** Represents the formal colonial status claimed by the Spanish crown through legal instruments like the encomienda.
6.  **$N_{sp,real}$ (Spanish Effective Administration):** Measures the actual, physical penetration of Spanish control across the provincial units (wamani).

These layers allow for the detection of "simultaneous criticality," where different dimensions of the empire (political, economic, and administrative) may undergo phase transitions at the same time.

## Defining Substitution Topology

The core contribution of the paper is the formalization of "Substitution Topology." This describes a specific collapse mechanism that differs from the military destruction of the Aztecs or the administrative fragmentation of the Han dynasty. A Substitution Topology is defined by three conditions:

*   **Political Decapitation ($ST1$):** The political network of the predecessor collapses abruptly, leading to a state where $H_{inca}(t^*) = 0$.
*   **Infrastructural Continuity ($ST2$):** The physical network remains stable throughout the transition. This is measured by a near-zero Wasserstein distance between the infrastructure at the beginning and the end of the period: $W_1(D_{infra}(t_0), D_{infra}(t_{end})) \approx 0$.
*   **Successor Convergence ($ST3$):** The successor state does not build a new network from scratch; instead, its administrative topology converges toward the predecessor's infrastructure:
$$W_1(D_{successor}(t_{end}), D_{infra}(t_{end})) \ll W_1(D_{successor}(t^*), D_{infra}(t^*))$$

The Inca-Spanish transition fits these criteria perfectly. The Qhapaq Ñan remained topologically constant (entropy $H \approx 1.486$) even as the Inca state vanished and the Spanish administrative layers aligned themselves with the existing road paths.

## Stages of the Inca Collapse

The topological analysis reveals that the collapse was not a single event but a three-stage discontinuous transition.

**Stage I: Stability (1400–1532 CE)**
During the expansionist phase, the political network ($N_{inca}$) was topologically indistinguishable from the infrastructure. The empire maintained a steady state of connectivity and redundancy, with a minor topological shift occurring during the conquest of the Chimu in 1463 CE.

**Stage II: The Cajamarca Criticality (1532–1533 CE)**
History often cites the capture of Atahualpa at Cajamarca in 1532 as the end of the empire. However, the topological data shows a more complex picture. While 1532 was a moment of "simultaneous criticality" (where four network layers reached a phase transition threshold), the Inca political entropy did not immediately vanish. Instead, the total persistence of the network spiked as competing power centers emerged, indicating a state of extreme instability rather than total collapse.

**Stage III: Structural Break (1537–1572 CE)**
The most significant "structural break" occurred in 1537 CE, coinciding with the establishment of the neo-Inca state at Vilcabamba. At this point, the Inca political entropy $H_{inca}$ dropped to zero. The network fragmented completely ($\beta_0$ rose from $538$ to over $4,400$), signifying that the Inca state no longer functioned as a coherent topological system. This suggests a revision of the imperial timeline: 1532 was the trigger of a phase transition, but 1537 was the year of actual structural death.

## Quantifying Colonial Legal Fiction

One of the paper's novel contributions is the quantification of "legal fiction" in colonial administration. This is defined as the divergence between the Spanish legal claims ($N_{sp,legal}$) and their effective administrative control ($N_{sp,real}$).

By measuring the Wasserstein distance between these two layers, the authors track how the "gap" between colonial law and reality was resolved. The divergence peaked at Cajamarca in 1532 ($W_1 = 0.5913$) and then steadily decayed. Interestingly, the analysis shows that the Spanish administrative penetration was nearly complete by 1569, which precedes the arrival of Viceroy Francisco de Toledo—a figure traditionally credited with the final stabilization of the colony. This indicates that the "legal fiction" was largely resolved by the organic expansion of effective control rather than solely by top-down bureaucratic reforms.

## Comparative Imperial Context

The study places the Inca collapse within a broader comparative framework. By using a consistent topological language, the authors distinguish between different civilizational endings:

*   **Roman/Byzantine:** Gradual erosion characterized by a slow, century-scale decline in cycle redundancy.
*   **Han Dynasty:** Administrative fragmentation where the network breaks into smaller, self-similar clusters.
*   **Aztec Empire:** Military destruction where physical infrastructure and political nodes are eliminated simultaneously.
*   **Inca Empire:** Substitution Topology/Decapitation where the "head" (Inca elite) is removed, but the "body" (Qhapaq Ñan) is preserved and re-animated by a new "head" (Spanish administration).

## Conclusion and Significance

By integrating physics-based tools like persistent homology into historical analysis, this research provides a quantitative means to classify imperial dynamics. The identification of Substitution Topology offers a new lens through which to view the Spanish conquest—not just as a military victory, but as a topological inheritance. 

The study demonstrates that infrastructure can act as a persistent "memory" of an empire, outlasting the political institutions that created it. Furthermore, the ability to measure "topological velocity" and "simultaneous criticality" allows researchers to identify hidden tipping points in the life cycles of civilizations that might be missed by traditional narrative history alone. This framework provides a template for future research into how modern networks—whether digital, economic, or physical—might navigate similar transitions of collapse and substitution.
