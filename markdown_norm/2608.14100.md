## Introduction to Exciton-Polariton Condensates and Optical Control

Exciton-polaritons are hybrid quasiparticles that emerge from the strong coupling between photons trapped in a semiconductor microcavity and excitons (electron-hole pairs) in quantum wells. These particles possess a unique combination of properties: the extremely light effective mass of photons and the strong nonlinear interactions of excitons. Because they are bosons, exciton-polaritons can undergo a transition into a macroscopic coherent state known as a condensate, even at temperatures higher than those required for traditional atomic Bose-Einstein condensates.

(b) Schematic of the experimental setup featuring the continuous-wave (CW) pump, pulse train generator, and control pulse line. (c) Real-space image of the two coupled traps.*

Unlike equilibrium systems, polariton condensates are inherently dissipative; polaritons constantly decay as photons through the cavity mirrors and must be continuously replenished by an external energy source. This replenishment is typically achieved through "nonresonant" optical pumping, where a laser creates high-energy excitons that relax down to the condensate state. This process also creates a repulsive "reservoir" of excitons that can be used to shape the potential landscape for the polaritons. By patterning the pump laser into specific shapes, such as rings, researchers can create optical traps that confine the condensate.

A significant challenge in the field is achieving precise, deterministic control over the phase and coherence of these condensates, especially when multiple traps are coupled together. This research focuses on characterizing how coherence emerges as two traps are brought closer and demonstrating a method to manipulate the relative phases of multiple modes within these traps using localized optical pulses.

## Sharp Coherence Crossover in Coupled Traps

The study first examines how the mutual coherence between two adjacent polariton traps develops as a function of their spatial separation. The traps are created by two ring-shaped pump profiles. The coupling between these traps is tuned by varying the width $L$ of the channel connecting them.

![coherence-crossover](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.14100v1/FIG_Coherence.png)
*{fig:coherence-crossover}: (a) Schematic of the trap geometry with variable channel width $L$. (b) First-order mutual coherence $g^{(1)}(\tau)$ for different channel widths. (c) The zero-delay coherence $g^{(1)}(0)$ showing a sharp, threshold-like transition as $L$ increases.*

The degree of mutual coherence is quantified using the first-order correlation function $g^{(1)}(\tau, r)$, which is extracted from interferometric measurements. The researchers found that the coherence does not increase linearly with coupling. Instead, there is a sharp crossover: for small $L$, the two condensates act almost independently with near-zero mutual coherence. As $L$ surpasses a critical threshold, the zero-delay coherence $g^{(1)}(0)$ jumps rapidly toward unity, indicating that the two traps have become phase-locked and behave as a single, extended coherent system.

The coherence is calculated from the reconstructed complex field correlation amplitude $|\tilde{J}(\tau, r)|$ using the expression:

$$g^{(1)}(\tau, r) = \frac{|\tilde{J}(\tau, r)|}{\sqrt{I_1(r)I_2(r)}}$$

where $I_1$ and $I_2$ are the intensities of the light from each interferometer arm. This sharp transition is a hallmark of synchronization in driven-dissipative systems and defines two distinct regimes for phase control: the intermediate-coherence regime and the high-coherence regime.

## Deterministic Phase Control via Localized Perturbations

The core contribution of this work is the demonstration of deterministic phase control. The goal is to change the relative phase between different modes of the condensate without significantly altering their populations (amplitudes). To achieve this, the authors introduce a third laser beam: a single, spatially localized nonresonant "control pulse."

The control pulse is timed to arrive during the evolution of the condensate. Because the pulse is nonresonant, it creates a local increase in the exciton reservoir. This local reservoir acts as a transient repulsive potential $\delta V(t,r)$, which shifts the energy levels of the polariton modes. Crucially, because different modes have different spatial distributions (wavefunctions), they "feel" this potential shift differently. This differential energy shift results in a relative phase accumulation over time.

The experimental setup utilizes a series of eight pulses to "kick" the system into a multimode oscillating state, followed by the control pulse to manipulate the phases. Time-resolved photoluminescence (PL) and interferometry allow the researchers to track the phase evolution in real-time with picosecond resolution.

## Phase Manipulation in the Intermediate-Coherence Regime

In the intermediate-coherence regime, the coupling is strong enough to establish some phase correlation but not enough to fully synchronize the traps into a single global mode. The system is best described as a collection of modes that are partially correlated.

(c) Time-resolved interferogram showing the effect of the control pulse (dashed orange line). When the control pulse is applied to one of the traps, the interferometric fringes show a distinct shift compared to a reference measurement without the pulse. The measurements reveal that the phase shift is not instantaneous; it develops over hundreds of picoseconds as the exciton reservoir created by the pulse relaxes. Despite this significant phase change, the amplitude of the oscillations remains largely unchanged, confirming that the perturbation primarily affects the phase.

## Phase Manipulation in the High-Coherence Regime

In the high-coherence regime, the two traps are strongly coupled, and the condensate occupies extended eigenstates that span both traps. The spatial phase profile in this regime is complex, often featuring phase singularities (vortices) or dislocations where the phase jumps abruptly.

![high-coherence-results](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.14100v1/FIG_Coher.png)
*{fig:high-coherence-results}: Experimental results in the high-coherence regime. (c) Spatial phase distribution showing dislocations. (e, f) Comparison of phase evolution at different spatial points, demonstrating the controlled phase shift induced by the optical pulse.*

The control pulse effectively "rotates" the phase of the combined multimode state. By choosing the spatial position of the control pulse, the researchers can target specific modes. For example, placing the pulse in a region where one mode has a high intensity and another has a node allows for selective phase shifting. The results show that even in this highly synchronized state, the relative phases of the underlying modes can be deterministically steered.

## Theoretical Modeling and Fidelity

To understand the underlying physics, the authors developed reduced four-mode theoretical models. Depending on the coherence regime, they used either a "two-particle" description (for weakly correlated traps) or a "coherent-field" description (for strongly coupled traps).

In the coherent-field model, the condensate state is expanded in a basis of four eigenstates $\Phi_\nu(r)$ of the double-trap potential. The dynamics of the complex amplitudes $u_\nu(t)$ are governed by a Hamiltonian that includes the time-dependent potential shift from the control pulse. The simulations showed that the effect of the pulse can be described by a diagonal phase operator:

$$S = \text{diag}(e^{i\phi_1}, e^{i\phi_2}, e^{i\phi_3}, e^{i\phi_4})$$

This operator $S$ shifts the phase of each mode $\nu$ by an amount $\phi_\nu$. To quantify how well this simple phase-shift model describes the actual physical process, the authors calculated the fidelity $F$.

![simulation-comparison](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.14100v1/FIG_SimulHalfQuant.png)
*{fig:simulation-comparison}: Theoretical simulation of the intermediate-coherence regime, matching the experimental observations of phase accumulation and amplitude preservation.*

The fidelity is defined as the normalized overlap between the coherence matrix resulting from the full simulation ($G_{ctrl}$) and a target matrix ($G_{tar}$) generated by applying the ideal phase operator to a reference state ($G_{ref}$). They found that the fidelity exceeds 0.995 in both regimes. This remarkably high value indicates that the localized nonresonant pulse acts almost perfectly as a phase manipulator, with negligible unwanted effects like mode scattering or significant population transfer.

## Significance of the Findings

This work provides a foundational method for controlling complex light-matter states in semiconductor microcavities. By demonstrating that a sharp coherence crossover exists, it establishes the operational boundaries for different types of coupling in polariton networks.

The deterministic phase control demonstrated here is unique because it:
1.  **Preserves Amplitudes:** It allows for "phase-only" manipulation, which is critical for maintaining the state of a system during computation or simulation.
2.  **Is Multimode:** It can simultaneously control the relationships between multiple spatial modes, rather than just a single global phase.
3.  **Is Reconfigurable:** The use of optical pulses means the control can be applied at any time and any position, providing a platform for dynamic, programmable phase transformations.

These capabilities are essential for the development of polariton-based simulators, which aim to solve complex optimization problems by mapping them onto the phase degrees of freedom of a polariton network. The ability to perform high-fidelity phase operations moves the field closer to realizing practical, optically controlled polariton logic and simulation devices.
