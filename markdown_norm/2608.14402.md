## Introduction to Mechanistic Mortality Modeling

The Gompertz law, formulated in 1825, remains one of the most resilient observations in demography and actuarial science. It states that the probability of death for adults increases exponentially with age. While the statistical fit of the Gompertz law is well-documented, the underlying biological and systemic reasons for this specific pattern are still being explored. Understanding the "why" behind mortality trajectories is essential for accurately pricing life insurance, valuing annuities, and managing pension risks, particularly as populations age and mortality patterns evolve at extreme ages.

Modern research into mortality typically follows one of two mechanistic pathways: the reliability approach or the vitality approach. The reliability approach draws from engineering principles, viewing the human body as a complex system of redundant components that gradually fail over time. In contrast, the vitality approach conceptualizes survival as the consumption of a latent "survival energy" or capacity that declines until it reaches zero, at which point death occurs.

The research presented here establishes a unified actuarial framework to compare these two paradigms. By calibrating both models to empirical data, the study investigates how different aging mechanisms—systemic deterioration versus capacity depletion—converge on the Gompertz law and how they differ in explaining phenomena like late-life mortality plateaus and biological age.

## The Reliability Approach: Accumulating System Failure

In the reliability framework, an organism is modeled as a system consisting of $N$ interdependent subsystems. Aging is characterized by the transition of these subsystems from a functional to a dysfunctional state. If $F(t)$ represents the number of dysfunctional subsystems at time $t$, the state of the system progresses as more components fail.

The rate of failure is often modeled using transition intensities. A common assumption is that the transition from $k$ to $k+1$ failed components depends on both the number of components already failed and those remaining functional. This is expressed as:

$$\lambda_{k,k+1}(x) = r k (N-k)$$

where $r$ is a rate constant. For a large number of subsystems $N$, the fraction of dysfunctional components $f(t) = F(t)/N$ can be approximated by a deterministic logistic growth function:

$$f(t) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-r(t-t_0)}}$$

In this model, the force of mortality $\mu(t)$ is assumed to be proportional to the frailty index $f(t)$. When the system is relatively "young" and the number of failures is small compared to $N$, the denominator remains dominated by the exponential term, leading to an exponential increase in the hazard rate:

$$\mu(t) = \kappa \frac{F(t)}{N} = \kappa f(t) \approx \frac{\kappa}{N} e^{r(t-t_0)}$$

This approximation shows how the reliability framework naturally generates a Gompertz-like mortality curve. As $t$ becomes very large, $f(t)$ approaches 1, which implies that the force of mortality reaches a plateau at $\kappa$. This reflects the biological reality that mortality rates often decelerate at extreme old ages rather than continuing an infinite exponential climb.

## The Vitality Approach: Depleting Survival Capacity

The vitality approach takes a different perspective, focusing on an individual's latent survival capacity, denoted as $V(t)$. Death occurs when this capacity is exhausted. The dynamics of vitality can be modeled as a stochastic process influenced by deterministic depletion, random fluctuations (diffusion), and sudden shocks (jumps):

$$V(t) = V_0 - \int_0^t \mu_{x_0}(s) ds - \sigma W(t) - \sum_{i=1}^{N(t)} Z_i$$

In this equation, $V_0$ is the initial vitality at birth or a baseline age. The term $\int_0^t \mu_{x_0}(s) ds$ represents the cumulative deterministic loss of vitality due to aging. The diffusion component $\sigma W(t)$ captures individual-specific health fluctuations, while $\sum Z_i$ accounts for sudden, catastrophic losses in health from external events.

To recover the Gompertz law within this framework, one can assume a population where initial vitality $V_0$ follows an exponential distribution $V_0 \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ and depletion is purely deterministic. If the depletion rate is set to follow the Gompertz form $\mu_{x_0}(t) = b^v c_v^t$, then the survival probability—the probability that $V(t) > 0$—matches the Gompertz survival function. 

Unlike the reliability model, which focuses on the accumulation of discrete failures, the vitality model emphasizes the continuous erosion of a survival buffer. This buffer allows for the integration of "intrinsic" risks (the diffusion process) and "extrinsic" risks (the jump process), providing a flexible way to account for various causes of death.

## Comparing the Two Pathways to Gompertz Mortality

While both models can generate the Gompertz force of mortality $\mu(x) = b c^x$, they respond differently to population heterogeneity. In the vitality model, the distribution of initial vitality $V_0$ is a primary driver of the mortality curve's shape. If $V_0$ follows a heavy-tailed distribution like the Pareto distribution, the model produces a significant mortality plateau at older ages because the surviving population is increasingly composed of "high-vitality" individuals.

The reliability model, however, is much less sensitive to the distribution of initial frailty $F_0$. Because the logistic growth of failures is driven by the internal interdependence of the subsystems, the exponential acceleration of failures quickly overrides the initial state. This suggests that the reliability approach views aging as a more "canalized" process where the systemic structure dominates individual starting conditions.

The research also extends both models to incorporate the Makeham law:

$$\mu(x) = \beta + b c^x$$

where $\beta$ represents age-independent mortality (e.g., accidents). In the vitality framework, $\beta$ can be interpreted as the intensity of a fatal jump process (an extrinsic risk). In the reliability framework, it is typically added as an external hazard. Calibration to Canadian female mortality data shows that adding this constant term significantly improves the fit for both models at younger ages (30–45), where the standard Gompertz law often underestimates mortality.

## Empirical Performance and Late-Life Mortality Plateaus

Using 2019 mortality data for Canadian females from the Human Mortality Database, the researchers compared the Gompertz law, the reliability model, and the vitality model. Both mechanistic models outperformed the standard Gompertz law in terms of Relative Squared Error (RSE). 

Specifically, the mechanistic models were better at capturing the deceleration of mortality at very old ages. While the Gompertz law assumes mortality continues to rise exponentially forever, the reliability model’s logistic structure and the vitality model’s selection effect (where only the strongest survive to old age) naturally predict a leveling off.

Interestingly, despite their different mathematical formulations, the calibrated parameters for the reliability and vitality models were quite similar. For instance, the growth parameters $c$ (representing the rate of aging) were nearly identical between the two. This suggests a form of "equifinality" where different biological assumptions lead to the same observable demographic outcomes.

## Biological Age and Subjective Survival Beliefs

One of the most practical applications of these models is the calculation of biological age (BA). Unlike chronological age (CA), which simply counts years passed, BA reflects an individual's actual physiological state or remaining life expectancy. The paper explores three ways to define BA:

1.  **Health-Matching ($BA^{\text{hm}}$):** Matching an individual's current health (vitality or frailty) to the population average. If an individual has the vitality of an average 60-year-old, their $BA^{\text{hm}}$ is 60, regardless of their CA.
    $$BA^{\text{hm}}(t) = m^{-1}(V_i(t))$$
2.  **Age-Shifting ($BA^{\text{as}}$):** Adjusting CA based on the difference between an individual’s expected remaining life $e_{i,t}$ and the population average $e_t$.
    $$BA^{\text{as}}(t) = t + (e_t - e_{i,t})$$
3.  **Lifetime-Matching:** Finding the age at which the population's average remaining lifetime matches the individual's expected remaining lifetime.

![figure-11-biological-age-vs-chronological-age-under-the-vitality-model-the-left-panel-shows-the-health-matching-method-and-the-right-panel-shows-the-age-shifting-method](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.14402v1/Figure_11.png)

The analysis reveals that the reliability model provides a more consistent framework for BA. In the reliability model, health-matching and lifetime-matching are theoretically equivalent. In the vitality model, however, $BA$ definitions can become problematic at extreme ages or for exceptionally healthy individuals, sometimes resulting in undefined values or hard upper bounds.

Finally, the paper addresses subjective survival beliefs. It is well-documented that people tend to underestimate their chances of survival at younger ages and overestimate them at older ages. The authors show that these biases can be explained through the lens of mechanistic models: if individuals use "wrong" parameters for their own vitality depletion or subsystem failure rates, their subjective survival curves will deviate from objective ones in exactly the patterns observed in behavioral studies.

## Conclusion

By bridging reliability theory and vitality modeling, this research offers a comprehensive look at the mechanisms of human aging. The reliability approach emphasizes the systemic, structural nature of decline, while the vitality approach highlights the depletion of a survival buffer. Both provide superior fits to empirical mortality data compared to traditional actuarial laws and offer robust ways to quantify biological age and population heterogeneity. For actuaries and demographers, these mechanistic models provide a path toward more accurate longevity risk management and a deeper understanding of the biological reality behind the numbers.
