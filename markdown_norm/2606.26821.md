## Context: The Evolution of Cosmic Mapping

Astronomical surveys rely on spectroscopy to measure the redshift of light from distant galaxies. These measurements allow researchers to determine the distance of celestial objects and calculate the expansion rate of the universe. Historically, fiber-fed Multi-Object Spectroscopy (MOS) required labor-intensive manual placement. For instance, the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) used pre-drilled plates into which optical fibers were hand-inserted for every exposure.

The development of robotic fiber positioners automated this process, allowing for the rapid reconfiguration of a telescope's focal plane. Current state-of-the-art instruments like the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) utilize 5,000 robotic positioners. These robots are typically designed as two-stage Selective Compliance Assembly Robot Arms (SCARA), featuring two angular degrees of freedom ($\alpha$ and $\beta$) that allow a fiber to be placed anywhere within a specific patrol radius.

![robotic-positioner-architecture](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.26821v1/x1.png)
*{fig:robotic-positioner-architecture}: Architecture of robotic fiber positioners showing the primary and secondary rotational axes and the arrangement within a Trillium raft.*

While DESI has been highly successful, contributing to refined measurements of the Hubble constant ($H_0 = (68.52 \pm 0.62) \text{ km s}^{-1} \text{ Mpc}^{-1}$) and matter density ($\Omega_m$), it also revealed a critical mechanical vulnerability. Approximately 15% of DESI's positioners experienced performance degradation after installation. Investigation revealed that small gear fractures occurred because the positioners were not adequately tested under the changing gravity vectors they encounter as the telescope tracks objects across the sky. This experience highlights the necessity of orientation-dependent testing during the development of next-generation instruments.

## Future Survey Requirements and the Gravity Problem

Future Stage V surveys, such as the MUltiplexed Survey Telescope (MUST), the Spectroscopic Stage-5 Experiment (Spec-S5), and the Wide-field Spectroscopic Telescope (WST), aim to observe hundreds of millions of spectra at high redshifts ($z > 2$). To achieve this, focal planes must be packed with 20,000 to 30,000 miniaturized positioners. These projects demand unprecedented mechanical precision:

*   **XY Placement Stability:** Deviation of less than $1 \mu\text{m}$ over a 30-minute tracking period.
*   **Focus Stability:** Displacement along the optical axis (defocus) of less than $5 \mu\text{m}$.
*   **Tilt Consistency:** Variation in the fiber axis tilt of less than $0.4^\circ$.

The primary challenge is that a positioner's internal components—gears, bearings, and arms—respond differently to gravity as the telescope moves from the zenith ($87^\circ$ altitude) toward the horizon ($0^\circ$ altitude). Gravitational loads can cause mechanical flexure, shifting the fiber tip away from the intended target. This work addresses the need for a testing environment that can simulate these operational conditions before full-scale deployment.

## The Automated Telescope Simulator Test Stand

To characterize these robots, researchers developed a dedicated test stand capable of replicating the telescope's movement. The system is built around an iOptron CEM120 equatorial mount, which provides automated control over the orientation of the Device Under Test (DUT) via a serial protocol.

![automated-test-stand-setup](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.26821v1/x2.jpg)
*{fig:automated-test-stand-setup}: The automated test stand featuring the equatorial mount and the dark box enclosure containing the metrology system.*

The metrology system consists of a high-resolution Basler camera and a 35 mm lens housed within a custom dark box for stray light shielding. The fibers within the positioners are back-illuminated by LEDs, appearing as bright spots to the camera. The setup also includes:

*   **Fixed Reference Fibers:** Four stationary fibers surrounding the DUT to provide stable reference points over a large scale.
*   **Fiducial Markers:** A coated glass block with etched 10 $\mu\text{m}$ spots to serve as a high-precision coordinate origin.
*   **Environmental Sensors:** Probes for temperature, humidity, and orientation (via accelerometer) to monitor conditions that might affect mechanical stability or induce thermal distortions.

## Metrology Optimizations: Noise and Turbulence

Achieving sub-micrometer precision requires overcoming two main physical limitations: shot noise and thermal air turbulence.

### Shot-Noise Reduction
Statistical uncertainty in the detected spot positions, $\sigma_{\mu_k}$, is limited by the number of photons captured by the camera sensor. To minimize this, the system employs image stacking. By averaging $N_{stack} = 50$ images, the researchers created a low-pass filter for fluctuations, effectively reducing the position uncertainty from $1.18 \mu\text{m}$ down to a noise floor of approximately $0.20 \mu\text{m}$.

$$\sigma_{\mu_k} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{stack}}}$$

![impact-of-image-stacking](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.26821v1/x8.png)
*{fig:impact-of-image-stacking}: Scatter plots showing the reduction in measurement uncertainty as the number of stacked images increases.*

### Mitigating Thermal Turbulence
The camera itself acts as a heat source within the enclosed dark box. When the mount is inverted ($\theta = -90^\circ$), rising warm air creates convective flow, which distorts the optical path and causes significant spot jitter. To solve this, researchers installed transparent acrylic (PMMA) sheets between the camera and the fibers. This effectively divided the air volume and suppressed convective turbulence, reducing position scatter at steep angles from $7.74 \mu\text{m}$ to $1.35 \mu\text{m}$.

## Calibration and the 7-Parameter Model

Because the dark box and mounting hardware flex under their own weight as the orientation changes, the mapping from the fiber's physical location to the camera's pixel space is not static. The researchers developed a 7-parameter calibration model to correct these distortions in every acquired image. The model accounts for the following variables:

*   Scale factor ($A(t)$) and optical distance ($D(t)$).
*   Rotations around the three principal axes ($\gamma_x(t), \gamma_y(t), \gamma_z(t)$).
*   Translational offsets ($x_0(t), y_0(t)$).

These parameters are calculated by comparing the observed positions of the five fixed reference spots to their known physical locations using a minimization routine. This calibration allows the system to compensate for enclosure deformation and thermal drift, achieving a final measurement uncertainty of approximately $0.5 \mu\text{m}$ during a standard tracking window.

## Testing the Orbray-6 Prototype

The researchers used this platform to test an Orbray-6 prototype module, which contains two "Trillium rafts" (groups of three coupled positioners each). The results highlighted significant challenges in meeting the stability requirements for future Stage V surveys.

### Observed Drifts
When tracking from the zenith to the horizon, the fibers exhibited substantial movement even while the positioner arms were kept static. In the X-direction, drifts were approximately $10 \mu\text{m}$. In the Y-direction, one group of positioners showed drifts as high as $80 \mu\text{m}$, characterized by a significant shift when the mount was oriented near the zenith.

### Analysis of Compliance
The project compared the maximum deviation within a 30-minute window (a $7.6^\circ$ tracking arc) against the $1 \mu\text{m}$ MUST requirement. Key findings include:

*   **Failure to Meet Specs:** Every positioner exceeded the $1 \mu\text{m}$ threshold in at least some orientations, with some reaching drifts of $20 \mu\text{m}$ near the zenith.
*   **Correlated Behavior:** Positioners within the same Trillium raft moved in a correlated fashion. This suggests that the instability may be systemic to the raft's mounting structure or common drive components rather than the individual robot arms.
*   **Axial Coupling:** The researchers noted that large apparent lateral drifts in the XY plane might actually be caused by displacements along the optical axis ($\Delta z$). A $9 \mu\text{m}$ shift in the focus direction could appear as a $1 \mu\text{m}$ lateral drift due to the geometry of the optical system. The observed $10 \mu\text{m}$ drifts suggest potential defocusing of over $100 \mu\text{m}$, which would severely violate the $5 \mu\text{m}$ focus requirement.

## Significance for Future Cosmological Research

The ability to identify these mechanical instabilities in a laboratory setting—rather than after installation on a telescope—is vital for the success of future astronomical surveys. The test stand developed in this study provides a high-precision tool for the iterative design of robotic positioners, allowing engineers to visualize exactly how components flex or slip under varying gravitational loads. 

By ensuring that these robots can maintain sub-micrometer stability during the long exposures required to capture light from the distant universe, researchers can minimize data loss and maximize the scientific output of next-generation instruments. This work establishes a foundation for more comprehensive testing, including direct measurements of focus and tilt, providing the validation necessary to deploy the complex robotic focal planes that will map the three-dimensional structure of our cosmos.
