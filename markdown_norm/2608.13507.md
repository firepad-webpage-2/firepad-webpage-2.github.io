## Introduction to Safety-Critical Quadrotor Coordination

The deployment of Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) teams is expanding across diverse sectors, including environmental surveillance, infrastructure inspection, and cooperative sensing. In these multi-agent scenarios, the primary challenge is to ensure that every vehicle reaches its destination while maintaining a strictly safe distance from its neighbors and static obstacles. This requirement is often termed "safety-critical control," where safety constraints must be satisfied at all times, even in the presence of external disturbances like wind or model inaccuracies.

Current approaches to this problem often rely on decentralized navigation functions. These functions act as a mathematical map, providing a "force" that guides an agent toward a goal while pushing it away from potential collisions. While theoretically sound, a significant gap exists when applying these methods to real-world quadrotors. Most navigation functions assume the robot can move in any direction instantly (a "fully actuated" system). However, quadrotors are "underactuated": they can only generate thrust along their vertical axis. To move sideways, they must first tilt, creating a delay and a discrepancy between the ideal navigation force and the force the vehicle can actually produce.

This paper addresses this gap by proposing a framework that integrates decentralized navigation functions with High-Order Control Barrier Functions (HOCBFs) and Gaussian Process (GP) learning. By explicitly modeling the underactuation and the uncertainty of the environment, the proposed method provides probabilistic safety guarantees for multi-quadrotor teams.

## The Challenge of Underactuation

To understand the core contribution of this work, one must first consider the configuration of a quadrotor agent $i$, defined by its state $x_i = (p_i, v_i, R_i, \Omega_i)$. Here, $p_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the position, $v_i$ is velocity, $R_i \in \text{SO}(3)$ represents the rotation or attitude, and $\Omega_i$ is the angular velocity. The quadrotor's dynamics are dictated by a thrust $f_i$ applied along its body-fixed vertical axis $R_i e_3$.

In a decentralized navigation framework, a navigation function $\psi_i(x)$ is used to generate a reference force:
$$F_{\text{nav},i}(x) = -k_p \nabla_{p_i} \psi_i(x) - k_v v_i$$
This $F_{\text{nav},i}(x)$ represents the ideal force required to achieve the mission objectives. Because the quadrotor cannot produce an arbitrary force vector in 3D space instantly, it must perform a "thrust-attitude" conversion. It calculates a nominal thrust magnitude $f_{\text{nom},i}$ and a target attitude that aligns its thrust axis with the desired acceleration vector $a_{d,i}(x)$.

A key contribution of this research is the formal quantification of the error introduced by this conversion. The researchers derive an explicit bound that accounts for two main factors:
1.  **Attitude Tracking Error $e_{\text{att},i}$:** The difference between the current orientation and the orientation required to point the thrust in the desired direction.
2.  **Estimation Error $e_{\text{GP},i}$:** The error in predicting external forces (like wind) using the learned model.

By acknowledging that the robot cannot follow the navigation function perfectly, the framework can build safety buffers that account for these specific, measurable inaccuracies.

## Learning Unknown Dynamics with Gaussian Processes

Quadrotors often operate in environments where forces like aerodynamic drag or wind gusts are not fully known beforehand. Traditional control methods might fail or become unsafe if these forces are ignored. This work utilizes Gaussian Process (GP) learning to estimate these unknown force terms $\Delta_{f,i}(x_i)$ online.

A Gaussian Process provides not just a mean prediction $\mu_{f,i}$ of the unknown force, but also a measure of uncertainty. The framework assumes that the learning model provides a high-probability confidence bound $\bar{\Delta}_i(x_i)$. This bound represents the maximum likely error in the force prediction.

The integration of these GP estimates allows the controller to compensate for disturbances. Instead of relying on a purely nominal model, the agent uses the prediction $\mu_{f,i}$ to adjust its thrust and orientation. Crucially, the uncertainty $\bar{\Delta}_i(x_i)$ is not discarded; it is passed to the safety layer to ensure the vehicle remains safe even if the prediction is slightly off.

## Robust High-Order Control Barrier Functions

The mechanism used to enforce safety is the Control Barrier Function (CBF). For quadrotor collision avoidance, the safety constraint is defined by the minimum separation distance $d_{\min}$ between any two agents $i$ and $j$:
$h_{ij}(p_i, p_j) = \|p_i - p_j\|^2 - d_{\min}^2 \geq 0$

In control theory, the "relative degree" of a constraint refers to how many times you must differentiate it before the control input (thrust) appears. For quadrotors, the position depends on acceleration, which depends on thrust. Thus, the safety constraint has a relative degree of two. High-Order Control Barrier Functions (HOCBFs) are specifically designed for such cases. They define a sequence of functions:
$\phi_{0,ij} = h_{ij}$
$\phi_{1,ij} = \dot{h}_{ij} + \alpha_1(h_{ij})$
$\phi_{2,ij} = \dot{\phi}_{1,ij} + \alpha_2(\phi_{1,ij})$

Safety is guaranteed if $\phi_{2,ij} \geq 0$. However, because the true dynamics include unknown forces $\Delta_{f,i}(x_i)$, the controller cannot calculate $\phi_{2,ij}$ exactly. To solve this, the researchers formulate a "robust" HOCBF. They use the GP estimate to calculate a predicted $\hat{\phi}_{2,ij}$ and then subtract a robustness margin $\rho_{ij}$:
$\phi_{2,ij} \geq \hat{\phi}_{2,ij} - \rho_{ij} \geq 0$

The margin $\rho_{ij}$ is derived from the GP confidence bounds $\bar{\Delta}_i(x_i)$. This effectively "tightens" the safety constraint, forcing the quadrotors to stay further apart when the uncertainty in the environment is high.

## The Unified Safety-Critical Framework

The final controller operates as a safety filter. At each time step, the system performs the following:
1.  **Nominal Control:** The decentralized navigation function calculates the desired reference force $F_{\text{nav},i}$.
2.  **Learning:** The GP model provides a mean estimate of external forces and an uncertainty bound.
3.  **Optimization:** An aggregated Quadratic Program (QP) is solved. The objective is to find a set of thrusts $f$ that are as close as possible to the nominal thrusts $f_{\text{nom}}$ while strictly satisfying the robust HOCBF constraints:
$$\min_{f} \frac{1}{2} \|f - f_{\text{nom}}\|^2$$
Subject to: $\hat{\phi}_{2,ij}(x, f) \geq \rho_{ij}(x)$ for all neighbors $j$.

This approach is "minimally invasive." If the nominal navigation force is already safe, the QP will output $f = f_{\text{nom}}$, and the safety filter does nothing. If a collision is predicted, the QP modifies the thrust magnitudes just enough to satisfy the safety boundary.

The paper provides a formal theorem stating that if this QP is feasible, the multi-agent system is guaranteed to remain safe with a probability of $1 - \sum \delta_i$, where $\delta_i$ represents the small probability that the GP error exceeds its bounds.

## Experimental Validation and Findings

The framework was tested in a simulation of six quadrotors performing a "viewpoint exchange" maneuver. This is a complex scenario where multiple agents must cross through a central point to reach opposite goals, creating a high risk of collision. To make the test realistic, a spatially varying wind field was added as an unknown disturbance.

The results highlighted several key findings:
*   **The Necessity of Safety Filtering:** Without the HOCBF-QP safety filter, the quadrotors violated the minimum separation distance $d_{\min}$ because the navigation function alone could not react quickly enough to the combined effects of underactuation and wind.
*   **The Role of Robustness:** The researchers compared their robust QP (with $\rho_{ij}$) against a non-robust version. While the non-robust version stayed just outside $d_{\min}$, it frequently violated the safety "certificates" intended to provide a buffer against uncertainty. The robust version maintained much higher safety margins when uncertainty was high.
*   **Minimal Intervention:** The safety filter only intervened when necessary. During the majority of the flight, the thrust values remained close to the nominal values, showing that the safety layer does not hinder the primary mission objectives.

## Conclusion

This research provides a rigorous bridge between high-level decentralized planning and the low-level physical constraints of quadrotor UAVs. By explicitly modeling the implementation error caused by underactuation and incorporating probabilistic uncertainty from learned models, the framework enables safer multi-robot coordination in unpredictable environments.

The unique contribution of this work lies in the synthesis of three distinct areas: decentralized navigation, learning-based control, and high-order safety certificates. Unlike previous methods that might ignore the "thrust-attitude" lag or assume perfect environmental models, this approach quantifies these discrepancies and builds them directly into the safety logic. This paves the way for more reliable autonomous operations in complex, real-world scenarios where safety cannot be left to chance.
