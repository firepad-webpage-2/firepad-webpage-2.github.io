## Physics-Informed Neural Networks and the Boundary Challenge

Neural network-based solvers for partial differential equations (PDEs), such as Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), have emerged as a prominent alternative to traditional mesh-based methods. These approaches leverage the universal approximation capabilities of neural networks to represent the solution of a PDE. Unlike finite element or finite volume methods that discretize the domain into a grid or mesh, PINNs are typically mesh-free, relying on the evaluation of the PDE residual at various collocation points within the domain. This characteristic makes them particularly attractive for solving problems in high-dimensional spaces or on complex, irregular geometries where generating a high-quality mesh is computationally prohibitive.

However, a fundamental challenge in these methods is the enforcement of essential boundary conditions, such as Dirichlet conditions, where the value of the solution is prescribed on the boundary. Because a generic neural network does not inherently satisfy these constraints, the boundary conditions must be explicitly integrated into the training process. Current strategies generally fall into two categories: penalty-based methods and hard-constraint constructions. Penalty methods add a term to the loss function that penalizes deviations from the boundary data, requiring a delicate balance between interior PDE residuals and boundary errors through hyperparameter tuning. Hard-constraint methods design the network architecture to satisfy the boundary conditions automatically, often using distance functions. However, these distance functions can be difficult to construct for complex geometries.

The BTF-PINN (Boundary-Training-Free Physics-Informed Neural Network) method addresses these limitations by introducing a variational formulation that allows for the enforcement of homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions without any explicit boundary training data, penalty terms, or specialized network architectures. By shifting the focus to a purely interior-based loss function, the method simplifies the optimization process and improves the accuracy of the resulting boundary traces.

## The Core Principle: A Boundary-Free Variational Formulation

The theoretical foundation of BTF-PINN lies in the observation that for certain elliptic problems, the boundary constraint can be embedded directly into a variational functional defined only over the interior of the domain. Consider the classic homogeneous Dirichlet Poisson problem:

$$
-\Delta u = f \text{ in } \Omega, \quad u = 0 \text{ on } \partial\Omega
$$

where $\Omega$ is a bounded domain in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and $\partial\Omega$ is its boundary. Standard PINNs would minimize a loss function consisting of the squared $L^2(\Omega)$ norm of the residual $(\Delta u + f)$ plus a penalty term for the boundary. In contrast, the BTF-PINN approach seeks to minimize a functional $J_C^f(v)$ defined for functions $v$ in the space $V = \{ v \in H^1(\Omega) : \Delta v \in L^2(\Omega) \}$:

$$
J_C^f(v) = C\| \Delta v + f \|_0 + |v|_1
$$

In this expression, $\| \cdot \|_0$ denotes the $L^2(\Omega)$ norm, which measures the interior PDE residual, and $|v|_1$ represents the $H^1(\Omega)$ seminorm (essentially the $L^2$ norm of the gradient $\nabla v$). The constant $C$ is a residual weight that plays a critical role in the enforcement of the boundary condition. 

The paper establishes that if $C$ is chosen to be sufficiently large, specifically greater than the Dirichlet Poincaré constant $C_P$ of the domain $\Omega$, then any minimizer of this functional will be equal to the true solution $u$ of the Dirichlet problem, up to an additive constant $c$. This equivalence is significant because the functional $J_C^f(v)$ does not include any terms involving evaluations on $\partial\Omega$. The minimization of the $H^1$ seminorm acting alongside the weighted residual term implicitly forces the function to satisfy the homogeneous boundary condition.

## The Importance of the Critical Residual Weight

The success of the BTF-PINN method hinges on the selection of the residual weight $C$. The Dirichlet Poincaré constant $C_P$ is defined by the inequality $|z|_1 \leq C_P \| \Delta z \|_0$ for all $z \in H^1_0(\Omega)$, which is the space of functions that already vanish on the boundary. The constant $C_P$ is related to the first Dirichlet eigenvalue $\lambda^D_1(\Omega)$ of the domain through the relationship $C_P = 1/\sqrt{\lambda^D_1(\Omega)}$.

The research proves that $C > C_P$ is a sharp threshold. If $C \leq C_P$, the functional may fail to uniquely identify the Dirichlet solution, as the energy term $|v|_1$ might not be strong enough to "pull" the function toward zero at the boundary against the residual term. In practical applications, the exact value of $C_P$ is often unknown, especially for irregular domains. To address this, the authors utilize the Li–Yau inequality to derive computable upper bounds for $C_P$ based on the volume of the domain, providing a reliable safety margin for choosing $C$.

This mechanism differs fundamentally from traditional penalty methods. In penalty methods, increasing the penalty weight usually improves boundary satisfaction at the cost of interior accuracy or optimization stability (due to gradient imbalance). In BTF-PINN, $C$ acts as a threshold parameter; once it exceeds $C_P$, the boundary condition is theoretically satisfied, and further increasing $C$ primarily shifts the focus toward the PDE residual without the typical negative trade-offs associated with penalty weights.

## Implementation and the Additive Constant

In the BTF-PINN framework, the trial solution is represented by a fully connected neural network $v_\theta$ with parameters $\vartheta$. The training objective is to find $v_\theta^*$ that minimizes the empirical version of the functional $J_C^f$. Because the functional only involves derivatives ($\Delta v$ and $\nabla v$), it is invariant under the addition of a constant. This means the neural network will converge to a solution $u+c$, where $u$ is the exact solution and $c$ is some arbitrary constant determined by the initialization and optimization path.

To recover the exact solution $u$, the additive constant must be removed. During post-training diagnostics, this is typically done by shifting the network output by its mean value or by its value at a specific point, if known. In the context of solving the homogeneous Dirichlet problem, the shifted solution is compared against the ground truth using metrics such as the shifted relative $L^2$ error ($E_{sh}^{L^2}$) and the relative $H^1$-seminorm error ($E_{H^1}$).

The training protocol generally utilizes Adam or AdamW optimizers followed by L-BFGS refinement to reach high precision. Since no boundary points are required for the loss function, the sampling strategy is simplified to generating quadrature points only within the interior of $\Omega$.

## Performance Across Complex Geometries and High Dimensions

A key strength of the BTF-PINN method is its robustness across diverse problem settings. The authors demonstrate its efficacy in several challenging scenarios:

1.  **Irregular Domains:** The method was tested on 2D and 3D domains with complex, "wavy" boundaries defined implicitly by level-set functions. Despite the lack of boundary training points, the method accurately captured the solution, achieving shifted relative $L^2$ errors below $10^{-2}$.
2.  **High-Dimensional Problems:** The method was applied to a 10-dimensional Poisson problem. In high dimensions, the surface-to-volume ratio changes dramatically, making traditional boundary sampling inefficient. BTF-PINN successfully solved the 10D problem by relying solely on interior samples, confirming that the interior-only variational principle remains valid in high dimensions.
3.  **Anisotropic Operators:** The framework was extended to handle general elliptic operators of the form $L_A v = \nabla \cdot (A \nabla v)$, where $A$ is a symmetric positive definite matrix representing material anisotropy. The method successfully solved heat conduction problems with varying conductivity matrices on irregular geometries.

Numerical experiments also verified the sharp threshold condition for $C$. For a range of dimensions, runs where $C$ was set below the theoretical Poincaré constant $C_P$ consistently failed to converge to the correct solution, while those with $C > C_P$ reached high accuracy.

## Significance and Comparison with Existing Methods

The primary contribution of BTF-PINN is the theoretical and practical demonstration that boundary training is not strictly necessary for solving homogeneous Dirichlet problems with neural networks. This leads to several advantages over existing PINN variants:

*   **Hyperparameter Reduction:** It eliminates the need to tune boundary penalty weights, which are often the most sensitive parameters in PINN training.
*   **Geometric Flexibility:** By avoiding distance functions and boundary sampling, the method is easily applied to any domain where interior points can be sampled and the volume is known (to estimate $C_P$).
*   **Superior Boundary Trace Accuracy:** Surprisingly, numerical results show that BTF-PINN often produces more accurate boundary traces than standard PINNs that are explicitly trained on boundary data. For example, in 3D Poisson tests, the shifted $H^{1/2}$ boundary trace error for BTF-PINN was significantly lower than that of the penalty-based baseline.
*   **Potential for Interface Problems:** The method's ability to handle boundary conditions implicitly makes it a strong candidate for hybrid problems. The authors demonstrated this by solving a 4D elliptic interface problem where BTF-PINN handled the outer Dirichlet boundary while explicit constraints were used for the internal interface.

By providing a rigorous Cèa-type convergence analysis, the authors ensure that the method is not just an empirical heuristic but a mathematically sound approach to mesh-free PDE modeling. The BTF-PINN method represents a shift toward more "natural" enforcement of essential boundary conditions, simplifying the implementation of neural solvers while maintaining high fidelity in both the interior and the boundary of the domain.
