## Introduction to Continued Fractions and Dimension Spectra

Every irrational number $x \in [0, 1]$ can be uniquely represented by an infinite continued fraction of the form:

$$
x = [a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots] = \frac{1}{a_1 + \frac{1}{a_2 + \frac{1}{a_3 + \dots}}}
$$

where each $a_i$ is a positive integer, known as a coefficient or digit. When we restrict these coefficients to a specific subset $\Lambda \subseteq \mathbb{N}$, we form a set of numbers $J_{\Lambda} := \{x = [a_1, a_2, \dots] : a_i \in \Lambda\}$. In the study of fractal geometry, a central question is the "size" of such sets, typically measured by the Hausdorff dimension, denoted $\text{dim}_H(J_{\Lambda})$.

The "dimension spectrum" of a set $\Lambda$ is the collection of all possible Hausdorff dimensions that can be obtained by taking subsets of $\Lambda$. Formally, this is defined as $\text{DS}(\Lambda) = \{\text{dim}_H(J_A) : A \subseteq \Lambda\}$. One of the most famous results in this field resolved the "Texan conjecture," proving that if we consider all natural numbers, the dimension spectrum is the entire unit interval: $\text{DS}(\mathbb{N}) = [0, 1]$.

In recent years, researchers have investigated whether this "fullness" of the spectrum (i.e., whether the spectrum is a complete interval $[0, \text{dim}_H(J_{\Lambda})]$) holds for other subsets of natural numbers, such as primes or squares. A significant finding by Chitanga, Lemmens, and Nussbaum established that if a set $\Lambda = \{a_1, a_2, \dots\}$ satisfies the multiplicative growth condition $a_n a_m \ge a_{n+m}$ for all $n, m \in \mathbb{N}$, then the dimension spectrum is guaranteed to be full. However, many interesting sequences in number theory do not satisfy this condition, leading researchers to wonder if gaps appear in their spectra.

This paper by Arpit Dawar and Amit Priyadarshi explores this question for the generalized Fibonacci sequence.

## The Generalized Fibonacci Sequence

The generalized Fibonacci sequence $F_{(a_1, a_2)}$ is defined by two initial positive integers $a_1 < a_2$ and the recurrence relation:

$$
a_n = a_{n-1} + a_{n-2} \text{ for } n \ge 3
$$

These sequences are of particular interest because they frequently violate the multiplicative growth condition mentioned above. For example, the standard Fibonacci sequence $F_{(1, 2)} = \{1, 2, 3, 5, 8, \dots\}$ fails the condition because $a_1 a_2 = 1 \times 2 = 2 < a_3 = 3$. Because this sequence grows "too fast" relative to its product, the existing general theorems cannot determine whether its dimension spectrum is a full interval. The authors set out to characterize the spectrum for these sequences, identifying which ones are full and which ones contain gaps.

## Dynamics and the Perron-Frobenius Operator

To calculate the Hausdorff dimension of these sets, the authors use the theory of Infinite Conformal Iterated Function Systems (CIFS). Each coefficient $a_i$ in the set $\Lambda$ corresponds to a contraction mapping $\phi_i(x) = \frac{1}{a_i + x}$ on the interval $[0, 1]$. The set $J_{\Lambda}$ is the "limit set" of these infinitely many maps.

A fundamental tool in this analysis is the Perron-Frobenius operator, $L_{s, \Lambda}$. For a given real number $s \ge 0$, the operator acts on a space of functions $f$ as follows:

$$
(L_{s, \Lambda} f)(x) = \sum_{i \in I} \left( \frac{1}{a_i + x} \right)^{2s} f\left( \frac{1}{a_i + x} \right)
$$

The Hausdorff dimension is deeply connected to the spectral properties of this operator. Specifically, Bowen's Formula states that the Hausdorff dimension $s = \text{dim}_H(J_{\Lambda})$ is the unique value for which the spectral radius of the operator, $r(L_{s, \Lambda})$, is exactly equal to 1. By analyzing how $r(L_{s, \Lambda})$ changes as we add or remove elements from the coefficient set $\Lambda$, the authors can determine the range of possible dimensions.

## Identifying Full Dimension Spectra

The first major finding of the paper is that for most generalized Fibonacci sequences, the dimension spectrum is indeed a full interval $[0, \text{dim}_H(J_{F_{(a_1, a_2)}})]$. The authors prove this for two broad categories:

1.  **When the first term is at least 2:** If $a_1 \ge 2$, the sequence always results in a full dimension spectrum.
2.  **When the first term is 1, but the second is at least 3:** If $a_1 = 1$ and $a_2 \ge 3$, the spectrum is also full.

The proof relies on a "strict break point" argument. The authors show that for any target dimension $s$ in the desired range, one can construct a subset of the Fibonacci sequence that yields exactly that dimension. This is done by starting with a finite set of digits and then "filling in" the dimension using the infinite tail of the sequence.

The challenge in these proofs is that when $a_1 = 1$, the map $\phi_1(x) = \frac{1}{1+x}$ is not a strong contraction (its derivative at $x=0$ is $-1$). To handle this, the authors analyze the "second-level" maps—compositions of two mappings—which ensures the necessary convergence properties for the operator theory to work. By combining these analytical bounds with numerical estimates, they show that for these sequences, the "gaps" that might have been caused by the fast growth of Fibonacci numbers are effectively "closed" by the density of the tail of the sequence.

## Discovering Gaps in the Spectrum

The most surprising result of the paper is the discovery of gaps in the dimension spectrum of the standard Fibonacci sequence and the Lucas numbers. 

### The Fibonacci Case $F_{(1, 2)}$
For the sequence $F_{(1, 2)} = \{1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, \dots\}$, the authors prove that the spectrum $\text{DS}(F_{(1, 2)})$ is **not** a full interval. Specifically, they identify a gap around the digit 8.

The logic for finding a gap involves comparing two specific subsets. If we can find a digit $a_k$ such that the dimension of the entire set excluding that digit is strictly less than the dimension of the finite set of digits up to that digit, a gap must exist. Mathematically, a gap exists if:

$$
\text{dim}_H(J_{F \setminus \{a_k\}}) < \text{dim}_H(J_{\{a_1, \dots, a_k\}})
$$

Using rigorous numerical methods, the authors find:
*   $\text{dim}_H(J_{\{1, 2, 3, 5, 8\}}) \ge 0.7986$
*   $\text{dim}_H(J_{F_{(1, 2)} \setminus \{8\}}) \le 0.7976$

Because the dimension of the set without 8 cannot "reach" the dimension of the small finite set containing 8, there is a range of values between 0.7976 and 0.7986 that can never be realized as the Hausdorff dimension of any subset of the Fibonacci numbers.

### The Lucas Case $F_{(2, 1)}$
A similar phenomenon occurs with the Lucas numbers (starting $2, 1, 3, 4, 7, \dots$). By reordering and analyzing the digits, the authors find a gap centered around the digit 4. They calculate:
*   $\text{dim}_H(J_{\{1, 2, 3, 4\}}) \ge 0.7889$
*   $\text{dim}_H(J_{F_{(2, 1)} \setminus \{4\}}) \le 0.7867$

In both cases, the authors also show that while the spectrum is not full, it still contains a non-trivial interval $[0, s]$ for some $s$. This means the spectrum consists of a solid interval at the bottom, followed by a more complex, "broken" structure at higher dimensions.

## Methodological Significance: Theory and Computation

This research is notable for how it bridges abstract operator theory and precise numerical computation. 

To prove the existence of gaps, the authors could not rely on qualitative descriptions; they needed highly accurate bounds on the Hausdorff dimension. They utilized numerical algorithms developed by Falk and Nussbaum, which calculate the spectral radius of the Perron-Frobenius operator by approximating its eigenfunctions. 

Furthermore, to bound the dimension of sets with infinite tails (like $J_{F \setminus \{8\}}$), the authors used the following approximation formula:
If $A \subset \Lambda$ and $\Lambda \setminus A$ is a "tail" of the sequence, the difference in dimension can be bounded by:

$$
\text{dim}_H(J_{\Lambda}) - \text{dim}_H(J_A) \approx \frac{r(L_{s, \Lambda \setminus A})}{\chi(S_{\Lambda})}
$$

where $\chi(S_{\Lambda})$ is the Lyapunov exponent, representing the average rate of contraction of the mappings. By combining these estimates, they could rigorously bound the dimension of infinite sets within very narrow margins (often to four decimal places), providing the evidence needed to confirm the existence of gaps.

## Conclusion and Research Impact

The paper "Dimension Spectrum of Continued Fraction Expansions with Coefficients Restricted to the Fibonacci Sequence" provides a detailed map of the dimensional properties of one of the most famous sequences in mathematics. It demonstrates that the "fullness" of a dimension spectrum is a delicate property that depends on the balance between the initial digits and the growth rate of the sequence tail.

The study achieves three main things:
1.  It extends the theory of dimension spectra to sequences that do not satisfy standard multiplicative growth conditions.
2.  It identifies specific Fibonacci-type sequences that possess full spectra, providing a new set of examples for this phenomenon.
3.  It proves that even very "natural" sequences like the standard Fibonacci and Lucas numbers can have gaps in their dimension spectra, counter-intuitively suggesting that these sets are "missing" certain sizes.

This work refines our understanding of the fractal structure of numbers and sets a precedent for using high-precision numerical analysis to solve theoretical problems in dynamical systems and number theory. It suggests that the "Texan conjecture" style of full intervals is not the universal rule for structured subsets of the natural numbers, and that the interplay between specific small digits and the infinite tail can create complex, gapped spectral structures.
