## The Challenge of Informational Efficiency in Football Forecasting

In the realm of association football analytics, the pursuit of a model that can "beat the market" is a common theme. Many studies report high accuracy figures, comparing their sophisticated machine learning or statistical models against simple baselines like uniform guessing. However, this approach often overlooks the most significant hurdle in sports forecasting: the betting market itself. The market's closing price—the final odds offered before a match begins—is an aggregation of vast amounts of data, expert opinion, and financial stakes.

Recent research addresses a critical methodological gap in this field: determining whether a statistical model adds "incremental information" to the information already embedded in market prices. Rather than asking if a model is "accurate," the focus shifts to whether it contains any signal that the market has missed. If a model's predictions, when combined with market odds, do not improve the overall forecast, the model is considered redundant for outcome prediction, regardless of its individual accuracy score.

## The Structural Modeling Framework: Dixon–Coles

The primary model examined in this study is based on the Dixon and Coles framework, a cornerstone of football analytics since the late 1990s. The model treats the number of goals scored by the home team ($X$) and the away team ($Y$) as independent Poisson variables. The expected goal rates for a match between home club $h$ and away club $a$ are determined by club-specific attack ($\alpha$) and defense ($\delta$) ratings, along with a home advantage parameter ($\gamma$).

The log-linear rates are expressed as:

$$\log \lambda = \gamma + \alpha_h - \delta_a$$
$$\log \mu = \alpha_a - \delta_h$$

To ensure the model is mathematically identifiable, a constraint is placed on the attack ratings such that $\sum_j \alpha_j = 0$. This ensures that the ratings are relative to a league average.

However, the assumption that home and away goals are perfectly independent often fails for low-scoring outcomes. The model applies a correction factor, $\tau_{\rho}(x,y)$, to the joint probability mass for scores like 0-0, 1-0, 0-1, and 1-1 to better reflect observed frequencies. 

Furthermore, team strengths are not static. To account for this, the model uses an exponential time-weighting scheme. Matches are weighted by their age $\Delta t$ (days before the forecast) using a decay function:

$$w(\Delta t) = \exp(-\xi\Delta t)$$

Validation experiments identified an optimal decay rate of $\xi = 0.002$ per day, implying that the relevance of a match result halves approximately every 347 days. This allows the model to adapt to transfers, managerial changes, and fluctuations in team form.

## Establishing a Rigorous Benchmark: Margin Removal

Comparing a model directly to quoted bookmaker odds is problematic because those odds include a "margin" or "vig"—the bookmaker's commission. To derive a true probability forecast from the market, this margin must be removed. 

The research utilizes the Shin (1993) model for this purpose. Unlike simpler methods that just normalize the odds, the Shin model assumes the existence of "insider traders" who possess superior information. It solves for a parameter $z$ that represents the proportion of such traders in the market. By accounting for the way bookmakers adjust prices to mitigate the risk from insiders, the Shin model provides a more theoretically grounded estimate of the market's "true" implied probabilities. The resulting de-vigged market price $p_M$ serves as the primary benchmark for comparison.

## Testing for Incremental Information

The core contribution of this work is the application of a formal test for incremental information using a logarithmic opinion pool. Instead of looking at the model and the market in isolation, it combines them into a single probabilistic forecast $p^{(w)}_i$:

$$p^{(w)}_i \propto (p_{M,i})^{1-w} (p_{S,i})^w$$

Here, $w$ is a weight between 0 and 1 assigned to the structural model ($p_S$), while $1-w$ is assigned to the market ($p_M$). If the optimal weight $\hat{w}$—found by minimizing the out-of-sample log loss—is zero, it implies that adding the structural model's predictions to the market's predictions only serves to degrade the forecast. In such a case, the model provides no incremental information.

This diagnostic tool is more rigorous than simple accuracy comparisons because it forces the model to compete directly for a share of the predictive "voice." It avoids the common pitfall of crediting a model for information it shares with the market, focusing only on the unique signal it brings to the table.

## Findings: The Informational Ceiling

The results across nineteen seasons of Serie A (7,220 matches) were decisive. In the head-to-head comparison on the test set, the market achieved a mean Ranked Probability Score (RPS) of 0.1905, significantly outperforming the structural model's 0.1972. 

![rps-comparison-across-seasons](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11505v1/img-2.jpeg)
*{fig:rps-comparison-across-seasons}: The mean RPS for the market, the goals-based model, and the shots-based model across the test seasons. The market (blue) consistently maintains the lowest (best) error rate.*

When the logarithmic opinion pool was applied, the fitted weight $w$ for the structural model was 0.000. Tracing the log-loss profile confirmed that any positive weight assigned to the model increased the overall error. Even when a "process-based" variant was created—using shots on target as a proxy for chance creation to reduce the "noise" inherent in goals—the result remained the same. While the shots-based model added information to the goals-based model, it added nothing to the market. This suggests that the betting market is highly efficient, absorbing not just historical goal data but also more granular performance metrics like shots on target.

## Calibration vs. Discrimination

One of the more nuanced findings of the study involves the distinction between calibration and discrimination.
*   **Discrimination** (or sharpness) is the ability of a model to distinguish between different outcomes, assigning higher probabilities to events that actually occur. The market excels here.
*   **Calibration** (or reliability) is the degree to which a model's stated probabilities match long-term frequencies. For example, if a model says a team has a 70% chance of winning, they should win 70 out of 100 such matches.

Interestingly, the research found that the structural model was often better calibrated than the market. The market tended to be slightly under-confident (a calibration slope > 1), whereas the structural model's probabilities were closer to the ideal 1:1 relationship with observed frequencies.

![calibration-curve](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11505v1/img-4.jpeg)
*{fig:calibration-curve}: Reliability curves for the market and the structural model. While the market is sharper, the structural model often exhibits better calibration, meaning its probabilities can be more "trusted" in absolute terms.*

This finding is significant: even if a model cannot beat the market in a betting context, its well-calibrated nature makes it valuable for other types of decision-making where accurate absolute probabilities are required.

## Applied Utility: Match Leverage

If a model cannot provide incremental information for betting, what is its value to a football club? The paper introduces the concept of "match leverage" as a strategic tool for clubs. 

Match leverage $L_m$ for a specific fixture $m$ is defined as the difference in the probability of a club achieving its season-long objective $\Omega$ (such as avoiding relegation or qualifying for Europe) depending on the outcome of that match:

$$L_m = P(\Omega | \text{club wins m}) - P(\Omega | \text{club loses m})$$

Using the calibrated probabilities from the structural model, researchers can simulate the remainder of a season thousands of times. By observing how survival or qualification rates change based on the result of fixture $m$, they can quantify the fixture's importance. 

In a case study of ACF Fiorentina's survival objective, the research found that matches against direct relegation rivals (e.g., Lecce) carried more than double the leverage of high-profile matches against league leaders (e.g., Inter Milan). This provides actionable intelligence for managers: if a key player must be rested, it is strategically "cheaper" to do so in a low-leverage match against a giant than in a high-leverage "six-pointer" against a rival, even if the former is more prestigious.

## Conclusion

This research demonstrates that for top-tier European football, the informational barrier set by the betting market is extremely high. Sophisticated structural models based on public statistics like goals and shots on target appear to offer no incremental predictive power over the closing price. However, the study also highlights that a model's utility is not binary. While the market may be "sharper," statistical models can provide better calibration and unique strategic metrics like match leverage. By shifting the focus from "beating the market" to "augmenting decision-making," the research provides a more realistic and productive path forward for sports analytics.
