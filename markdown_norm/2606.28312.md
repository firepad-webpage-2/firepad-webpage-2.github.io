## The Challenge of Regional Aviation Decarbonization

The global aviation sector is responsible for approximately $2.5\%$ of worldwide carbon emissions. While international long-haul flights often dominate the conversation surrounding decarbonization, regional and domestic aviation presents a more immediate opportunity for the deployment of sustainable technologies. In Canada, the government has set a target of achieving net-zero greenhouse gas emissions by 2050, as detailed in its Aviation Climate Action Plan. Because more than half of Canada's domestic flights cover distances of less than one hour, the country is uniquely positioned to serve as a testing ground for electric aircraft (EA).

Electric aircraft offer a path toward zero-emission flight, but they face significant technical hurdles. The most prominent constraint is the energy density of current battery technology, which is far lower than that of conventional jet fuel. This limitation restricts EAs to shorter ranges and smaller passenger capacities compared to traditional turboprops or helicopters. Consequently, transitioning a fleet from conventional internal combustion engines to electric propulsion is not a simple one-to-one replacement. It requires a strategic redesign of airport infrastructure, flight schedules, and fleet management.

## Integrated Planning: Beyond Fragmented Optimization

Existing research in the field of electric aviation has often been siloed. Some studies focus exclusively on the placement of charging stations, treating the flight schedule as a fixed input. Others focus on battery degradation models or the optimal dispatch of energy without considering the long-term capital investments required for fleet renewal. This paper addresses these gaps by proposing an integrated planning framework.

The authors argue that fleet composition, airport infrastructure, and service allocation are deeply interdependent. For instance, the decision to electrify a specific route depends on whether the destination airport has charging capabilities. In turn, the installation of chargers depends on the expected volume of electric flights, which is dictated by the availability of electric aircraft in the fleet. By treating these variables as joint decisions rather than independent parameters, the research provides a holistic view of the transition process over a multi-year planning horizon. This approach helps avoid suboptimal outcomes, such as investing in infrastructure for routes that are not yet prioritized for electrification or purchasing aircraft that cannot be effectively supported by existing ground facilities.

## A Mathematical Framework for Transition Strategy

To solve this multi-dimensional problem, the researchers developed a multi-period Mixed-Integer Linear Programming (MILP) model. This model is designed to minimize the total system cost over a specific planning horizon, typically several years. The objective function, which represents the goal of the optimization, accounts for several cost categories:

$$TC = \sum_{t \in T} (C_{inv, t} + C_{fixed, t} + C_{op, t} + C_{elec, t} + C_{bat, t} + C_{pen, t})$$

In this equation, $TC$ is the total cost, and $t$ represents each year in the planning horizon $T$. The components include capital investments $C_{inv}$, fixed fleet costs $C_{fixed}$, operating costs $C_{op}$, electricity costs $C_{elec}$, battery degradation costs $C_{bat}$, and penalties for unmet demand $C_{pen}$.

The model manages a variety of decision variables:
- $x_{t}^{ea}$ and $x_{t}^{ca}$: The number of electric and conventional aircraft purchased or retired in year $t$.
- $y_{at}$: A binary variable (0 or 1) indicating whether airport $a$ is electrified in year $t$.
- $c_{at}$: The number of chargers installed at airport $a$.
- $f_{it}^{ea}$ and $f_{it}^{ca}$: The number of electric and conventional flights operated on itinerary $i$ during year $t$.

The framework is subject to several constraints. Policy constraints include a minimum share of demand served by electric operations, denoted as $\alpha_t$, and a maximum allowable greenhouse gas emission level, denoted as $\rho_t$. Operational constraints ensure that the number of flights does not exceed the available aircraft hours and that the energy required for charging does not exceed the capacity of the installed infrastructure.

## The Case Study: Helijet in British Columbia

To test the model in a real-world scenario, the authors applied it to Helijet International, a helicopter operator in British Columbia. Helijet operates high-frequency routes between Vancouver Harbour (CXH), Victoria Harbour (YWH), and Nanaimo Harbour (ZNA). These routes are ideal for electrification because of their short distances (64 km to 96 km) and the high demand for rapid regional transit.

The study compares the existing fleet of 12-seat Sikorsky S-76 helicopters with the Beta Technologies ALIA A250, a 5-passenger electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) aircraft. The ALIA A250 has a range of approximately 460 km and a 225 kWh battery. While the electric aircraft has zero direct emissions and lower operating costs per hour, its smaller capacity (5 seats vs. 12 seats) presents a significant challenge for meeting current passenger demand. The model's task is to determine how to phase in these 5-seat electric units while phasing out the 12-seat helicopters without compromising service quality or exceeding budgets.

## Identifying the Real Bottlenecks

One of the most significant findings of this research is that the primary obstacle to electrification is not infrastructure, but rather fleet capacity and operational structure. In many discussions about electric vehicles, the "charging desert" is cited as the main deterrent. However, in the context of regional aviation, the model found that a single high-power charger (320 kW) per hub was sufficient to support all electric operations throughout the study period.

The real bottleneck identified was the capacity mismatch. Because the electric ALIA A250 carries only 5 passengers compared to the 12-seat S-76, replacing aircraft on a one-to-one basis leads to a severe shortage of seats. As shown in {fig:breakdown-of-passenger-demand-showing-the-share-served-by-electric-vs-conventional-flights-and-the-volume-of-unmet-demand}, unmet demand reaches nearly $37\%$ by 2030. This suggests that for electric aviation to be successful, operators must either significantly increase flight frequency or wait for larger-capacity electric aircraft to become available. In the current model, the cost of adding enough extra flights to cover the capacity gap was higher than the penalty for unmet demand, leading the optimization to accept a lower level of service.

## Economic and Environmental Trajectories

Despite the capacity challenges, the model successfully identified a path to meet and even exceed environmental targets. The emissions reduction achieved by the optimized plan was notably aggressive, reaching over $70\%$ by 2029. This was possible because the model prioritized the electrification of the most frequent route (Vancouver Harbour–Victoria Harbour), where the highest volume of fuel consumption occurs.

Economically, the transition involves a shift in cost structure. In the early years, costs are dominated by the operation of conventional helicopters and the initial capital investment for electric aircraft and chargers. As the transition progresses, conventional operating costs decrease, but the penalties for unmet demand ($C_{pen}$) become a dominant factor. This highlights a critical trade-off for airline management: the "green" transition may be technically feasible and meet emissions targets, but it could lead to a loss in market share if the replacement aircraft are significantly smaller than the ones they replace.

The study's significance lies in its ability to quantify the specific requirements for a successful transition. It clarifies that simply buying electric planes is not enough; the entire business model, including the flight frequency and the acceptance of different aircraft sizes, must change. For operators, the research suggests that prioritizing high-demand corridors for early electrification yields the best return on investment and the most significant environmental impact. For policymakers, the study emphasizes that financial support might be needed not just for chargers, but to help airlines manage the capacity gap created by the current generation of small-scale electric aircraft.

## Future Directions in Sustainable Aviation

While this work provides a robust framework, it also opens new avenues for inquiry. The authors note that the current model assumes a fixed flight schedule based on historical conventional operations. Future iterations of the model could incorporate dynamic scheduling, which would allow the optimizer to change the departure times and frequencies to better exploit the faster turnaround times and lower per-hour operating costs of electric aircraft.

Additionally, the integration of stochastic demand—accounting for the fact that passenger numbers fluctuate daily—would make the model even more resilient. As battery technology improves and larger electric aircraft (such as 19-seat or 40-seat models) enter the market, this MILP framework can be updated to determine the optimal time to integrate these more capable platforms. By providing a mathematical bridge between today's conventional operations and tomorrow's electric future, this research serves as a foundation for a more sustainable domestic aviation industry in Canada and beyond.
