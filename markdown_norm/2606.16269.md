## The Challenge of Unifying Market Microstructure Regularities

In the study of financial market microstructure, two empirical observations have historically dominated the theoretical landscape: the long-memory of trade signs and the square-root law of price impact. The first, often referred to as the Lillo–Mike–Farmer (LMF) law, describes the persistent autocorrelation in the sequence of buy and sell orders. The second, the square-root law (SQRL), characterizes the concave relationship between the size of a large "meta-order" and the resulting change in the asset's price.

While both phenomena are fundamentally driven by the same underlying order flow, they have typically been treated as separate problems. Price impact models often rely on reaction-diffusion dynamics or latent order book theories to explain concavity, while trade-sign memory is usually explained through renewal processes or hidden-order theories. Furthermore, many models conflate different definitions of time—such as the discrete sequence of events versus the continuous flow of physical time.

The paper "Revisiting Trade-sign Long-memory and Square-root Law price impact" by Chris Angstmann and Tim Gebbie provides a unified theoretical framework. By starting from a discrete, event-driven model of the order book, the authors demonstrate that both the LMF law and the SQRL can be derived as consequences of the same underlying liquidity dynamics. A central contribution of the work is the explicit separation of event time, operational time, and calendar time, which clarifies how these regularities interact across different scales of market activity.

## A Discrete Foundation: The Imbalance Field

The authors begin by constructing a microscopic model of the order book. Rather than assuming continuous dynamics from the start, they define the market on a discrete grid of log-prices $x_j = j\Delta x$. At each discrete event $m$, which might represent a child-order execution or a book update, the densities of bid liquidity $\rho_{B,j}^m$ and ask liquidity $\rho_{A,j}^m$ are updated.

The model incorporates several key features of real-world markets:
1.  **Diffusive Relocation**: Liquidity moves across price levels as traders adjust their limit orders.
2.  **Cancellation and Replenishment**: Orders are removed from the book or added from both "lit" (visible) and "latent" (hidden) sources.
3.  **Local Matching**: When buy and sell orders meet, they annihilate each other, a process typically modeled as a non-linear interaction term proportional to the product of the bid and ask densities.

The critical insight that enables analytical progress is the definition of the "imbalance field":
$$\phi_j^m := \rho_{B,j}^m - \rho_{A,j}^m$$
By focusing on the difference between bid and ask densities, the authors show that the non-linear matching term cancels out exactly. This reduces a complex, non-linear system of equations to a single, linear update equation for the imbalance. This simplification is significant because it allows the mid-price to be tracked as a "reaction front"—the point where the imbalance field crosses zero.

## The Taxonomy of Time

A recurring issue in microstructure literature is the ambiguity of time. This paper addresses this by rigorously distinguishing between three temporal scales:

*   **Event Time ($m$):** A discrete counter that sequences every individual trade or update. This is the natural domain for trade-sign persistence, as the LMF law describes the correlation between the $m$-th and $(m+\tau)$-th trade.
*   **Operational Time ($u$):** A continuous "business clock" that represents the idealized flow of market activity. This is the scale where the partial differential equations (PDEs) for liquidity diffusion are formulated.
*   **Calendar Time ($t$):** The physical clock time measured in seconds or minutes. This is the scale relevant to real-world execution horizons and participation rates.

The authors use a mathematical framework called subordination to link these clocks. If $Y_m$ represents the price process in event time and $N_t$ is a random process counting the number of events up to calendar time $t$, the physical-time price process is given by:
$$X(t) = Y_{N_t}$$
This explicit separation allows the authors to show that while trade-sign memory is an event-time statement, the square-root law of impact is a physical-time statement.

## Deriving the Square-root Law of Price Impact

To move from the discrete model to a description of price impact, the authors take the continuum limit of the imbalance equation. This yields a linear reaction-diffusion PDE. They then apply the Local-Linear Order Book (LLOB) approximation, which assumes that near the current mid-price $p_0$, the background imbalance field is approximately linear:
$$\phi^*(x) \approx -L(x-p_0)$$
where $L$ represents the local slope of liquidity (the "stiffness" of the order book).

By introducing a meta-order as a localized forcing term and solving the PDE using Green's functions, the authors derive a Volterra equation that describes the trajectory of the price front. In the regime of weak liquidity cancellation and small price displacements, this equation simplifies to a form where the current price depends on the history of the order flow:
$$Lp(t) = \int_0^t \frac{m(s) e^{-\nu(t-s)}}{\sqrt{4\pi D(t-s)}} ds$$
Assuming a constant execution rate $m(s) = m_0$, the integration yields a transient impact that scales with the square root of time:
$$p(t) \propto \sqrt{t}$$
When translated into terms of the total volume $Q$ executed over a duration $T$, this recovers the classic square-root completion impact law:
$$I(Q) \propto \sqrt{Q}$$
This result is achieved without assuming a specific "impact function" a priori; instead, it emerges from the fundamental diffusive nature of the order book's response to an imbalance.

## The Lillo–Mike–Farmer Law and Trade-sign Memory

The second half of the unification involves the trade-sign process. The authors model the market as a sequence of meta-orders, where each meta-order has a specific sign (buy or sell) and a length $L$ (the number of child orders it contains). 

If the lengths of these meta-orders are drawn from a heavy-tailed distribution, such as a Pareto distribution:
$$p_L(\ell) \sim c_L \ell^{-(\alpha+1)}$$
where $\alpha > 1$, then the sequence of trade signs will exhibit long-memory. Using renewal theory, the authors derive an exact formula for the event-time trade-sign autocorrelation $C_\tau(\epsilon)$. They show that for large lags $\tau$, the autocorrelation decays according to a power law:
$$C_\tau(\epsilon) \sim \tau^{-(\alpha-1)}$$
This confirms the LMF exponent relation $\gamma = \alpha-1$, where $\gamma$ is the autocorrelation decay exponent. Importantly, this derivation is purely in event time. It demonstrates that the persistence of trade signs is a structural property of how large orders are split into smaller pieces, independent of the physical time that elapses between trades.

## Reconciling Long-memory with Market Efficiency

A major puzzle in finance is why returns are not highly predictable if trade signs have such long memory. If one can predict that the next ten trades will likely be "buys," why can't one easily predict that the price will go up?

The paper addresses this by analyzing how the persistent order flow is filtered by the order book's liquidity dynamics. They propose two primary mechanisms:
1.  **Transient Impact**: The impact of a single trade is not permanent but decays over time. The "propagator" (the mathematical description of how impact lingers) acts as a high-pass filter that dampens the long-range correlations of the trade signs.
2.  **Liquidity Adaptation**: The slope of the order book $L$ is not a fixed constant but can adapt to the flow. If a persistent stream of buy orders occurs, the ask side of the book may thicken or the bid side may thin, effectively "cushioning" the impact and preventing the price from trending too predictably.

By expressing the return covariance as a convolution of the sign-memory kernel and the impact propagator, the authors show that a specific balance between sign persistence and impact decay is required to maintain the observed weak autocorrelation in returns.

## Significance of the Unified View

This research provides a more parsimonious description of market dynamics by showing that the two most famous "laws" of market microstructure are two sides of the same coin. By grounding the theory in a discrete, event-based model, the authors bridge the gap between microscopic trading behavior and macroscopic price properties.

The work's insistence on the separation of time scales also has practical implications for empirical researchers. For example, it suggests that if the waiting times between trades are themselves heavy-tailed (as is often observed), the price impact measured in calendar time may show different scaling than the impact measured in event time. This "anomalous scaling" can be explained through the subordination framework provided in the paper, offering a path to reconcile conflicting empirical findings across different assets and time periods.

In summary, the paper moves beyond treating price impact and sign memory as isolated statistical curiosities. Instead, it positions them as integrated features of a mesoscopic liquidity system, where the square-root law describes the physical-time response of the order book and the LMF law describes the event-time structure of the order flow.
