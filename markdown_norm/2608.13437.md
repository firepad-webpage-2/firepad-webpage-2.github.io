The numerical simulation of compressible fluid flow is a cornerstone of modern aerospace engineering, astrophysics, and combustion research. These flows are characterized by two distinct and often conflicting phenomena: sharp discontinuities known as shock waves and complex, multi-scale turbulence. Capturing shocks requires numerical dissipation to prevent oscillations, while resolving turbulence requires minimizing dissipation to preserve fine-scale structures.

Traditional methods often rely on complex, non-local reconstruction schemes to balance these needs. The Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM) provides an alternative framework, decomposing fluid motion into local "collision" and "streaming" steps. While LBM is renowned for its efficiency on parallel hardware, extending it to the full compressible Navier–Stokes–Fourier (NSF) equations—which describe mass, momentum, and energy conservation alongside heat conduction—has historically been difficult without sacrificing its signature locality.

This work validates a specific LBM approach derived through automated symbolic compilation. By treating viscous stresses and heat fluxes as independent transported variables, the researchers demonstrate that it is possible to resolve supersonic turbulence and shocks accurately while maintaining the local computational structure that makes LBM attractive for high-performance computing.

## The Challenge of Compressibility in LBM

Standard LBM schemes were originally designed for weakly compressible or incompressible flows. In these regimes, the fluid density is nearly constant, and energy fluctuations are negligible. To model truly compressible flows, such as those where the Mach number exceeds unity, the solver must account for the full coupling between thermodynamics and fluid mechanics.

There are several ways to approach this in LBM. Some researchers use enlarged velocity sets (more directions for particles to move) or high-order equilibria. Others use "hybrid" methods, where LBM handles the momentum but a traditional finite-difference solver handles the energy equation. However, hybrid methods often lose the "locality" of LBM—the property where a grid cell only needs information from its immediate neighbors.

The researchers in this paper utilize a "relaxed-flux" formulation. In this setup, the macroscopic state vector $Q$ is extended to 14 components in three dimensions:
$$
Q = (\rho, \rho u, E, \tau, q)^\top
$$
Here, $\rho$ is density, $\rho u$ represents the three components of momentum, $E$ is the total energy, $\tau$ represents the six independent components of the symmetric viscous stress tensor, and $q$ represents the three components of the heat flux vector. By including $\tau$ and $q$ as primary variables that evolve over time, the system can naturally recover the Navier–Stokes–Fourier equations without requiring high-order velocity sets or non-local gradient calculations.

## Automated Scheme Derivation

A distinguishing feature of this research is the use of the `pde2lbm` symbolic compiler. Rather than manually deriving the collision rules and equilibrium distributions for the 14-component system—a process prone to human error—the researchers define the physics in a high-level language.

The compiler takes a system of conservation laws in the form:
$$
\partial_t Q + \nabla \cdot \Phi = S
$$
where $\Phi$ is the physical flux and $S$ is a source term. The compiler then automatically generates the corresponding LBM populations, equilibria, and optimized C++ kernels. This "relaxed-flux" approach embeds the physical flux exactly into the first moment of the distribution functions.

This automation allows for rapid testing of different physical models. For example, the pressure $p$ is defined via the ideal gas law $p = \rho R T$, and the temperature $T = p/\rho$ is consistently updated. The viscous stress $\tau$ and heat flux $q$ are relaxed toward their constitutive targets (Newtonian viscosity and Fourier heat conduction) using specific relaxation times $\tau_R$ and $\tau_q$. This structure ensures that the modeling error $O(\tau)$ decreases as the mesh is refined.

## Maintaining Locality with a Shock Sensor

When a flow becomes supersonic, shocks appear. Shocks are mathematical discontinuities that can cause numerical solvers to "explode" or produce non-physical oscillations. To prevent this, solvers usually add "artificial viscosity" in regions of high compression.

Standard shock-capturing methods calculate gradients (like the divergence of velocity $\nabla \cdot u$) by looking at many neighboring cells. This breaks LBM's locality. A key contribution of this work is a **purely local shock sensor**. Because the viscous stress $\tau$ is already a part of the local state vector $Q$, the researchers can algebraically recover the local dilatation $\nabla \cdot u$ within a single cell, without looking at neighbors.

The sensor works by calculating a compression Mach number:
$$
s = \max(0, -\nabla \cdot u) / c_s
$$
where $c_s^2 = 1/4$ is the lattice sound speed. A switching function $\chi$ is then defined:
$$
\chi = \text{clamp}(C_\sigma \cdot s, 0, 1)
$$
This $\chi$ is only active when $s > J_{\min}$, where $C_\sigma$ is a slope parameter and $J_{\min}$ is a threshold. This sensor locally modulates the relaxation rate $\omega_h$:
$$
\omega_h = \omega - \chi(\omega - \omega_{\min})
$$
By reducing the relaxation rate (increasing the local viscosity) only where the fluid is being strongly compressed, the scheme stabilizes the shock without blurring the rest of the turbulent flow.

## Validating the Physics: From Shocks to Turbulence

The researchers validated the scheme using three primary benchmarks, moving from simple 1D cases to complex 3D turbulence.

### 1. Sod Shock Tube and Becker Traveling Wave
The Sod shock tube is a standard test for inviscid flows, involving a shock wave, a contact discontinuity, and an expansion fan. The LBM scheme successfully captured these features with first-order convergence in shock thickness, meaning the shock width was consistently confined to a few grid cells regardless of the total resolution.

The Becker traveling wave provides a more rigorous test for viscous, heat-conducting flows because it has an exact analytical solution. The scheme matched the analytical profiles for density and pressure with high accuracy, confirming that the coupling between viscous stress $\tau$ and heat flux $q$ was correctly handled by the automated derivation.

### 2. Compressible Taylor–Green Vortex
The most demanding test was the 3D Taylor–Green vortex (TGV) at a Reynolds number $Re = 1600$ and an initial Mach number $M_0 = 1.25$. In this scenario, large vortices break down into smaller and smaller structures, eventually dissipating into heat. Because the flow is supersonic, "shocklets" (tiny, localized shocks) form throughout the volume.

The researchers compared their results against a multi-solver study involving seven other high-order CFD methods and a very high-resolution ($2048^3$) reference. They tracked three key metrics:
- **Kinetic Energy ($E_k$):** The total energy in the fluid motion.
- **Solenoidal Dissipation ($\epsilon_s$):** Dissipation due to the swirling, rotational part of the flow.
- **Dilatational Dissipation ($\epsilon_d$):** Dissipation specifically caused by the shocklets.

The results showed that the LBM scheme was highly competitive. Notably, for dilatational dissipation—the hardest metric to get right in supersonic turbulence—the LBM scheme at a resolution of $512^3$ achieved a relative error of only 12.78%. This was roughly 30% better than the next best high-order solver in the reference group.

## Efficiency and Performance

The choice of the D3Q7 lattice (only 7 discrete velocity directions per variable) combined with the 14-variable state results in a compact and computationally efficient stencil. The simulations were performed using single-precision floating-point numbers, which further reduces memory bandwidth requirements.

The parametric study of the shock sensor showed that a slope of $C_\sigma = 40$ provided a good balance between stabilizing shocks and maintaining the accuracy of the turbulent cascade. Even in the subsonic regime ($M_0 = 0.5$), where no shocks are present, the solver remained accurate, following the reference data closely without the shock sensor interfering with the physics.

## Conclusion and Future Directions

This work demonstrates that LBM is not limited to simple, incompressible flows. By using a relaxed-flux formulation and an automated symbolic compiler, the authors created a solver capable of handling the full complexity of the Navier–Stokes–Fourier equations, including shocks and supersonic turbulence.

The primary achievements of this study are:
1.  **Verification of Automated Derivation:** Proving that compiler-generated LBM schemes can handle highly non-linear, dissipative physical benchmarks.
2.  **Local Shock Capturing:** Implementing a stabilization mechanism that preserves the "embarrassingly parallel" nature of LBM by using local state information to detect compression.
3.  **Accuracy in Turbulence:** Showing that a compact LBM scheme can outperform some traditional high-order methods in capturing specific dissipation mechanisms in compressible turbulence.

The success of this approach in periodic domains (like the TGV benchmark) sets the stage for more complex applications. The researchers suggest that the next step is the automated generation of boundary treatments, which would allow this method to be applied to wall-bounded flows, such as air flowing over a wing or through a turbine, where both shocks and boundary-layer turbulence are critical.
