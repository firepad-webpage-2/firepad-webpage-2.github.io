## The Challenge of Grounded Visual Reasoning in Finance

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) are increasingly being applied to interpret complex visual data, ranging from medical imaging to financial charts. In the financial domain, candlestick charts (OHLCV: Open-High-Low-Close-Volume) serve as a primary medium for technical analysis. While modern VLMs can generate fluent, professional-sounding narratives describing these charts, a critical question remains: are these models genuinely understanding the local visual evidence—such as specific candle patterns or volume confirmations—or are they merely extrapolating global trends?

![real-market-candlestick-chart](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.17423v1/real_market.png)
*{fig:real-market-candlestick-chart}: A typical real-market candlestick chart showing price action and volume. Distinguishing whether a model's prediction is based on specific candle patterns or overall price momentum is the central challenge of this research.*

The stakes for this distinction are high. If a model provides a "bullish continuation" prediction simply because the price line is sloping upward, it is relying on a **momentum shortcut**. While such shortcuts might align with market outcomes during strong trends, they fail during reversals or "null" markets where no clear signal exists. In high-stakes decision support, a model that sounds confident but lacks visual grounding can be dangerously misleading. "Martingale Doppelgänger-Eval" introduces a rigorous identification framework designed to audit whether VLM interpretations are truly grounded in local visual evidence.

## The Identification Problem: Trend vs. Evidence

A fundamental obstacle in evaluating VLMs on real-market data is the natural coupling of features. In finance, specific diagnostic patterns (like a "breakout") often occur at the end of established trends. This creates a "confounding" effect: if a model correctly predicts an upward move, it is observationally impossible to tell if it saw the breakout pattern (evidence) or just the upward slope (trend).

The paper formalizes this through a theoretical lens, proving that no evaluation based purely on observational data can reliably distinguish a grounded model from a shortcut-reliant one when the trend and evidence are perfectly correlated. To solve this, the framework shifts from observational evaluation (checking if the model is "right" about the future) to **interventional evaluation** (checking how the model reacts when we surgically change the chart). By creating "doppelgänger" charts—versions of the same data where specific features are manipulated—the researchers can isolate the causal influence of different visual components on the model's output.

## The Martingale Doppelgänger-Eval Framework

The framework is organized into four "splits," each serving as a controlled diagnostic test. Unlike traditional benchmarks that measure accuracy against real-world stock prices, these splits use synthetic interventions to reveal the internal logic of the model.

### M0: The Martingale-Null Market
The M0 split tests how a model behaves when there is no directional information at all. The researchers generate "null" markets where the future price move is essentially a coin flip, yet the past trend remains visible. 

![m0-null-market](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.17423v1/m0_null.png)
*{fig:m0-null-market}: Examples from the M0 split, designed to evaluate model bias in the absence of directional signals.*

In this split, a perfectly calibrated model should report a probability $p_{\uparrow} \approx 0.5$ (neutral). If a model consistently predicts "up" for upward-sloping charts in a null market, it exhibits a measurable **trend bias**.

### M1: Injected Alpha
The M1 split tests sensitivity to local rules. Researchers take "dead" market histories and surgically inject specific candlestick signals, such as volume-confirmed breakouts or long-shadow reversals.

![m1-injected-evidence](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.17423v1/m1_injected.png)
*{fig:m1-injected-evidence}: The M1 split involves injecting specific, rule-based evidence into charts to see if the model reacts to the visual semantics.*

Each injected signal has a matched counterfactual pair: for example, one chart showing a breakout on high volume (bullish) versus the same chart with the same breakout on low volume (neutral or bearish). This allows the audit to measure if the VLM is sensitive to the *interaction* of price and volume, rather than just the price slope.

### M2: Trend-Confounder Swaps
The M2 split deliberately manipulates the relationship between the past trend and the future label. In one version, the trend is aligned with the outcome; in another, the trend is reversed. A model relying on momentum shortcuts will perform exceptionally well on the aligned version but fail catastrophically on the reversed version.

![m2-trend-confounder-swap](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.17423v1/m2_swap.png)
*{fig:m2-trend-confounder-swap}: M2 manipulations expose model reliance on trend shortcuts by reversing the relationship between past momentum and future outcomes.*

### M3: Market Regimes
Finally, M3 evaluates how models adapt their confidence across different market conditions, such as high-volatility regimes versus stable ranges.

![m3-market-regimes](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.17423v1/m3_regime.png)
*{fig:m3-market-regimes}: M3 tests model robustness across varying market environments.*

## Quantifying VLM Behavior: The Structural Model

To interpret the results of these tests, the framework uses a structural behavioral model. For a given chart, the model's reported probability of an upward move $p_{\uparrow} \in [0, 1]$ is analyzed on a logit scale (where $p_{\uparrow} = 1 / (1 + e^{-x})$). The relationship is defined by the following equation:

$$\text{logit}(p_{\uparrow}) = \alpha + \beta_S S + \beta_E E + \gamma_{\text{prompt}} + \gamma_{\text{style}} + \epsilon$$

Where:
*   $\alpha$ represents the baseline bias of the model.
*   $\beta_S$ measures **trend sensitivity**: how much the model's prediction changes based on the past momentum $S \in [-1, 1]$.
*   $\beta_E$ measures **evidence sensitivity**: how much the model reacts to the injected rule-based evidence $E \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$.
*   $\gamma_{\text{prompt}}$ and $\gamma_{\text{style}}$ capture how much the model's answer varies due to the specific phrasing of the prompt or the visual style of the chart (e.g., color scheme).
*   $\epsilon$ is the residual noise.

By fitting this model to the VLM's responses across the different splits, the researchers can assign numerical scores to each model's "behavioral profile." A "grounded" model would show a high $\beta_E$ and a low $\beta_S$, while a "shortcut" model would show the opposite.

## Empirical Findings: Shortcuts over Semantics

The researchers audited several prominent VLMs, including commercial APIs (like GPT-4 and Claude) and open-source models (like LLaVA and Qwen). The results across the board revealed a significant reliance on shortcuts.

![core-diagnostics](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.17423v1/core_diagnostics_clean.png)
*{fig:core-diagnostics}: A comparison of model diagnostics showing high trend bias (TBI) and poor performance on trend-reversed splits across all tested models.*

### 1. The Dominance of Trend
Every model tested showed a high $\beta_S$ (trend sensitivity). In the M0 null-market tests, models consistently predicted directional moves based on the slope of the chart, even though the future was mathematically random. This trend bias was particularly pronounced in open-source models.

### 2. Failure to Capture Evidence
On the M1 split, where specific candlestick signals were injected, model performance was near random. Most models failed to distinguish between a "strong" signal (e.g., high-volume breakout) and a "weak" one (e.g., low-volume breakout). Surprisingly, some commercial API models actually moved in the *opposite* direction of the rule-implied signal, likely because they were prioritizing the surface motion over the underlying semantic rules.

### 3. The Shortcut Gap
The M2 split confirmed these findings. Models showed a massive "shortcut gap"—performing well when the trend matched the label and poorly when it didn't. This suggests that the apparent "accuracy" of VLMs on financial charts in previous benchmarks may be largely driven by their ability to guess the prevailing trend, rather than an understanding of technical analysis patterns.

## Faithfulness and Diagnostic Reliability

The audit also looked at "evidence faithfulness": if a model highlights a specific part of the chart as "evidence," does that part actually drive its prediction? Using a metric called the Causal Evidence Score (CES), the researchers found that editing the regions models claimed to be important often had no more impact than editing random parts of the chart.

![reliability-and-risk-coverage](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.17423v1/reliability_risk_coverage.png)
*{fig:reliability-and-risk-coverage}: Reliability curves (left) show that models are often poorly calibrated, with reported probabilities not matching empirical frequencies. Risk-coverage curves (right) show how selective classification—allowing models to "pass" on uncertain cases—affects performance.*

Furthermore, reliability curves ({fig:reliability-and-risk-coverage}) showed that models were poorly calibrated. Their reported confidence (e.g., "I am 90% sure") did not match their actual success rate on the injected signals. In many cases, the more confident a model was, the *less* likely it was to be correct about the rule-based signal, indicating a systemic overconfidence in shortcut-based reasoning.

## Broader Significance and the Future of AI Auditing

"Martingale Doppelgänger-Eval" provides a blueprint for a more rigorous way of evaluating AI in domain-specific tasks. Its significance extends beyond finance in several ways:

*   **Identifiability as a Requirement**: The framework proves that for any domain where global shape and local evidence are correlated (like EKG waveforms or satellite imagery), researchers *must* use interventional benchmarks to detect shortcut learning.
*   **A Shift in Metrics**: The paper argues for moving away from single accuracy scores toward "behavioral coordinates" ($\alpha, \beta_S, \beta_E$). This allows developers to see *why* a model is failing—is it too biased, too fragile, or just insensitive to evidence?
*   **Responsible AI Deployment**: By exposing the gap between fluency and grounding, this research serves as a warning for the deployment of VLMs in advisory roles. It emphasizes that a model's ability to "talk the talk" of a financial analyst does not mean it is actually "looking" at the chart in a meaningful way.

Ultimately, the framework promotes a move toward "structural" audits—testing the causal mechanics of AI reasoning rather than just its final outputs. As VLMs continue to penetrate specialized industries, such rigorous "stress testing" will be essential to ensure they provide reliable, grounded insights rather than just sophisticated trend extrapolation.
