## Introduction to Causal Inference and Missing Data

In epidemiological and social science research, the primary goal is often to estimate the average causal effect (ACE) of an exposure $X$ on an outcome $Y$. The ACE is formally defined as the difference in the expected potential outcomes:

$$
ACE = E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)]
$$

where $Y(x)$ represents the potential outcome that would have been observed if the individual had been assigned to exposure level $x \in \{0, 1\}$. In practice, researchers face two major hurdles: confounding and missing data. While confounding is addressed by adjusting for a set of confounders $Z$, missing data requires additional assumptions about why data are absent.

Multiple imputation (MI) is a standard technique used to handle missingness. It involves filling in missing values multiple times to create complete datasets, analyzing each, and pooling the results. A key advantage of MI is its ability to incorporate "auxiliary variables"—variables that are not part of the main analysis model but are associated with the missing variables or the process of missingness itself. Traditionally, these auxiliary variables have been viewed as tools to improve the precision of estimates. However, this paper demonstrates that auxiliary variables $A$ are often essential for "recoverability," meaning the ACE cannot be mathematically identified from the observed data without them.

The paper focuses on scenarios where an auxiliary variable is a common cause of both the variable that is missing and the indicator of its missingness, a structure that makes the auxiliary variable vital for removing bias.

## The Causal Role of Auxiliary Variables: Mediators and Non-Mediators

A central contribution of this research is the distinction between two types of auxiliary variables: non-mediators and mediators. The causal role of an auxiliary variable dictates how it should be handled in the statistical analysis.

1.  **Non-mediator Auxiliary Variables ($A_1$):** These variables are associated with the exposure, outcome, or confounders but do not lie on the causal path between $X$ and $Y$. For example, in a study of cannabis use ($X$) and mental health ($Y$), academic proficiency ($A_1$) might be a common cause of both but not a mechanism through which cannabis affects health.
2.  **Mediator Auxiliary Variables ($A_2$):** These variables lie on the causal pathway from the exposure to the outcome ($X \to A_2 \to Y$). In the same cannabis study, sleep problems ($A_2$) might be an intermediate step; cannabis use leads to sleep issues, which in turn affect mental health.

This distinction is critical because of how standard adjustment methods work. If a researcher uses a Complete-Case Analysis (CCA) and tries to adjust for a mediator by including it in the outcome regression model, they will inadvertently block part of the total causal effect they are trying to measure. This leads to "over-adjustment bias." The paper shows that while MI can safely use mediators in the imputation step to recover missing information, the final analysis model must *exclude* them to ensure the total ACE is estimated correctly.

## Recoverability: When are Auxiliary Variables Essential?

"Recoverability" refers to whether an estimand (like the ACE) can be expressed as a function of the distribution of the observed data. When data are missing, we only observe values when $R=1$. If the missingness of $Y$ depends on $Y$ itself, even after conditioning on confounders $Z$, the effect is generally not recoverable. However, if an auxiliary variable $A$ fully explains that relationship, recoverability might be restored.

The authors derive formal recoverability expressions for several m-DAGs. For a scenario where the outcome $Y$ is missing and $A_1$ is a non-mediator common cause of $Y$ and $R_Y$, the distribution of the potential outcome $P(Y_x)$ is recovered as:

$$
P(Y_x) = \sum_{z, a_1} P(Y|X=x, Z=z, A_1=a_1, R_Y=1)P(A_1|Z=z, R_{A_1}=1)P(Z|R_Z=1)
$$

This expression shows that to estimate the effect, we must weight the observed outcome distribution by the distributions of the auxiliary variable and the confounders. This highlights a theoretical necessity: if $A_1$ is missing or ignored, the calculation cannot be completed, and the resulting ACE estimate will be biased. The paper extends this logic to multivariable missingness, where $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ might all be incomplete, showing that auxiliary variables are often the "missing link" required to bridge the gap between observed data and the true causal effect.

## Multiple Imputation Strategies: Compatibility and Performance

Even when recoverability is theoretically possible, the choice of MI implementation matters significantly. A common challenge in MI is "incompatibility," which occurs when the model used to fill in missing data (the imputation model) makes different assumptions than the model used for the final analysis (the substantive model).

The paper evaluates several popular MI approaches:

*   **Regression (REG) and Predictive Mean Matching (PMM):** These are standard components of Fully Conditional Specification (FCS). They include auxiliary variables as predictors in the imputation equations.
*   **Classification and Regression Trees (CART):** A non-parametric approach that uses machine learning to fill in missing values. Because it does not assume a specific functional form, it naturally handles complex interactions and non-linear relationships involving auxiliary variables.
*   **Substantive Model Compatible FCS (SMCFCS):** This approach is designed to ensure the imputation model is mathematically consistent with the substantive analysis model. The authors specifically test **A-SMCFCS**, which uses a version of the outcome model that includes the auxiliary variable during the imputation phase but excludes it during the final ACE estimation phase.

The simulation studies revealed that standard methods like REG and PMM often fail in complex multivariable missingness scenarios, particularly when there are interactions between exposures and confounders. In contrast, **A-SMCFCS** and **CART** were the most robust. A-SMCFCS succeeded because it maintained "semi-compatibility"—it used the auxiliary information to fill in the data correctly while respecting the fact that the auxiliary variable should not be adjusted for in the final causal analysis.

## Practical Recommendations for ACE Estimation

Based on extensive simulations across univariable and multivariable missingness mechanisms, the paper provides clear guidance for researchers.

1.  **Identify Auxiliary Variables Early:** During the study design phase, researchers should identify variables that might predict both the values of incomplete variables and the probability that they are missing. These should be measured even if they are not of direct interest in the causal analysis.
2.  **Determine the Causal Role:** Before imputing, determine if an auxiliary variable is a mediator. If it is, **never** include it in the final outcome regression model (the substantive model), as this will bias the total ACE.
3.  **Choose Compatible MI Methods:**
    *   For most applications, **A-SMCFCS** is recommended. It allows for the inclusion of auxiliary variables in the imputation process in a way that remains compatible with the target analysis.
    *   **CART** is an excellent alternative, especially when the relationships between variables are complex or unknown, as it requires fewer parametric assumptions.
4.  **Avoid Adjusted-Complete-Case Analysis (A-CCA) for Mediators:** While A-CCA (simply adding the auxiliary variable to a standard regression on observed cases) can sometimes work for non-mediators, it produces severe bias when the auxiliary variable is a mediator. Multiple imputation is the superior choice in these settings.

## Significance and Conclusion

This research shifts the perspective on auxiliary variables from being optional "precision-boosters" to being potentially "essential identifiers" of causal effects. By using m-DAGs to map out the missingness process, the authors provide a rigorous framework for determining when and how these variables must be used.

The study's most significant practical finding is that the implementation of multiple imputation must be carefully aligned with the causal structure of the data. The success of A-SMCFCS and CART in realistic, multivariable missingness scenarios provides researchers with a clear path forward. These methods allow for the effective use of auxiliary information—including mediators—to recover causal effects that would otherwise be lost to missing data bias, without falling into the trap of over-adjustment. Ultimately, this work enhances the reliability of causal conclusions drawn from imperfect, real-world observational data.
