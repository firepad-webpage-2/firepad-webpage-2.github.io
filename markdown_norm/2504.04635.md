## Introduction

Language model steering has emerged as a promising approach to modifying model behavior at inference time without the computational expense of full fine-tuning. By manipulating the model's internal activations, steering methods aim to guide language models toward desired behaviors like improved factuality or enhanced reasoning. However, a critical question remains: how reliable are these steering techniques across different models and tasks?

A collaborative team from The Ohio State University, University of Washington, Fastino AI, and the Allen Institute for AI has conducted a comprehensive evaluation of three prominent steering methods: DoLa (Decoding by contrasting layers), function vectors, and task vectors. Their analysis spans an unprecedented scope, examining 36 language models from 14 different families, ranging from 1.5B to 70B parameters.

![unreliable-behavior-of-steering-methods](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures/2504.04635/x1.png)
*{fig:unreliable-behavior-of-steering-methods}: The top row shows DoLa's unreliable behavior across different models, with inconsistent patterns in token probabilities for correct vs. incorrect answers. The middle and bottom rows demonstrate function vectors and task vectors showing highly variable performance across model layers.*

The results reveal significant limitations in the generalizability of these steering methods. While prior work has reported promising results, these findings were often limited to specific models or tasks, creating a "generalization blind spot" in our understanding. This paper takes a critical step in addressing this gap, challenging some of the fundamental assumptions underlying these steering approaches.

## Understanding Steering Methods

Steering methods for language models operate on a fundamental principle: by modifying the model's activations during inference, they aim to alter the model's behavior without changing its parameters. This research examines three prominent steering approaches:

1. **DoLa (Decoding by contrasting layers)**: Based on the logit lens interpretability technique, DoLa attempts to improve factuality by contrasting the outputs of different model layers. The method assumes that factual knowledge emerges gradually through the model's layers, with deeper layers potentially introducing biases.

2. **Function Vectors**: This activation patching approach identifies and manipulates specific attention heads believed to be responsible for in-context learning (ICL). The technique rests on the "localization hypothesis" - the idea that ICL capabilities are localized in a small subset of attention heads.

3. **Task Vectors**: Similar to function vectors but extracted from a model fine-tuned on a specific task, task vectors aim to transfer the learned capabilities to models without fine-tuning, by manipulating activations.

These methods have gained popularity because they promise to modify model behavior efficiently, without the computational expense of fine-tuning. However, their effectiveness has been evaluated primarily on a limited set of models and tasks, raising questions about their broader applicability.

## Methodology and Evaluation Approach

The research team employed a rigorous evaluation framework to test the reliability of steering methods across diverse language models. Their approach included:

1. **Comprehensive Model Coverage**: The study evaluated 36 models from 14 families, including state-of-the-art models like OLMo, Llama, Qwen, Gemma, Mistral, and Pythia, with sizes ranging from 1.5B to 70B parameters.

2. **Task-Specific Evaluation**:
   - For DoLa: Multiple-choice factuality tasks (TruthfulQA and FACTOR)
   - For Function and Task Vectors: A set of 11 in-context learning word-pair tasks spanning linguistic tasks, factual knowledge, and translation

3. **Extensive Hyperparameter Tuning**: Rather than using default hyperparameters, the researchers performed extensive tuning for each model and task combination, exploring various settings for:
   - Activation patching layers
   - Vector strength multipliers
   - Number of attention heads

4. **Analysis of Underlying Assumptions**: The study critically examined the assumptions behind each steering method, such as the gradual evolution of factual knowledge across layers (for DoLa) and the localization of in-context learning in specific attention heads (for function vectors).

This methodology allowed the researchers to move beyond the limitations of previous evaluations, providing a more comprehensive understanding of when and why steering methods succeed or fail.

## DoLa and the Limitations of Logit Lens

DoLa relies on the logit lens interpretability technique, which assumes that factual knowledge gradually develops across a model's layers, with earlier layers potentially containing more accurate information than later ones. The research findings challenge this assumption in several important ways:

```python
# Simplified representation of DoLa algorithm
def dola_inference(model, input_text, early_layer=8, late_layer=24, alpha=0.5):
    # Get logits from the early layer
    early_logits = model.get_layer_logits(input_text, layer=early_layer)
    # Get logits from the late layer
    late_logits = model.get_layer_logits(input_text, layer=late_layer)
    # Combine logits with weighting
    combined_logits = late_logits - alpha * (late_logits - early_logits)
    # Return combined logits
    return combined_logits
```

The researchers' analysis of token probabilities across model layers revealed inconsistent patterns that contradict the fundamental assumptions of DoLa:

1. **Inconsistent Layer Evolution**: Rather than showing a consistent pattern of factual knowledge evolution across layers, models exhibited widely varying behaviors. As shown in {fig:unreliable-behavior-of-steering-methods} (top row), models like OLMo 7B, Qwen-2 72B, and Llama 7B display distinctly different patterns in how token probabilities evolve through layers.

2. **Variable Performance Improvement**: Performance gains from DoLa were much lower than initially reported in the original study after correcting the metric computation. Moreover, many models showed no improvement or even deteriorated in performance when DoLa was applied.

3. **Model-Specific Behavior**: The study found that layer dynamics vary dramatically across model families and even within the same family at different scales. {fig:model-specific-token-probability-evolution} illustrates this variability across different models:

![model-specific-token-probability-evolution](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures/2504.04635/x2.png)
*{fig:model-specific-token-probability-evolution}: Token probability evolution across model layers for different LLMs, showing inconsistent patterns that challenge the assumptions underlying DoLa.*

The visualizations in {fig:unreliable-behavior-of-steering-methods} and {fig:model-specific-token-probability-evolution} clearly demonstrate that the evolution of token probabilities through layers doesn't follow a universal pattern. While some models show a gradual increase in probability for correct answers in later layers, others show the opposite pattern or no clear pattern at all. This inconsistency undermines DoLa's core assumption about how factual knowledge emerges in language models.

## Function Vectors and the Challenges of Activation Patching

Function vectors represent an approach to steering that relies on identifying and manipulating specific attention heads believed to be responsible for in-context learning (ICL). This method is based on the "localization hypothesis," which posits that ICL capabilities are concentrated in a small subset of attention heads. The research findings raise significant questions about this assumption:

1. **Inconsistent Effectiveness**: Function vectors showed high variability in performance across models and tasks. As shown in {fig:unreliable-behavior-of-steering-methods} (middle row), the effectiveness of function vectors varied dramatically across different models, with some models (like Llama-2 7B) showing improvement, while others (like Mistral-v0.3 7B and Llama-3.1 70B) showing negligible effects.

2. **Localization Hypothesis Challenges**: The study discovered that the number of attention heads needed to recover performance varied significantly across models and tasks. Some models required manipulating a large number of attention heads to see any benefit, contradicting the localization hypothesis.

*{fig:best-number-of-heads-for-function-vectors}: Heatmap showing the optimal number of attention heads for function vectors across different models and tasks. The wide variation challenges the localization hypothesis.*

3. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity**: The effectiveness of function vectors was highly dependent on three critical hyperparameters:
   - The layer where activation patching is applied
   - The strength of the function vector
   - The number of attention heads to modify

{fig:best-number-of-heads-for-function-vectors} shows the optimal number of heads required for different models and tasks, revealing significant variability. Similarly, {fig:best-function-vector-strength} illustrates the best function vector strength values, which also vary widely:

The optimal activation patching layer also varied considerably across models, as shown in {fig:best-activation-patching-layer}:

These findings collectively challenge the idea that function vectors offer a reliable, generalizable method for steering language models. The research indicates that successful function vector application requires extensive hyperparameter tuning specific to each model and task combination, limiting its practical utility.

## Task Vectors and Performance Variability

Task vectors take a similar approach to function vectors but are derived from models fine-tuned on specific tasks. The research revealed several key insights about task vector performance:

1. **Layer-Specific Effectiveness**: Task vectors showed high variability in their effectiveness across different layers of models. As illustrated in {fig:unreliable-behavior-of-steering-methods} (bottom row), task vector performance for Pythia 6.9B, Qwen-2.5 7B, and GPT-J 6B varies dramatically depending on which layer is targeted.

2. **Task-Dependent Performance**: The effectiveness of task vectors varied significantly across different tasks. {fig:gpt-j-6b-task-vector-performance} shows how task vector performance for GPT-J 6B varies across different tasks:

*{fig:qwen-2-7b-task-vector-performance}: Task vector performance for Qwen-2 7B across different tasks and layers, highlighting model-specific behavior.*

3. **Complex Interplay with Hyperparameters**: Like function vectors, task vectors required careful tuning of hyperparameters, with optimal settings varying by model and task. {fig:gpt-j-6b-function-vector-grid-search} illustrates how the number of heads and vector strength interact for GPT-J 6B on the country-capital task:

These results indicate that while task vectors can be effective in some contexts, their performance is highly dependent on the specific model, task, and hyperparameter configuration. This variability significantly limits their reliability as a general-purpose steering method.

## The Impact of Post-Training and Hyperparameter Sensitivity

The study revealed two additional important factors affecting steering method reliability:

1. **Post-Training Effects**: The impact of instruction tuning on steering methods was inconsistent across models. Some instruction-tuned models became more amenable to steering, while others became less responsive. {fig:post-training-impact} illustrates the difference in performance between base and instruction-tuned models:

![post-training-impact](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures/2504.04635/x16.png)
*{fig:post-training-impact}: The difference in performance recovery between instruction-tuned and base models across different tasks, showing inconsistent effects of post-training.*

2. **Casual Indirect Effects**: The researchers observed unexpected "casual indirect effects" where manipulating specific attention heads affected unrelated tasks. This suggests that the functions of attention heads are not as cleanly separated as previously assumed. {fig:casual-indirect-effects-for-llama3-8b} and {fig:casual-indirect-effects-for-gpt-j-6b} show these effects for different models:

![casual-indirect-effects-for-llama3-8b](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures/2504.04635/x10.png)
*{fig:casual-indirect-effects-for-llama3-8b}: Casual indirect effects in Llama-3 8B, showing how manipulating attention heads for one task affects performance on unrelated tasks.*

These findings highlight the complexity of steering language models and the limitations of current approaches that treat model components as having distinct, separable functions.

## Conclusions and Implications

The comprehensive evaluation conducted in this research reveals significant challenges to the reliability and generalizability of current steering methods:

1. **Limited Generalization**: Steering methods that work well for specific models and tasks often fail to generalize across different architectures, scales, and domains.

2. **Flawed Underlying Assumptions**: The fundamental assumptions of steering methods—such as the gradual evolution of factual knowledge (for DoLa) and the localization of in-context learning capabilities (for function vectors)—don't hold consistently across models.

3. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity**: Effective steering requires careful tuning of multiple hyperparameters, with optimal settings varying widely across models and tasks.

4. **Post-Training Variability**: The effects of instruction tuning on steerability are inconsistent, further complicating the reliable application of steering methods.

These findings have important implications for both researchers and practitioners. For researchers, they highlight the need for more comprehensive evaluation protocols that test steering methods across diverse models and tasks. The paper demonstrates the value of challenging underlying assumptions rather than just measuring performance improvements.

For practitioners, the results suggest caution in applying steering methods without extensive validation specific to their use case and model. The high variability in performance and hyperparameter sensitivity means that steering methods require significant tuning and may not deliver consistent benefits.

Looking ahead, the research points to several promising directions for improving steering methods:

1. Developing more robust theoretical frameworks that account for the diverse behaviors observed across different models

2. Creating adaptive steering approaches that can automatically adjust to different model architectures and behaviors

3. Combining multiple steering techniques to mitigate the limitations of individual methods

In conclusion, while steering methods offer potential benefits for modifying LLM behavior at inference time, their current implementation faces significant reliability challenges. This work represents an important step toward a more nuanced understanding of steering techniques and sets the stage for developing more robust approaches in the future.
