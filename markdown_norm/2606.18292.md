## Formalizing the Foundations of Human Action

The Austrian school of economics, particularly as formulated by Ludwig von Mises, rests on "praxeology"—the logic of purposeful human action. Unlike mainstream neoclassical economics, which frequently relies on differential calculus and equilibrium models, Misesian praxeology was historically developed through verbal logic. This methodological choice has often led to the perception that Austrian economics is incompatible with mathematical formalization. However, recent research by Komendarczyk et al. from Tulane University and Loyola University New Orleans provides a rigorous formalization of these foundations using many-sorted first-order logic.

The study aims to translate the core axioms of Mises's *Human Action* into a symbolic system, denoted $T_{prx}$. By doing so, the researchers provide a Hilbert-style verification of praxeological arguments, making implicit assumptions explicit and verifying the logical consistency of the system. This approach does not seek to transform economics into a branch of statistics, but rather to clarify the deductive structure of economic theory itself.

## The Logical Framework: Many-Sorted First-Order Logic

To represent the complexity of human action, the authors employ a many-sorted first-order logic. This variety of logic allows for different categories (sorts) of objects to coexist, preventing the logical errors that might arise if "actions" and "actors" were treated as the same type of entity. The language $L_{prx}$ is built upon five fundamental sorts:

*   **Actors ($a, b, p$):** The individuals capable of conscious choice.
*   **Actions ($\alpha, \beta$):** The purposeful acts performed by actors.
*   **Ends ($E, F, G$):** The goals or states of affairs that an actor seeks to achieve.
*   **Things ($x, y, z$):** The physical objects or resources available in the world.
*   **Times ($t, s, r$):** The discrete moments at which actions and preferences exist.

Within this environment, the researchers define six primitive relations that describe the interaction between these sorts:
1.  $Acts(a, \alpha, t)$: Actor $a$ performs action $\alpha$ at time $t$.
2.  $Avail(a, \alpha, t)$: Action $\alpha$ is available to actor $a$ at time $t$.
3.  $EndOf(\alpha, E)$: Action $\alpha$ is directed toward achieving end $E$.
4.  $Use(\alpha, x)$: Action $\alpha$ employs thing $x$ as a means.
5.  $E \succ_{ta} F$: Actor $a$ at time $t$ prefers end $E$ over end $F$.
6.  $t < s$: Time $t$ precedes time $s$.

By establishing these primitives, the researchers create a formal vocabulary that can express Mises’s verbal definitions with mathematical precision. For instance, a "means" is not merely an object, but a thing $x$ that is part of the relation $Use(\alpha, x)$ for some purposeful action.

## The Axiom System of Praxeology

The formalization structures praxeology into three layers of axioms. This layered approach ensures that the most basic properties of action are established before introducing complex concepts like preference and scarcity.

### Layer 1: The Structure of Action
The first layer defines the basic constraints on what it means to act. A critical axiom here is the "Action Choice" axiom (C1), which states that an actor can perform at most one action at any given time:

$$\forall a, t, \alpha, \beta, (Acts(a, \alpha, t) \land Acts(a, \beta, t)) \implies \alpha = \beta$$

This axiom formalizes the Misesian principle that human life is a sequence of choices; one cannot do everything at once. Additionally, the axioms ensure that every action has exactly one unique end ($EndOf$) and that time is a linear, discrete progression.

### Layer 2: Preference and Choice
The second layer connects internal, unobservable preferences to observable choices. The researchers define "Revealed Preference" ($E \triangleright_{ta} F$) as a situation where an actor chooses an action for end $E$ while an action for end $F$ was also available:

$$E \triangleright_{ta} F \iff \exists \alpha, \beta, (Acts(a, \alpha, t) \land EndOf(\alpha, E) \land Avail(a, \beta, t) \land EndOf(\beta, F) \land \alpha \neq \beta)$$

The "Grounding Axiom" (O0) then asserts that if an actor reveals a preference through action, it must reflect their internal preference order: $E \triangleright_{ta} F \implies E \succ_{ta} F$. This layer also establishes that the internal preference order $\succ_{ta}$ is a strict total order on the actor's current menu of ends ($E_{a,t}$), meaning the actor can always rank any two available options without being "indifferent" in a way that prevents action.

### Layer 3: Material Scarcity
The final layer introduces the concept of scarcity ($S1$). It asserts that there exists at least one situation where two different possible actions require the use of the same physical resource. This axiom ensures that the system describes an economic world where choices matter because resources are limited.

## Deriving Economic Theorems

The power of this formalization is demonstrated by the derivation of classic economic laws as formal theorems. This process shows that these laws are not merely empirical observations but are logically implied by the definition of action itself.

### Opportunity Cost
The researchers prove that "opportunity cost" is a structural necessity. Because an actor can only perform one action at a time, choosing $E$ necessitates the sacrifice of the next best alternative $F$. The theorem establishes that for any choice, there is a unique, subjective opportunity cost—the highest-ranked end that was not pursued.

### Diminishing Marginal Utility (DMU)
One of the most significant contributions of the paper is the formal derivation of the Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility. This law states that as an actor acquires more units of a homogeneous good, the value of the marginal unit decreases. The authors identify several "hidden" premises required for this to hold logically:
1.  **Homogeneity:** The units must be interchangeable ($UnitOf(x, g)$).
2.  **Allocation ($Allot(a, t, x, E)$):** Actors assign units to specific ends.
3.  **Preference-Respecting Allocation:** An actor will always satisfy their most urgent ends first.

Given these premises, the paper provides a proof that removing a unit of a good necessarily results in the loss of the least-important end currently being served. Since the remaining units continue to serve the more important ends, the "utility" (rank) of the marginal unit must have been lower than those above it.

### Disutility of Labor
Similarly, by defining a predicate $Labor(\alpha)$, the authors derive the increasing marginal disutility of labor. They show that since labor is a means to achieve ends, and since leisure is itself a highly-ranked end, every additional hour of labor requires the sacrifice of an increasingly valuable unit of leisure time.

## Machine Verification and the Crusoe Model

To ensure the system $T_{prx}$ is logically sound and free of contradictions, the researchers implemented the axioms in **Lean 4**, a modern interactive theorem prover and programming language. This provides a "computational proof of consistency."

The authors constructed a formal model based on the "Robinson Crusoe" economy—a standard pedagogical tool in Austrian economics. In this model, Crusoe exists in different states: "subsistence" (gathering berries), "coastal" (fishing with a net), and "deep-sea" (fishing with a boat). The researchers proved that this model satisfies all the axioms of the $T_{prx}$ system. By successfully building a mathematical model where all the axioms are true, they demonstrate that the axioms are mutually consistent.

Furthermore, the model serves to illustrate the subjectivity of economic categories. For instance, the "opportunity cost" of fishing is shown to be purely dependent on Crusoe's internal preference scale ($\succ_{ta}$), which can vary even if the physical "menu" of available actions remains identical.

## Significance of the Formalization

This research represents a significant step in the methodological evolution of the Austrian school. By providing a formal foundation for praxeology, the authors achieve several objectives:

1.  **Logical Transparency:** The formalization reveals exactly which assumptions are necessary for specific conclusions. For example, the derivation of DMU requires an explicit "Coherence Axiom" regarding how actors allocate goods, which is often left as an implicit assumption in verbal treatments.
2.  **Defending against Misinterpretation:** Critics often argue that Austrian economics is "anti-math." This work demonstrates that while Austrians reject certain types of math (like those assuming cardinal utility or continuous functions), praxeology is perfectly compatible with, and can be strengthened by, the math of formal logic.
3.  **A Basis for Extension:** The base system $T_{prx}$ provides a starting point for more complex economic theories. Future work can build on this foundation to formalize exchange, monetary prices, and the "Economic Calculation" problem. The authors specifically suggest that the undecidability of certain economic problems (like central planning) might be provable using this logical framework.

By moving from prose to many-sorted first-order logic, the researchers have provided a rigorous, verifiable, and precise foundation for the science of human action. This not only aids in the internal consistency of the theory but also makes Austrian insights more accessible to the broader scientific community using the universal language of logic.
