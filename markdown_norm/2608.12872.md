## Introduction to 6G Digital Twins and Channel Reconstruction

The transition to sixth-generation (6G) mobile communication marks a fundamental shift from reactive to proactive environment intelligence. Central to this evolution is the concept of the Digital Twin (DT), a high-fidelity virtual representation of physical environments. In wireless communications, a digital twin allows operators to simulate, predict, and optimize signal propagation in complex urban or industrial settings without the need for constant physical measurements. To build these twins, researchers rely on Ray Tracing (RT), a deterministic method that simulates electromagnetic (EM) wave propagation by calculating paths—such as reflection, diffraction, and scattering—between a transmitter and a receiver.

For an RT simulation to be accurate, the virtual environment must accurately mirror the electromagnetic properties of the real world. This requires precise knowledge of materials like concrete, glass, and asphalt, specifically their relative permittivity $ \epsilon_r $ and conductivity $ \sigma $. However, these parameters are not universal; they vary significantly across different frequency bands and environmental conditions. Directly measuring these parameters for every building or road surface in a city is physically and economically impractical. Consequently, researchers employ "reverse calibration," an optimization process that adjusts the EM parameters in a simulation until the predicted path loss matches empirical data collected from the field.

## The Challenge of Electromagnetic Parameter Calibration

Calibrating EM parameters is a formidable task. It is mathematically categorized as a multi-variable, non-convex optimization problem. In a typical urban scenario, the interactions between signals and diverse materials create a complex "error landscape" with many local minima—points that appear to be optimal solutions but are actually inferior to the true global optimum.

Current methodologies for solving this problem generally fall into two categories, both of which possess significant drawbacks:
1.  **Heuristic and Swarm Intelligence Algorithms:** Algorithms such as the standard Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO) or Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) are excellent at searching wide spaces. However, they require a full population of candidate solutions to be evaluated in every iteration. In the context of EM calibration, each evaluation requires a time-consuming forward ray-tracing simulation. For a population of $ N $ candidates over dozens of iterations, the computational cost becomes prohibitive.
2.  **Static Machine Learning Surrogates:** Some researchers train neural networks offline to replace the RT engine entirely. While these surrogates are fast during the optimization phase, they require a massive dataset of pre-simulated RT results to train effectively. Generating this training data is often just as computationally expensive as the optimization problem itself, making it difficult to adapt to new environments.

This research addresses these limitations by introducing the Surrogate-assisted Grey Wolf Optimizer with Individual Memory (SGWO-IM), a framework that reduces computational overhead while simultaneously improving the precision of the calibrated parameters.

## The SGWO-IM Methodology

The Grey Wolf Optimizer (GWO) is a nature-inspired algorithm that mimics the social hierarchy and hunting behavior of grey wolves. In a standard GWO, the population is led by three elite individuals: $\alpha$ (the best), $\beta$ (the second best), and $\delta$ (the third best). The rest of the wolves (the $\omega$ population) update their positions based on these leaders to converge on a target.

The proposed SGWO-IM algorithm enhances this foundation through two key innovations: an online surrogate model for efficiency and a dynamic search mechanism for accuracy. The core objective of the optimizer is to minimize the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) between the simulated path loss $ L_{RT}^m $ and the measured path loss $ L_{\text{Meas}}^m $ across $ M $ measurement points:

$$
RMSE = \sqrt{\frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^{M} (L_{RT}^m - L_{\text{Meas}}^m)^2}
$$

Unlike static AI approaches, the SGWO-IM framework does not require a pre-existing dataset. Instead, it builds its own surrogate model "on the fly" during the first few iterations of the optimization, creating a highly efficient feedback loop.

## Computational Efficiency through Online Surrogates

The most significant bottleneck in EM calibration is the execution time of the Ray Tracing engine. In standard optimizers, if the population size is $ N = 30 $, the RT engine must run 30 times per iteration. The SGWO-IM introduces an online surrogate model based on a Radial Basis Function (RBF) neural network to mitigate this.

The process functions in two distinct phases:
*   **Initial Training Phase:** For the first few iterations (typically the first 5), the algorithm behaves like a standard GWO. All $ N $ individuals are evaluated using the actual RT engine. This data—pairs of EM parameters and their resulting RMSE—is stored in a historical database.
*   **Surrogate-Assisted Phase:** Once enough data is collected, the RBF network is trained online. In subsequent iterations, the optimizer uses the RBF model to *predict* the RMSE for all $ N $ individuals. This prediction is nearly instantaneous. The algorithm then selects only the top $ K $ "elite" individuals (where $ K < N $) based on these predictions. Only these $ K $ individuals are then sent to the actual RT engine for precise evaluation.

By setting $ K = 5 $ for a population $ N = 30 $, the framework reduces the number of expensive RT calls by approximately 62.5% in the later stages of the optimization. This selective evaluation ensures that computational resources are focused only on the most promising regions of the parameter space.

## Enhancing Search Accuracy and Global Exploration

A common failure mode of swarm algorithms is "premature convergence," where the population rushes toward a local minimum and loses the diversity needed to find the true global optimum. To prevent this, SGWO-IM implements a dynamic search mechanism.

First, it replaces the standard linear convergence factor with a non-linear cosine convergence factor $ a(t) $:

$$
a(t) = 2 \cdot \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} \cdot \frac{t}{T_{\text{max}}}\right)
$$

Where $ t $ is the current iteration and $ T_{\text{max}} $ is the maximum number of iterations. This allows for a wider, more aggressive search in the early stages, followed by a rapid narrowing to fine-tune the results as the optimization nears its end.

Second, the algorithm incorporates an "Individual Best Memory" (Pbest) mechanism. In standard GWO, wolves only follow the group leaders. SGWO-IM allows each individual wolf to remember its own personal best discovery $ \vec{X}_{\text{Pbest}}(t) $. The position of a wolf at time $ t+1 $ is a weighted fusion of the group's guidance $ \vec{X}_{\text{GWO}}(t+1) $ and its individual memory:

$$
\vec{X}_{\text{new}}(t+1) = (1-w(t)) \cdot \vec{X}_{\text{GWO}}(t+1) + w(t) \cdot \vec{X}_{\text{Pbest}}(t)
$$

The memory weight $ w(t) $ is also dynamic, increasing as the simulation progresses:

$$
w(t) = w_{\text{min}} + (w_{\text{max}} - w_{\text{min}}) \cdot \left(\frac{t}{T_{\text{max}}}\right)^2
$$

This ensures that the population maintains spatial diversity, preventing the "huddling" effect that leads to suboptimal calibration.

## Real-World Validation and Results

To test the algorithm, the researchers conducted experiments in a complex urban street environment in Qingdao, China. The scenario involved vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communication at a carrier frequency of 4 GHz. Data was collected at 40 distinct receiver points using specialized signal generators and spectrum analyzers.

![path-loss-comparison](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12872v1/picture/PLNEW1.png)

The results demonstrated clear advantages for the SGWO-IM approach:
*   **Accuracy:** The final calibrated model achieved an RMSE of 2.97 dB, compared to 3.65 dB for the standard GWO. This improvement of 0.68 dB is significant in high-precision channel modeling. As shown in {fig:path-loss-comparison}, SGWO-IM was particularly effective in "high-loss regions," where standard algorithms often deviate by several decibels.
*   **Efficiency:** The total execution time was reduced from 831.68 seconds to 336.51 seconds—a 59.5% reduction. The number of real RT simulation calls dropped from 600 to 225.

![efficiency-and-rmse-curves](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12872v1/picture/Efficiency.png)

The calibrated relative permittivities for concrete, glass, and soil were found to be 5.82, 4.65, and 4.21, respectively. These values differed from the initial ITU-standard estimates, highlighting the necessity of environment-specific calibration to achieve high-fidelity digital twins.

## Significance for Future Wireless Networks

The SGWO-IM framework provides a practical solution for the construction of 6G digital twin channels. By successfully balancing efficiency and accuracy, it removes the computational barrier that previously made large-scale RT calibration difficult. 

The significance of this work extends to the development of 6G foundation models. Just as Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT require massive text datasets, 6G environment intelligence models like ChannelGPT require massive amounts of high-fidelity channel data. The ability to efficiently calibrate and generate this data using digital twins is a prerequisite for the next generation of proactive wireless optimization, network planning, and spectrum management. Through the use of online surrogates and dynamic search mechanisms, this research offers a pathway toward more responsive and intelligent wireless environments.
