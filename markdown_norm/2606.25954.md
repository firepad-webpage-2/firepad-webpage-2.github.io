## The Problem of Measurable Majorities

In the study of collective decision-making, we often encounter situations where a group of voters must reach a consensus based on a majority rule. A "social decision frame" is a formal way to represent these scenarios, consisting of a finite set of voters $W$ and a collection of subsets $M$ that are considered "strict majorities." Usually, we assume that the number of voters is even, $|W| = 2n$, and that any set containing more than half the voters is a majority. However, the exact definition of a majority can be more nuanced, especially when we consider whether qualitative judgments (like "set $A$ is a majority") can be represented by a quantitative measure.

A social decision frame $(W, M)$ is said to be "measurable" if there exists a finitely additive probability measure $\mu$ such that for any subset $A \subseteq W$, the judgment is consistent with the measure:

$$
A \in M \iff \mu(A) > 1/2
$$

In this context, the measure $\mu$ assigns a weight to each voter such that the sum of weights is 1, and every voter has a positive weight $\mu(\{v\}) > 0$. If such a measure exists, the frame is "coherent." If no such measure can be found, the frame is "incoherent." Incoherence implies that the qualitative assignments of "majority status" are structurally contradictory; there is no way to assign weights to voters that would produce the specified set of majorities.

A central question in the logic of social choice is whether the class of these measurable frames can be described by a finite set of rules or axioms. This paper demonstrates that they cannot. The authors show that the structural complexity required to expose incoherence can grow without bound, meaning no finite set of axioms can ever fully capture the requirements for measurability.

## Coherence and the Incoherence Index

To understand why some frames are incoherent, researchers look for "balanced sequences" of sets. A frame $(W, M)$ is coherent if it satisfies a specific condition related to how often voters are covered by a sequence of majority sets. Specifically, if we have a sequence of subsets $A_1, \dots, A_m$ where each set is either a majority or a "tie," and the total number of times any voter appears in these sets is at most $m/2$, then the sequence must be "perfectly balanced."

In a perfectly balanced sequence, every voter is covered exactly $m/2$ times. Formally, if $\mathbb{1}_{A_i}$ is the indicator function for set $A_i$ (equal to 1 if a voter is in $A$ and 0 otherwise), the condition for a sequence of length $m$ is:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^m \mathbb{1}_{A_i} \leq \frac{m}{2} \mathbb{1}_W
$$

If a frame $(W, M)$ is incoherent, there must exist some sequence of majorities that satisfies this inequality but is not perfectly balanced (or consists of sets that are not allowed to be majorities under the measure). The "incoherence index" of a frame is defined as the length $m$ of the shortest sequence that exposes this structural contradiction. 

Historically, similar concepts were studied for comparative probability. This paper shifts the focus to strict majorities and investigates the "minimal logic" required to reason about them. A key goal was to determine if the incoherence index is bounded by some constant or if it can be arbitrarily large. If the index is bounded, we could potentially write down a finite list of axioms to describe measurable frames. If it is unbounded, the axiomatization must be infinite.

## The Geometric Methodology

The researchers use a geometric approach to solve this problem, translating set-theoretic properties into linear algebra. Instead of standard 0-1 indicator vectors, they utilize "bipolar indicator vectors." For a subset $A \subseteq W$, the vector $x_A \in \{-1, 1\}^W$ is defined such that:

$$
x_A(v) = 1 \text{ if } v \in A \text{ and } -1 \text{ if } v \notin A
$$

This transformation is mathematically convenient because the vector for a set $A$ and its complement $W \setminus A$ are simply negatives of each other: $x_A = -x_{W \setminus A}$. Most importantly, the condition for a sequence of sets to be perfectly balanced (covering each voter exactly $m/2$ times) simplifies to the condition that their bipolar vectors sum to the zero vector:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^m x_{A_i} = 0
$$

By working in the rational vector space $\mathbb{Q}^W$, the authors can treat the search for incoherent frames as a problem of finding linear dependencies among vectors in a Boolean hypercube. They focus on "maximal standard frames," which are frames where any set larger than half the universe is a majority, any set smaller is not, and for sets of exactly half the size, exactly one of the set or its complement is chosen as a majority. This eliminates the complexity of "ties" and allows them to isolate the behavior of the middle layer of subsets.

## The Core Construction: Dictator Blocs

The main result of the paper is the construction of a sequence of frames $M_k$ (indexed by an integer $k \geq 1$) where the incoherence index increases as $k$ increases. 

The construction starts with a base set of "points" $V_k = \{1, \dots, 2k+2\}$. The actual universe of voters $W_k$ is defined as the set of all subsets of $V_k$ that have exactly $k+1$ elements. The number of voters is thus the central binomial coefficient:

$$
|W_k| = \binom{2k+2}{k+1}
$$

To create the majority sets, the authors define "dictator blocs." For each point $j \in V_k$, the dictator bloc $M_{k,j}$ consists of all voters (subsets of $V_k$) that contain the element $j$:

$$
M_{k,j} = \{ v \in W_k : j \in v \}
$$

Each of these dictator blocs contains exactly half the voters: $|M_{k,j}| = \binom{2k+1}{k} = \frac{1}{2} |W_k|$. Because they are exactly half the size, they sit in the "middle layer" of the power set. Crucially, the sum of the bipolar vectors $y_{k,j}$ corresponding to these dictator blocs is exactly zero:

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{2k+2} y_{k,j} = 0
$$

This means the set of dictator blocs forms a perfectly balanced sequence of length $2k+2$. The challenge was to prove that this sequence is the *shortest* possible balanced sequence in a frame where these blocs are the only "problematic" sets.

## Resolving Axiomatizability

The authors prove two critical lemmas to ensure their construction works as intended. First, they show that any perfectly balanced sequence made only from these dictator blocs must have a length that is a multiple of $2k+2$. This establishes that $2k+2$ is the "minimal" complexity for this specific core.

Second, they use a "Linear Intersection Lemma" to prove that the linear span of these dictator bloc vectors does not contain any other vectors from the middle layer (except for the blocs themselves and their negatives). They then use a hyperplane-avoidance argument to find a specific rational vector $u^*_k$ that is orthogonal to the dictator blocs but not orthogonal to any other middle-layer vectors:

$$
u^*_k \cdot y_{k,j} = 0
$$

By using $u^*_k$ to "break ties" for all other sets in the middle layer, they construct a frame $M_k$ where the *only* perfectly balanced sequence of majorities is the one composed of the dictator blocs. Since the length of this sequence is $2k+2$, the incoherence index of the frame $M_k$ is exactly $2k+2$.

As $k$ can be any positive integer, the incoherence index can be arbitrarily large. This provides the answer to the main question: because there is no upper bound on the length of the sequences required to prove incoherence, the property of being "measurable" cannot be captured by a finite set of axioms. In any finite logic, there would always be a frame whose incoherence is too complex for the axioms to detect.

## Significance and Broader Impact

This research has several major implications for the formal study of voting and measurement theory:

1.  **Axiomatic Limits:** It establishes a fundamental limit on the expressive power of finite axiom systems. If we want a logic that can perfectly distinguish between measurable and non-measurable majorities, that logic must include an infinite number of rules or a rule schema that accounts for all possible sequence lengths.
2.  **Geometric Clarity:** By providing a purely geometric proof, the authors offer a new way to visualize the "boundary" of representability. They show that incoherence is not just a combinatorial fluke but a property deeply embedded in the linear dependencies of subsets within vector spaces.
3.  **Comparison to Prior Work:** This work refines and proves conjectures from the authors' previous research (2026). It also complements recent combinatorial findings by other researchers (e.g., Blanco, 2026), who used different methods to reach similar conclusions about the unboundedness of the index. While the geometric method used here requires a very large number of voters (scaling exponentially with $k$), it is prized for its "first-principles" transparency.
4.  **Future Extensions:** The techniques developed here—mapping sets to rational vector spaces and using hyperplane avoidance to construct tie-breaking rules—could be applied to other voting rules. For example, researchers could investigate "super-majority" rules (like 2/3 requirements) to see if they also exhibit this infinite axiomatic complexity.

In summary, the paper proves that the "measurable" world of majority voting is infinitely more complex than it might appear on the surface, requiring an infinite sequence of conditions to fully describe its structure.
