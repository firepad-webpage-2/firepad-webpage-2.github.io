## Modeling Non-Stationary Climate Risk in Finance

Traditional actuarial and financial models often rely on the assumption of "stationarity"—the idea that the statistical properties of catastrophic events, such as their frequency and severity, remain stable over time. However, the increasing consensus from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) indicates that human-induced climate change is fundamentally altering these patterns. As global temperatures rise, historical loss data becomes a less reliable predictor for future risk, creating a significant gap in the pricing of Insurance-Linked Securities (ILS) and reinsurance treaties.

The research by Karimi and Shokrollahi addresses this challenge by developing a climate-aware pricing framework. Instead of treating climate change as a static parameter adjustment, the authors embed stochastic climate dynamics directly into the valuation engine. By linking catastrophe arrival rates to a time-varying climate index, they provide a method to price Excess-of-Loss (XL) reinsurance and Catastrophe (CAT) bonds that accounts for both gradual warming trends and climate volatility.

## The Stochastic Climate Engine

At the core of this framework is a temperature-related climate index, $X_t$, which represents global or regional temperature anomalies. Unlike simpler models that might assume a straight-line increase in temperature, this framework models $X_t$ as a mean-reverting Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process. This mathematical structure allows the index to fluctuate while being pulled back toward a long-term mean that itself is shifting due to global warming. The dynamics of the climate index are defined as:

$$
dX_t = \kappa (\theta(t) - X_t) dt + \sigma_X dW_t
$$

In this equation, $\kappa$ represents the speed at which the temperature returns to its mean, $\sigma_X$ denotes the climate volatility, and $W_t$ is a standard Wiener process representing random shocks. The "moving target" or mean level $\theta(t)$ is modeled as a linear warming trend:

$$
\theta(t) = \theta_0 + \theta_1 t
$$

where $\theta_1$ captures the rate of deterministic warming over time. By calibrating these parameters to historical NASA GISTEMP data, the model reflects the actual physical trajectory of global temperatures.

## From Temperature to Catastrophe Arrivals

To translate climate changes into financial risk, the authors utilize a Cox process, also known as a doubly stochastic Poisson process. In a standard Poisson process, the rate of event arrivals is constant. In a Cox process, however, the arrival intensity $\lambda_t$ is itself a stochastic process that depends on the climate index $X_t$. The relationship is modeled exponentially:

$$
\lambda_t = \lambda_0 \exp(\beta X_t) \text{ where } \lambda_0 > 0, \beta \geq 0
$$

Here, $\lambda_0$ is the baseline arrival rate and $\beta$ is the climate-sensitivity parameter. This exponential form is particularly important because it implies a nonlinear response: as the climate index $X_t$ increases, the frequency of catastrophes grows at an accelerating rate. This captures the compounding effect where even small increases in average temperature can lead to significant jumps in the probability of extreme weather events.

The total aggregate loss $S_T$ at maturity $T$ is then calculated by summing individual claim severities $Y_i$ over the number of events $N_T$ that occurred:

$$
S_T = \sum_{i=1}^{N_T} Y_i
$$

The researchers assume that severities $Y_i$ follow a lognormal distribution. They also extend the model to allow these severities to be climate-dependent, suggesting that when the climate is in an adverse state, individual disasters are not only more frequent but also potentially more severe.

## Valuation in Incomplete Markets

Pricing catastrophe-linked instruments presents a unique challenge: the market is "incomplete." Unlike stocks or bonds, catastrophe risk cannot be perfectly hedged or replicated using other liquid financial instruments. Consequently, there is no single "arbitrage-free" price.

To navigate this, the paper adopts a reduced-form risk-adjusted valuation approach. This involves shifting from the physical probability measure $P$ (the real-world likelihood of events) to a risk-adjusted pricing measure $Q$. Under this new measure, parameters are adjusted to reflect the risk premium that investors demand for bearing catastrophe risk. The time-zero value of a contract with a payoff $\Phi(S_T)$ is expressed as:

$$
\Pi_0(\Theta_Q) = e^{-rT} E_Q[\Phi(S_T)]
$$

where $r$ is the risk-free rate and $E_Q$ is the expected value under the pricing measure. The authors specifically analyze two major contract types:

1.  **Excess-of-Loss (XL) Reinsurance:** This contract covers losses that exceed a specific attachment point $D$, up to a maximum limit $L$. The payoff is:
    $$
    \Phi_{XL}(S_T) = \min((S_T - D)^+, L)
    $$
2.  **CAT Bonds:** These are high-yield debt instruments. If the aggregate loss $S_T$ stays below a trigger $K$, the investor receives the face value $F$. If the trigger is breached, the principal is lost. The payoff is modeled as:
    $$
    \Phi_{CAT}(S_T) = F \mathbf{1}_{\{S_T < K\}}
    $$

## Distributional Shifts and Economic Impact

By comparing the climate-aware model to a "stationary benchmark" (which assumes a constant catastrophe rate based on initial conditions), the research highlights significant discrepancies in risk assessment.

![aggregate-loss-distribution-comparison](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.14830v1/fig2_loss_dist.png)
*{fig:aggregate-loss-distribution-comparison}: The probability density of aggregate losses under the climate-adjusted model shows a significantly heavier right tail compared to the stationary benchmark, indicating a higher probability of extreme losses.*

The numerical results indicate that ignoring climate trends leads to a material underestimation of risk:
*   **Premiums:** The XL reinsurance premium is approximately $11\%$ higher in the climate-aware model.
*   **Spreads:** CAT bond yield spreads are nearly $30\%$ wider (810 bps vs. 625 bps in the benchmark), as investors require more compensation for the non-stationary risk.
*   **Tail Risk:** The model predicts heavier "tails" in the loss distribution. The 99.5% Tail Value-at-Risk (TVaR)—a critical metric for Solvency II regulatory capital—is roughly $13.7\%$ higher than what stationary models suggest. This implies that insurance companies using traditional models might be holding insufficient capital to survive extreme climate-related events.

## Sensitivity and Long-Term Trends

A key takeaway of the study is that the impact of climate change is not uniform across all financial instruments. The divergence between climate-aware and stationary pricing accelerates as the contract maturity increases.

![evolution-of-pricing-ratio-over-maturity](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.14830v1/longterm.png)
*{fig:evolution-of-pricing-ratio-over-maturity}: The ratio between climate-aware and stationary CAT bond prices decreases sharply as maturity extends, showing that long-term instruments are far more vulnerable to climate trend misspecification.*

For a 1-year contract, the price difference might be negligible ($1.8\%$), but for a 20-year horizon, the climate-aware price can be more than $12\%$ lower than the stationary estimate. This maturity dependence is vital for pension funds and long-term institutional investors who hold multi-year catastrophe exposures.

Furthermore, the paper explores the "super-additive" effect of climate sensitivity. When both the frequency and the severity of events are linked to the temperature index, the resulting tail risk increases much faster than if only one factor were affected. This interaction emphasizes that climate risk is multifaceted and requires integrated modeling to be fully understood.

## Significance for Risk Management

The framework provided by Karimi and Shokrollahi offers a tractable way to integrate physical climate science into financial valuation. By distinguishing between climate dynamics (like mean reversion and warming trends) and catastrophe-specific parameters (like sensitivity $\beta$), the model allows for flexible scenario analysis.

![cat-bond-spreads-vs-warming-trend](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.14830v1/figure7_corrected.png)
*{fig:cat-bond-spreads-vs-warming-trend}: As the deterministic warming trend ($\theta_1$) intensifies, CAT bond spreads increase linearly, providing a direct link between physical warming rates and financial market requirements.*

The study concludes that stationary assumptions are no longer viable for robust risk management in an era of persistent environmental change. By adopting climate-dependent Cox processes, insurers and investors can better quantify climate risk premia, ensure regulatory capital adequacy, and protect against the long-term systemic threats posed by a warming planet. This research sets a foundation for future extensions, such as incorporating spatially resolved climate indices or modeling portfolios affected by multiple types of climate-related perils.
