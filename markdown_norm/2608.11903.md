## Submarine Cables as Planetary-Scale Sensors

Earthquake early warning (EEW) systems are critical for mitigating the impact of seismic events and protecting lives and infrastructure. Historically, these systems have relied on terrestrial networks of seismometers. However, a significant portion of the Earth’s surface—specifically the ocean floor—remains sparsely monitored. Installing and maintaining dedicated seismic sensors in the deep ocean is prohibitively expensive and logistically challenging, leaving gaps in our ability to detect submarine earthquakes and subsequent tsunamis.

To address this challenge, recent research has focused on repurposing the extensive network of submarine optical communication fibers. There are currently over 1.2 million kilometers of these cables crisscrossing the ocean floor, forming a global infrastructure that can potentially serve as a planetary-scale sensor array. When an earthquake occurs, the resulting ground motion induces physical changes in the fiber cables. By monitoring the signals already being transmitted for global internet traffic, researchers can detect these subtle seismic signatures.

This paper addresses a fundamental hurdle in this field: the lack of data. Significant seismic events that intersect with specific cable sections are rare, and accessing proprietary data from cable operators is often difficult. The authors present an end-to-end simulation model designed to generate realistic, earthquake-perturbed optical data. This tool allows researchers to simulate how various earthquakes affect submarine fibers under different conditions, providing a foundation for developing and testing new sensing algorithms without waiting for a natural disaster to occur.

## Physical Principles of Earthquake Sensing

The core mechanism for detecting seismic activity through optical fibers lies in the property of birefringence. In an ideal optical fiber, the two orthogonal polarizations of light travel at the same speed. However, real fibers possess inherent, random imperfections that make one axis slightly "faster" than the other. This polarization-dependence of light speed is called birefringence, denoted as $b$.

When seismic waves propagate through the seafloor, they create time-varying pressure and strain on the cable. This strain slightly deforms the fiber’s geometry, modulating its birefringence. As a result, the State of Polarization (SOP) of the light exiting the fiber changes. By monitoring these SOP fluctuations—represented mathematically as a normalized real Stokes vector $\vec{S}$—researchers can work backward to infer the characteristics of the earthquake.

Unlike previous models that focused on terrestrial fibers, this work uses the coupled Schrödinger equation (CSE) to describe the propagation of a dual-polarization complex signal envelope $\vec{A}(z, t')$ along the fiber length $z$ and time $t'$. This choice is significant because it resolves deterministic, time-dependent perturbations at the carrier frequency, whereas the Manakov-PMD equations used in earlier terrestrial studies typically predict no SOP perturbations at the carrier frequency during seismic events.

## The Geophysical Simulation Pipeline

The model follows a multi-stage pipeline that translates a geological event into a measurable optical signal. 

1. **Ground Displacement Synthesis**: The process begins by obtaining ground displacement data $u_k(t)$ for discrete segments along the fiber. The authors utilize Syngine, a web service that synthesizes seismograms from historical earthquake parameters or hypothetical scenarios.
2. **Pressure Conversion**: For submarine environments, the vertical ground displacement $u_k(t)$ is converted into water pressure changes $\Delta P_k(t)$. Assuming the ocean water is incompressible, the relationship is defined by:
$$\Delta P_k(t) = \rho h \frac{\partial^2 u_k(t)}{\partial t^2}$$
where $\rho$ is the water density and $h$ is the water depth.
3. **Frequency-Dependent Filtering**: The model incorporates a critical step that accounts for the ocean's behavior. Real-world ocean dynamics do not follow the incompressible assumption at all frequencies. The authors apply a pressure filter derived from experimental seafloor measurements to reflect how the water column attenuates or amplifies different frequencies of seismic motion.
4. **Strain Mapping**: Finally, the filtered pressure is mapped to the fiber strain $\epsilon_k(t)$ using the following relationship:
$$\epsilon_k(t) = \alpha \xi \epsilon_P \Delta P_k(t)$$
Here, $\xi$ is the photoelasticity factor, $\epsilon_P$ scales pressure to strain, and $\alpha$ is a calibration factor used to account for environmental uncertainties.

## Optical Propagation Modeling

To simulate the light traveling through the perturbed fiber, the authors divide the cable into $K$ small steps, each of length $\Delta z$. Each step $k$ introduces a random rotation of the fiber's fast axis and a phase shift due to birefringence. 

The inherent randomness of the fiber is modeled using a Gaussian random walk for the rotation angle $\theta_k$:
$$\theta_k - \theta_{k-1} \sim N(0, \Delta z / (2L_{\text{corr}}))$$
where $L_{\text{corr}}$ is the correlation length of the fiber. 

During an earthquake, the nominal birefringence $b = \pi/L_{\text{beat}}$ is modulated by the strain $\epsilon_k(t)$. The time-dependent birefringence $b_k(t')$ at each step is calculated as:
$$b_k(t') = (1 + \epsilon_k(t' + \beta_1 k\Delta z))b$$
where $\beta_1$ is the polarization-averaged group velocity. This allows the model to capture how the earthquake wave, which moves much slower than light, affects the light pulse as it traverses different parts of the cable at different times.

The numerical implementation requires a very small step size $\Delta z$ (e.g., 1.67 meters) to accurately capture these variations, leading to a computational runtime of several hours on high-performance GPUs like the NVIDIA Tesla V100 for a 10,000 km cable.

## Validation: The 2020 Oaxaca Earthquake

To validate the model, the researchers simulated the 2020 Oaxaca earthquake ($M_w = 7.4$) and its impact on the Curie submarine cable, which connects Chile to the United States. This specific event was chosen because experimental data from the actual cable was publicly available for comparison.

The simulation used parameters specific to the Curie link, including a total length of 10,429 km, a water depth $h$ of 4 km, and a beat length $L_{\text{beat}}$ of 50 m. The output of the simulation—the Stokes parameters—was processed using the same methodology as the experimental data to create comparable spectrograms.

## Results and Identified Discrepancies

The results demonstrate that the inclusion of the frequency-dependent pressure filter is essential for accuracy. The filtered model successfully reproduced the low-frequency "bandgap" (0.02 Hz to 0.1 Hz) and the broad band of fluctuations (0.1 Hz to 0.4 Hz) observed in the Curie cable data. A quantitative comparison using normalized cross-correlation of spectral centroids yielded a value of 0.70, indicating a strong alignment in the overall spectral pattern.

However, the model also revealed several discrepancies:
- **Attenuation Rate**: The simulated SOP fluctuations decayed significantly faster than the measured data. While the measured signal persisted for over 30 minutes, the simulation returned to baseline much earlier.
- **Amplitude**: The initial amplitude of the perturbations was higher in the simulation than in reality, likely due to uncertainties in the local seafloor environment or cable deployment.
- **Harmonics**: The experimental data showed harmonic components at 0.4 Hz and 0.6 Hz that the model did not replicate. The authors suggest these might be caused by separate environmental factors, such as ocean surface waves, which were not included in the earthquake-centric model.

These mismatches highlight the complexity of the submarine environment. The authors note that while their model focuses on vertical pressure, other mechanisms—such as the longitudinal pulling of the cable due to seafloor shifting—likely play a role in the real-world signal.

## Significance for Future Research

This work provides the first end-to-end open-source modeling framework for earthquake-induced polarization perturbations in submarine cables. Its significance lies in its utility as a platform for algorithmic development. By generating synthetic but physically grounded datasets, researchers can now:

1.  **Benchmarking**: Compare the performance of different earthquake detection and localization algorithms under identical, controlled conditions.
2.  **Sensitivity Analysis**: Explore how cable characteristics (like beat length or depth) and earthquake parameters (like magnitude or distance) affect the detectability of seismic events.
3.  **Optimization**: Inform the design of future sensing systems, helping to identify which sections of the global cable network are most sensitive to specific types of seismic activity.

By making the implementation open source, the authors facilitate a collaborative approach to refining these models. Future iterations are expected to incorporate more sophisticated mechanisms, such as longitudinal strain and analytical pressure filters, further closing the gap between simulation and the complex reality of the seafloor. This research brings the community closer to a reliable, global earthquake and tsunami early warning system built upon the world's existing fiber-optic infrastructure.
