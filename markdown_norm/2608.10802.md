The field of magnonics explores the use of spin waves—and their elementary particles, magnons—to carry and process information without the movement of electric charges. By eliminating the Joule heating associated with electron flow, magnonic devices offer a potential path toward low-power, high-speed computation. A central challenge in this field is finding materials that allow for the efficient generation, transport, and control of these magnon currents. Recently, two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals (vdW) magnets have emerged as a versatile platform for these studies due to their tunable magnetic properties and reduced dimensionality.

One such material is $CrPS_4$, a monoclinic vdW antiferromagnet. While many studies have focused on isotropic materials, $CrPS_4$ possesses a significant degree of intrinsic crystalline anisotropy. This means that its physical properties, such as magnetic exchange coupling and thermal conductivity, differ depending on the direction within the crystal lattice. Understanding how this anisotropy influences the flow of magnons is crucial for developing directional magnonic devices.

This research specifically investigates how magnon spin transport in $CrPS_4$ varies along its two primary in-plane crystallographic axes: the $a$-axis and the $b$-axis. By comparing magnons excited electrically via the spin Hall effect with those excited thermally via the spin Seebeck effect, the study reveals that intrinsic crystalline anisotropy acts as a powerful control parameter for spin current propagation. Furthermore, it addresses a critical technical challenge: how to accurately measure the magnon spin diffusion length when heat flow—which is also anisotropic—is involved.

## Magnetic and Structural Properties of $CrPS_4$

$CrPS_4$ is characterized as an A-type antiferromagnetic insulator. In this arrangement, the magnetic moments of the chromium ($Cr$) atoms are aligned ferromagnetically within each individual layer, but these layers are coupled antiferromagnetically to their neighbors. The crystal structure is monoclinic (space group $P2/c$), which breaks the symmetry in the $a$-$b$ plane.

The anisotropy in $CrPS_4$ is primarily driven by its exchange coupling constants. As shown in {fig:figure-1-a-crystal-structure-of-crps4-showing-the-monoclinic-arrangement-and-different-exchange-coupling-constants-j-1-j-2-and-j-3-b-scanning-electron-microscopy-sem-images-of-devices-with-pt-strips-oriented-along-the-a-and-b-axes-c-schematic-of-the-nonlocal-magnon-transport-measurement-setup}a, the interactions between $Cr$ atoms are described by different exchange parameters: $J_1$, $J_2$, and $J_3$. These parameters quantify the strength of the magnetic interaction between neighboring spins. Because the distances between atoms along the $a$-axis and $b$-axis are different ($5.43\text{ \AA}$ vs. $3.55\text{ \AA}$ and $3.71\text{ \AA}$), the exchange stiffness—a measure of how much energy it takes to twist the magnetic alignment—is also direction-dependent. This microscopic structural difference is the fundamental origin of the macroscopic transport anisotropy explored in this work.

Under an external magnetic field, $CrPS_4$ undergoes a magnetic phase transition known as a spin-flip transition. Below a critical field (approximately $6\text{ T}$), the system remains in its antiferromagnetic state, where magnon transport is often suppressed. Above this field, the spins align with the external field in a collinear state, which facilitates the long-range diffusion of magnons.

## Experimental Setup for Nonlocal Transport

To measure magnon transport, the researchers used a "nonlocal" geometry. In this setup, several platinum (Pt) strips are fabricated on top of a thin flake of $CrPS_4$. These strips act as either "injectors" or "detectors."

When an electric current is passed through a Pt injector, two distinct processes occur:
1.  **Electrical Excitation:** The spin Hall effect (SHE) in the Pt creates a spin accumulation at the interface with the $CrPS_4$. This accumulation exerts a torque on the $Cr$ spins, creating a non-equilibrium population of magnons that diffuse through the material.
2.  **Thermal Excitation:** The current causes Joule heating in the Pt strip, creating a temperature gradient in the $CrPS_4$. This gradient drives magnons away from the hot injector via the spin Seebeck effect (SSE).

At a separate Pt detector strip located a distance $d$ away, these magnons are converted back into an electrical voltage via the inverse spin Hall effect (iSHE). By measuring the first-harmonic resistance ($R_{NL}^{1\omega}$) and the second-harmonic resistance ($R_{NL}^{2\omega}$), the researchers can distinguish between electrically and thermally generated magnons, respectively.



## Anisotropy in Electrically Generated Magnons

The most direct evidence for transport anisotropy comes from the first-harmonic measurements ($R_{NL}^{1\omega}$), which represent magnons excited by pure spin accumulation. The researchers found that the efficiency of magnon transport depends heavily on whether the magnons travel along the $a$-axis or the $b$-axis.

By measuring the signal at various distances $d$ between the injector and detector, the magnon spin diffusion length ($\lambda_m$)—the average distance a magnon travels before decaying—and the magnon spin conductivity ($\sigma_m$) can be extracted. The relationship between the nonlocal resistance and these parameters is described by the diffusion model:

$$
R_{NL} = \frac{\sigma_m t_{CrPS_4} \eta_{Pt}^2}{\lambda_m} \text{csch}\left(\frac{d}{\lambda_m}\right)
$$

where $t_{CrPS_4}$ is the flake thickness and $\eta_{Pt}$ is a conversion coefficient characterizing the Pt electrodes.

![figure-4-distance-dependence-of-the-normalized-nonlocal-first-harmonic-resistance-r-nl-1-omega-along-a-the-a-axis-and-b-the-b-axis-the-dashed-lines-represent-fits-to-the-diffusion-model-showing-a-significantly-longer-diffusion-length-for-transport-along-the-b-axis](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10802v1/x4.png)

The results, shown in Figure 4, reveal a stark contrast:
*   **Along the a-axis:** The signal decays relatively quickly with distance, yielding a magnon spin diffusion length of $\lambda_m^a \approx 211 \pm 72\text{ nm}$.
*   **Along the b-axis:** The signal persists over much longer distances. In fact, the decay is so slow (following an approximately $1/d$ trend) that only a lower bound could be determined: $\lambda_m^b \geq 575\text{ nm}$.

Quantitatively, the diffusion length along the $b$-axis is at least $2.72 \pm 0.96$ times longer than along the $a$-axis. Similarly, the magnon spin conductivity was found to be anisotropic, with a ratio $\sigma_m^b / \sigma_m^a \approx 2.22 \pm 0.90$. 

This confirms that the intrinsic crystal structure of $CrPS_4$ dictates how easily magnons can move through it. The $b$-axis, which features closer $Cr$-$Cr$ spacing and stronger exchange coupling, acts as a "fast lane" for magnon transport. This finding is significant because it suggests that just by orienting a device relative to the crystal axes, one can double the conductivity and triple the reach of a spin signal.

## Transverse Spin Currents and Magnon Conductivity

A fascinating implication of this anisotropic conductivity is the possibility of generating transverse magnon currents. In isotropic materials, a gradient in the magnon population (a magnon chemical potential gradient) only drives a current in the same direction as the gradient. However, in an anisotropic medium like $CrPS_4$, the magnon spin conductivity is a tensor rather than a single number. 

If the crystal axes are not perfectly aligned with the injector electrodes, a longitudinal gradient can generate a transverse magnon current. This effect is mathematically analogous to the electrical planar Hall effect but occurs entirely within the magnonic system. It provides a method to "steer" spin currents by leveraging the internal symmetry of the crystal, offering a potential tool for routing information in complex magnonic circuits.



## The Complexity of Thermal Magnon Transport

While electrical measurements provided a clear picture of transport anisotropy, the results from thermally excited magnons ($R_{NL}^{2\omega}$) were more complex. The researchers observed a much stronger apparent anisotropy in the thermal signals—magnitudes differed by factors as high as 7 between the two axes. However, they caution against interpreting this solely as a difference in magnon transport properties.

The second-harmonic signal depends on the nonlocal spin Seebeck effect (SSE), which is driven by temperature gradients. In an anisotropic material, heat does not flow evenly in all directions. To understand this, the team performed thermometry measurements using the $3\omega$ method and nonlocal sensors.

![figure-7-a-local-3-omega-thermometry-measurements-and-b-nonlocal-thermometry-used-to-determine-the-anisotropy-of-heat-transport-the-results-indicate-that-thermal-conductivity-along-the-a-axis-kappa-aa-is-higher-than-along-the-b-axis-kappa-bb-leading-to-different-temperature-profiles-for-the-same-heating-power](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10802v1/x7.png)

Their findings, illustrated in Figure 7, show that the thermal conductivity ($\kappa$) is itself anisotropic, with $\kappa_{aa} > \kappa_{bb}$. This means that for a given amount of electrical power applied to an injector:
1.  Heat spreads more easily along the $a$-axis, leading to a "shallower" temperature gradient.
2.  Heat is more "trapped" along the $b$-axis, leading to a steeper temperature gradient ($|\nabla_b T| > |\nabla_a T|$).

Because the SSE signal is proportional to the local temperature gradient, the measured $R_{NL}^{2\omega}$ becomes a convolution of the material's thermal properties and its magnon transport properties. A larger thermal signal along the $b$-axis might simply be due to a larger local temperature difference rather than superior magnon diffusion.

This observation is critical for the research community. Many previous studies have used $R_{NL}^{2\omega}$ to estimate the magnon spin diffusion length $\lambda_m$. This work demonstrates that such estimates can be highly unreliable and may lead to significant overestimations if the spatial temperature profile and its anisotropy are not rigorously accounted for. For instance, while prior literature suggested a diffusion length of $1.6\text{ \mu m}$ in $CrPS_4$ based on thermal data, this study's more direct electrical measurements show it is significantly shorter along the $a$-axis.

## Distinguishing Mechanisms: $R_{NL}^{1\omega}$ vs. $R_{NL}^{2\omega}$

The researchers also noted qualitative differences in how these two signals behave. For example, at low temperatures and short distances, the thermal signal ($R_{NL}^{2\omega}$) along the $b$-axis actually reverses its sign. No such reversal is seen in the electrical signal ($R_{NL}^{1\omega}$). 

This suggests that the mechanisms for generating magnons via heat (SSE) and via spin accumulation (SHE) are fundamentally different. The SSE involves a broad spectrum of thermal magnons, whereas the SHE-driven process is more selective. The interplay between the anisotropic magnon dispersion (how magnon energy relates to its momentum) and the temperature-dependent occupancy of those magnon states likely contributes to these diverging behaviors.

## Conclusion and Future Directions

This study establishes that intrinsic crystalline anisotropy in the van der Waals antiferromagnet $CrPS_4$ is a dominant factor in determining magnon spin transport. By isolating the electrical excitation channel, the researchers successfully quantified the anisotropy in both the magnon spin diffusion length and conductivity, showing that transport along the $b$-axis is significantly more efficient than along the $a$-axis.

The significance of this work is twofold. First, it demonstrates that the crystal lattice itself can be used as a design element to control the direction and reach of spin information. This "intrinsic steering" could simplify the architecture of magnonic devices. Second, it provides a vital methodological correction, showing that thermal magnon transport measurements are easily confounded by anisotropic heat flow and should be interpreted with caution.

Looking forward, the monoclinic structure of $CrPS_4$ offers a rich playground for exploring more exotic phenomena. The possibility of using external gates to tune the magnetic state, combined with the material's intrinsic anisotropy, could lead to reconfigurable magnonic logic gates. By aligning or misaligning the direction of magnon propagation with the high-conductivity $b$-axis, researchers may soon be able to dynamically switch the "magnonic resistance" of a device, moving one step closer to practical, energy-efficient spin-based technologies.
