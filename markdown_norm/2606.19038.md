## Understanding Collective Finance and Multi-Agent Markets

Traditional financial mathematics often operates under the "representative agent" paradigm. In this view, the complexities of a market are distilled into a single decision-maker who interacts with a frictionless environment to determine asset prices. This framework is the foundation of the Fundamental Theorems of Asset Pricing (FTAP), which link the absence of arbitrage to the existence of equivalent martingale measures and market completeness to unique pricing.

However, modern financial systems are inherently multi-agent. Different entities—banks, insurance companies, or retail investors—operate with different information, face unique regulatory constraints, and trade in different subsets of assets. "Collective completeness and pricing-hedging duality II" by Alessandro Doldi, Marco Frittelli, and Marco Maggis advances a relatively new field called **collective finance**. This framework explicitly models the interactions between $N \geq 1$ agents who can not only trade individually in their own sub-markets but also engage in cooperative risk-sharing arrangements.

The central innovation of this work is the treatment of "admissible risk exchanges." While previous models often assumed that any risk transfer between agents could be perfectly reversed, this paper models these exchanges as a **finitely generated convex cone**. This shift allows for the representation of directional or asymmetric transfers, such as insurance contracts or limited-liability agreements, where a payment made in one direction does not imply the possibility of a reciprocal payment under the same terms.

## The Collective Market Structure

In this model, time is discrete, spanning $T = \{0, \ldots, T\}$. We consider $N$ agents, where each agent $i$ has their own information set or filtration $\mathcal{F}^i$. Each agent has access to a specific set of assets $X^{(i)}$, and their terminal trading gains form a vector space $K^i \subseteq L_0(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_T^i, P)$.

The agents can also exchange risk. An exchange is represented by a vector $Y = (Y^1, \ldots, Y^N) \in \mathcal{Y} \subset (L_0(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_T, P))^N$, where $Y^i$ is the payoff received by agent $i$. Because these are internal transfers within the group, the net aggregate payoff is zero:
$$\sum_{i=1}^N Y^i = 0$$

The set of all possible exchanges, $\mathcal{Y}$, is assumed to be a finitely generated convex cone. Crucially, it contains deterministic zero-sum transfers, represented by the set $R_0^N = \{ (a_1, \dots, a_N) \in \mathbb{R}^N \mid \sum_{i=1}^N a_i = 0 \} \subseteq \mathcal{Y}$. By using a cone rather than a vector space, the authors acknowledge that cooperation agreements are often "directional." If agents agree to share losses in a specific way, they cannot necessarily "un-share" those losses by simply flipping the sign of the transaction if the contract does not allow it.

## Redefining Arbitrage in a Cooperative Context

The classical definition of arbitrage refers to a "free lunch"—a way to make money without risk. In a multi-agent system, we must consider **No Collective Arbitrage (NCA(Y))**. This condition posits that the agents, even by pooling their trading strategies and their allowed risk exchanges, cannot create a situation where every agent's net payoff is non-negative and at least one agent has a strictly positive payoff with positive probability.

Mathematically, let the aggregate feasibility cone be:
$$K_{\mathcal{Y}} = \prod_{i=1}^N (K^i - L_0^+(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_T^i, P)) + \mathcal{Y}$$

The NCA(Y) condition holds if $Z \in K_{\mathcal{Y}} \cap L_0^+(\Omega, \mathcal{F}_T, P)^N \implies Z = 0 \text{ P-a.s.}$ This ensures the market is "viable." A major technical contribution of this paper is proving that the NCA(Y) condition itself implies that this aggregate cone $K_{\mathcal{Y}}$ is closed. In many prior works, closedness was an assumption; here, it is shown to be a fundamental consequence of the absence of collective arbitrage.

## The Collective First Fundamental Theorem

The First FTAP traditionally connects the absence of arbitrage to the existence of a probability measure that makes asset prices martingales. In the collective setting, this generalizes to a vector of probability measures $Q = (Q^1, \dots, Q^N)$, one for each agent.

The paper proves the **Collective First FTAP**: NCA(Y) holds if and only if the set $M_e(\mathcal{Y})$ is non-empty, where:
$$M_e(\mathcal{Y}) = \{ Q = (Q^1, \dots, Q^N) \mid Q^i \sim P, \mathbb{E}_{Q^i}[k^i] \leq 0, \forall k^i \in K^i, \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}_{Q^i}[Y^i] \leq 0, \forall Y \in \mathcal{Y} \}$$

The inequality $\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}_{Q^i}[Y^i] \leq 0$ reflects the cone structure. Since $Y$ is a cone, we cannot assume the expectation is exactly zero. This condition ensures that the "collective value" of any exchange is not positive under the risk-neutral measures, preventing a collective free lunch.

## Pricing and Hedging Duality

How do we price a vector of contingent claims $f = (f^1, \dots, f^N)$ in such a market? The authors define the **collective superhedging price** $\rho_{\mathcal{Y}}^+(f)$ as the minimum aggregate initial capital needed to guarantee that each agent can cover their claim $f^i$ using individual trading and collective exchanges:
$$\rho_{\mathcal{Y}}^+(f) = \inf \{ \sum_{i=1}^N x_i \mid \exists (k^1, \dots, k^N) \in \prod K^i, Y \in \mathcal{Y} \text{ s.t. } x_i + k^i + Y^i \geq f^i \text{ for each } i \}$$

Theorem 2.18 establishes the **Collective Pricing-Hedging Duality**:
$$\rho_{\mathcal{Y}}^+(f) = \sup_{Q \in M_e(\mathcal{Y})} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}_{Q^i}[f^i]$$

This result is powerful because it links the "physical" problem of super-replication to the "dual" problem of taking expectations under collective martingale measures. However, because $\mathcal{Y}$ is a cone, there is a fundamental asymmetry. Reversing an exchange might not be possible, meaning the superhedging price of a claim and the subhedging price of the same claim do not behave as simply as they do in classical models.

## Strong Replicability and the Quest for Unique Prices

One of the most significant challenges in collective finance is that even "replicable" claims (claims that can be exactly generated by trading and exchanges) might not have a unique price. This happens because some exchanges might have an aggregate value of zero but different individual valuations across agents.

To resolve this, the authors introduce the concept of **strong collective replicability**. They define a restricted cone of exchanges, denoted $\mathfrak{b}\mathcal{Y}$:
$$\mathfrak{b}\mathcal{Y} = \{ Y \in \mathcal{Y} \mid \mathbb{E}_{Q^i}[Y^i] = 0 \text{ for all } i=1, \dots, N, \text{ for all } Q \in M_e(\mathcal{Y}) \}$$

These are "fair" exchanges where every agent's contribution is valued at zero by all valid martingale measures. A claim is strongly collectively replicable if it can be replicated using only exchanges from this restricted set $\mathfrak{b}\mathcal{Y}$. The paper demonstrates that under certain conditions, this strong replicability is exactly what is needed to ensure that the set of arbitrage-free prices, $\Pi(f)$, is a singleton.

## The Collective Second Fundamental Theorem

The classical Second FTAP states that a market is complete if and only if there is a unique equivalent martingale measure. The authors extend this to the collective cone-constrained setting, but they identify a crucial requirement: we must assume not only NCA(Y) but also **NCA(-Y)**. This dual condition implies that no collective arbitrage is possible even if all allowed exchanges were reversed—essentially ensuring the "viability" of the group's cooperative structure in both directions.

Under these conditions (NCA(Y) and NCA(-Y)), the paper establishes the **Collective Second FTAP (CFTAP II)**. It shows that the following are equivalent:
1.  The market is collectively complete (every claim can be replicated).
2.  The vector of measures $M_e(\mathcal{Y})$ is a singleton.
3.  Superhedging and subhedging prices coincide for all claims.

This theorem restores the fundamental link between market completeness and unique pricing, but clarifies that in a multi-agent world with directional transfers, this link requires more stringent "symmetry" in the no-arbitrage conditions than the single-agent case.

## Why This Matters: Economic Implications

The transition from vector spaces to convex cones is not merely a mathematical exercise; it captures the reality of asymmetric financial commitments. For example, consider an insurance arrangement where a parent company agrees to cover the losses of a subsidiary. This is a one-way risk transfer. In a vector space model, the subsidiary would also be assumed capable of covering the parent's losses in the same way, which might be factually impossible or legally prohibited.

By using cones, Doldi, Frittelli, and Maggis provide a framework that respects these institutional and legal boundaries. Their work shows that while these asymmetries complicate the math—leading to situations where the "fair price" might be a range rather than a single number—the fundamental principles of asset pricing still hold if we refine our definitions of replicability and arbitrage.

The introduction of the $\mathfrak{b}\mathcal{Y}$ cone and strong replicability provides a roadmap for how groups can structure their cooperation to ensure stable, unique valuations of their shared risks. This has potential applications in market design, the regulation of financial conglomerates, and the structuring of large-scale risk-sharing pools.
