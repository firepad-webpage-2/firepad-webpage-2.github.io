Understanding the relationship between genetic interventions and their effects on cellular behavior is a foundational goal in functional genomics. Technologies like CRISPR-based perturbation screens coupled with single-cell RNA sequencing (Perturb-seq) have enabled researchers to measure the transcriptional consequences of knocking out or knocking down thousands of genes simultaneously. However, the combinatorial space of possible genetic perturbations and cellular contexts is far too vast to be explored through experiments alone. Consequently, computational models that can predict the effects of "unseen" perturbations—those involving genes that were not present in the training data—are essential for prioritizing future experiments.

Stable-Shift is a perturbation-level modeling framework designed to address this challenge. It specifically focuses on predicting the genome-wide transcriptional response to the perturbation of a gene whose effect has never been observed by the model. By integrating diverse biological priors with a structured, low-rank representation of the transcriptional response, the method provides a mechanism for robustly extrapolating beyond its training set.

## The Challenge of Generalization to Unseen Genes

Predicting the effect of a genetic perturbation is complex because the resulting transcriptional change, often referred to as an "expression shift," is high-dimensional and contains significant noise. In a typical experiment, knocking out a single gene might influence the expression of thousands of other genes.

Current computational approaches often struggle with "unseen-gene" prediction. While models like scGen or CPA (Compositional Perturbation Autoencoder) are proficient at interpolating within a known space of perturbations or reconstructing data for known genes in new contexts, they frequently falter when faced with a gene whose response was entirely absent during training. This is often because these models rely heavily on the expression patterns observed during training, which may not capture the underlying biological principles needed to generalize to a novel functional neighborhood.

The difficulty is compounded by "global expression programs"—large-scale transcriptional changes that are shared across many different perturbations. These shared signals can dominate the data, making it difficult for models to learn the specific, subtle effects of a particular gene. Stable-Shift addresses these issues by defining a rigorous extrapolation task where the training and test genes are strictly disjoint.

## A Low-Rank Basis for Transcriptional Responses

To create a more robust prediction target, Stable-Shift avoids predicting the high-dimensional expression vector directly. Instead, it utilizes a "leakage-controlled" latent response basis derived exclusively from the training data.

The process begins by calculating the average expression shift for each perturbation $i$ in the training set. Let $x_{pert,i}$ be the mean expression of cells where gene $i$ was perturbed, and $x_{ctrl}$ be the mean expression of control (non-targeted) cells. The expression shift is defined as:

$$\Delta_i = x_{pert,i} - x_{ctrl}$$
