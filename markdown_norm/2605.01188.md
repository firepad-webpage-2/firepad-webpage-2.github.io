## The Evolution of Scaling Laws

In the development of large language models (LLMs), scaling laws have served as a vital map for researchers. These empirical rules, established by foundational works like the Chinchilla scaling laws, describe how the performance of a model improves as its size, the amount of training data, and the total compute budget increase. Traditionally, these laws have focused on "tokens" as the fundamental unit of data. A common rule of thumb is to train a model on roughly 20 tokens for every parameter in the model.

However, the definition of a "token" is not universal. Depending on the tokenizer used—whether it is a standard Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) or a character-level system—the amount of information packed into a single token can vary wildly. This information density is measured by the compression rate $T$, which represents the average number of bytes represented by each token. By treating tokens as the primary metric, traditional scaling laws overlook the impact of this compression rate on training efficiency.

![isoflop-curves-showing-the-relationship-between-bits-per-byte-bpb-and-the-ratio-of-bytes-per-parameter-across-different-compression-rates](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2605.01188v2/img-4.jpeg)

*Compute Optimal Tokenization* addresses this gap by shifting the perspective from tokens to bytes. The researchers demonstrate that compute efficiency is best understood when data is measured in bytes and the compression rate is treated as a core variable. This approach allows for a generalized scaling law that remains robust even as tokenization schemes change.

## Redefining the Unit of Information: Tokens vs. Bytes

The core challenge in existing scaling laws is the ambiguity of the token. A model that uses highly compressed tokens (many bytes per token) processes a larger volume of text for the same number of tokens compared to a model with low compression. This difference directly affects the computational cost of the forward and backward passes during training.

The researchers define the compression rate $T$ as:
$$
T = \frac{\text{Total Bytes}}{\text{Total Tokens}}
$$

If a model uses standard BPE, $T$ might be around 4.5. If it uses character-level tokenization, $T$ is closer to 1. By systematically varying $T$, the study investigates how much text (in bytes) a model should see and how many parameters ($N$) it should have to achieve the lowest possible loss for a given compute budget ($C$).

To maintain a fair comparison, the authors use "bits-per-byte" (BPB) as their primary evaluation metric. Unlike token-based cross-entropy loss, which is incomparable between models with different vocabularies, BPB normalizes the loss by the physical size of the text, providing a universal benchmark for information processing efficiency.

## The First Scaling Law: Optimal Parameter and Data Ratios

One of the most significant findings of this work is the "Byte-Centric" scaling rule. Traditional laws suggested that the optimal ratio between data and parameters is constant when measured in tokens. This study reveals that the ratio is actually constant when measured in *bytes*.

The researchers trained nearly a thousand models, varying the parameter count $N$, the data size in bytes $B$, and the compression rate $T$. They found that for compute-optimal models, the training data size and parameter count scale according to the following power laws:
$$
B^*(C, T) \sim B_0 C^\alpha T^\beta
$$
$$
N^*(C, T) \sim N_0 C^{1-\alpha} T^{1-\beta}
$$

Empirically, both $\alpha \approx 0.5$ and $\beta \approx 0.5$. Because $\beta$ is approximately 0.5 for both data and parameters, the ratio between bytes and parameters ($\rho^* = B^* / N^*$) remains essentially constant regardless of the compression rate. For English text, this optimal ratio was found to be approximately $\rho \approx 60$ bytes per parameter.

This finding provides a practical rule for model developers: when switching to a new tokenizer or a different vocabulary size, do not look at the number of tokens. Instead, ensure the model sees the same ratio of raw bytes per parameter to maintain compute optimality.

## The Second Scaling Law: Finding the Optimal Compression Rate

While the first law tells us how to balance data and model size for a *fixed* compression rate, the second law asks: is there an *optimal* compression rate $T$ for a given compute budget?

The researchers found that there is a "sweet spot" for compression. If $T$ is too low (e.g., character-level), the model spends too much compute processing redundant, low-level patterns. If $T$ is too high (over-compression), the model must learn excessively complex mappings between tokens and meaning, which hinders learning.

The relationship for the optimal loss $L^*$ was modeled as:
$$
L^*(C, T) \sim L_0 C^\gamma + f(C, T)
$$
where $f(C, T)$ represents the penalty for deviating from the optimal compression. The specific form of this penalty was determined to be:
$$
f(C, T) = F \cdot \log_2(T / (T_0 C^\delta))^2 + E
$$

![the-optimal-compression-rate-decreases-slowly-as-the-total-compute-budget-flops-increases](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2605.01188v2/img-1.jpeg)

Crucially, the optimal compression rate $T^*$ is not a fixed number; it decreases slightly as the compute budget $C$ increases. For smaller models and budgets, a compression rate similar to Llama 3's BPE ($T \approx 4.57$) is quite efficient. However, as models scale to trillions of parameters and massive compute, the results suggest that slightly *less* compression (lower $T$) may be more optimal.

## Consistency Across Tokenization Paradigms

A key question was whether these scaling laws were unique to specific model architectures. The researchers tested two very different tokenization approaches:
1.  **Latent Tokenization (BLT):** The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) architecture was used to precisely control $T$. BLT groups bytes into latent representations based on local entropy, allowing for a continuous range of compression rates.
2.  **Subword Tokenization (BPE):** Standard subword tokenizers like those used in Llama 3 and SuperBPE were also tested by varying vocabulary sizes and masking parts of the vocabulary.

The results were remarkably consistent across both methods. The byte-centric scaling ratio and the existence of an optimal $T$ held true regardless of whether the model used a fixed vocabulary or a learned latent grouping. This suggests that these laws are fundamental properties of language modeling compute efficiency rather than artifacts of a specific implementation.

## The Multilingual Dimension and Cross-Lingual Parity

Languages vary in their orthographic complexity and information density. A byte in English does not necessarily carry the same "weight" as a byte in Hindi or Arabic. To explore this, the researchers introduced the concept of cross-lingual parity—the ratio of the byte-length of a sentence in a given language compared to its English translation.

The study found that the optimal scaling parameters are language-specific and highly correlated with this parity:
*   **Optimal Ratio:** Languages with high parity (more bytes per unit of information) actually benefit from a higher ratio of bytes per parameter. 
*   **Optimal Compression:** The optimal compression rate $T^*_l$ for a language $l$ scales almost linearly with its parity. If a language takes twice as many bytes as English to express the same idea, its optimal tokenizer should ideally pack twice as many bytes into each token.

![optimal-compression-rates-vary-significantly-across-different-languages-and-correlate-with-their-information-density-relative-to-english](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2605.01188v2/img-2.jpeg)

The researchers observed a significant "tokenizer mismatch" in current popular LLMs. Existing multilingual tokenizers tend to over-compress high-resource languages like English and Arabic while under-compressing others like Hindi or Vietnamese. This leads to an "efficiency tax" on certain languages, where the model is forced to operate at a sub-optimal compression rate, effectively receiving less compute per unit of information.

## Practical Implications for LLM Development

The findings of this paper offer several concrete recommendations for building the next generation of language models:

*   **Move to Byte-Based Data Tracking:** Developers should track training progress in bytes rather than tokens. This ensures that scaling recipes remain valid even when the tokenizer is updated or the data mixture changes.
*   **The 60-Byte Rule:** For English-centric models, aiming for a training data size of roughly 60 bytes per parameter provides a compute-optimal starting point.
*   **Adaptive Tokenization for Multilingual Models:** Since optimal compression is language-dependent, static subword tokenizers are inherently limited. Adaptive methods, like latent tokenization, that can adjust the compression rate based on the specific language being processed, offer a path toward more efficient and equitable multilingual models.
*   **Inference Efficiency vs. Training Optimality:** While higher compression can reduce inference costs (by processing fewer tokens), if the compression deviates too far from the training-optimal $T^*$, the model's overall performance will suffer. The study suggests that for long-term efficiency, the tokenization scheme should be chosen based on training compute-optimality first.

By integrating the compression rate into the mathematical framework of scaling laws, this work provides a more complete understanding of how to allocate computational resources. It reveals that the way we break down text into units is just as important as the number of parameters in the model or the size of the dataset.
