## The Challenge of Low-Grade Waste Heat Recovery

A significant portion of global primary energy is dissipated as waste heat, particularly at temperatures below $100 \text{ }^{\circ}\text{C}$. Recovering this low-grade energy is a major objective for environmental sustainability, as it could power small electronics, sensors, or contribute back to the grid. However, conventional recovery methods like the Organic Rankine Cycle (ORC) often lack the reliability or compactness required for these scales, and solid-state alternatives like Thermoelectric Generators (TEGs) frequently suffer from low efficiency and reliance on scarce materials when operating at small temperature differences.

Thermoelastic harvesting (TEH) presents a promising alternative. This technology utilizes shape-memory alloys (SMAs), specifically Nickel-Titanium (NiTi) wires, which undergo a structural phase transformation between a low-temperature martensitic state and a high-temperature austenitic state. When heated, the SMA wire contracts and generates significant mechanical work, which can then be converted into electrical power.

Despite its potential, historical TEH designs have struggled to compete with TEGs due to three fundamental bottlenecks: inefficient material utilization, fixed operating geometries, and scalability constraints related to heat exchange. The research detailed here introduces a new system architecture that addresses these limitations, demonstrating a thermoelastic harvester that outperforms modern TEGs in the sub-$100 \text{ }^{\circ}\text{C}$ regime.

## Architectural Innovations in Thermoelastic Harvesting

To overcome the inefficiencies of previous designs, the researchers implemented three key technical shifts in the harvester's architecture.

### 1. Protagonist–Antagonist Configuration
Previous TEH systems often used continuous loops of SMA wire, where only a fraction of the material was active at any time. This research employs a "seesaw" mechanism connecting two sets of SMA bundles. In this protagonist–antagonist setup, as one set of wires is heated and contracts (the protagonist), it provides the mechanical force necessary to stretch the opposing cold set (the antagonist). This ensures that 100% of the active SMA volume participates in the thermodynamic cycle during every half-cycle, maximizing the work potential of the material.

### 2. Continuously Tunable Prestrain
The performance of an SMA harvester is highly dependent on the initial mechanical tension, or prestrain $\epsilon_0$, applied to the wires. Prior designs usually fixed this parameter through their rigid mechanical geometry. This system incorporates a motorized bearing that allows for the continuous adjustment of the seesaw's central height. By dynamically tuning $\epsilon_0$, the researchers can optimize the force-strain balance to match the mechanical load, similar to impedance matching in electrical circuits.

### 3. Transversal Fluid Flow
One of the most significant barriers to scaling TEH has been the coupling of wire length to heat exchange time. If water flows axially along a long wire, the tail end of the wire heats up much later than the front, limiting the cycle frequency $f$. By switching to transversal flow—where the heat-transfer fluid moves perpendicular to the wire bundles—the researchers decoupled the active volume from the thermal time constant. This allows the system to use long wires for high volume while maintaining the fast thermal cycling required for high power output.

## Characterizing the Harvesting Cycle

The researchers systematically explored the operational parameter space of the device, focusing on how cycle frequency $f$, temperature span $\Delta T$, and prestrain $\epsilon_0$ influence the strain amplitude $\Delta \epsilon$ and the final power output.

### Load-Free Dynamics
In experiments without an external mechanical load, the team observed that a minimum prestrain of $0.25\%$ was required to initiate the phase transformation. As the prestrain increased to $3\%$, the strain amplitude $\Delta \epsilon$ rose to approximately $2.7\%$. They also found that cycle frequency plays a critical role: at $0.5 \text{ Hz}$, the wires had sufficient time for complete transformation, but at $10 \text{ Hz}$, the rapid switching led to incomplete heat exchange and a negligible $\Delta \epsilon$.

The temperature sensitivity was also noted. The commercially available $\text{Ni}_{56.11}\text{Ti}_{43.89}$ wires used in this study had a transformation temperature near $-10 \text{ }^{\circ}\text{C}$. This meant that cooling the wires to a low $T_{\text{cold}}$ was more effective for increasing power than raising $T_{\text{hot}}$, suggesting that performance could be further enhanced by selecting SMAs with transformation temperatures better matched to the ambient waste heat environment (e.g., $15 \text{ K}$ higher).

### Power Extraction and Efficiency
To measure actual work, the seesaw was connected to hydraulic cylinders that pumped oil through a throttle valve. The instantaneous power $P_i$ on each side of the seesaw is defined by the product of the measured force $F_i(t)$ and the velocity of displacement $\dot{x}$:

$$
P_i(t) = F_i(t) \cdot \dot{x}(t)
$$

The total average power $\bar{P}$ was then calculated by time-averaging the sum of the powers from both sides over several cycles. The system reached a maximum power output of $190 \text{ mW}$ using an active SMA volume of $0.518 \text{ cm}^3$. This occurred at an optimal frequency of $1 \text{ Hz}$ and a prestrain of $3.6\%$.

The system efficiency $\eta_{\text{sys}}$ is calculated as the ratio of average power output to the thermal input power $\dot{Q}_{\text{in}}$:

$$
\eta_{\text{sys}} = \frac{\bar{P}}{\dot{Q}_{\text{in}}}
$$

While the measured system efficiency reached $0.033\%$ of the Carnot limit $\eta_{\text{Carnot}}$, material-level simulations indicate that the NiTi SMA itself can reach $51.6\%$ of the Carnot bound. This discrepancy highlights that the primary bottleneck is not the material's physics, but rather the engineering of the heat exchanger, where much of the thermal energy in the water is currently not captured by the wires.

## Performance Benchmarking and Significance

The most significant finding of this work is the competitive performance of the thermoelastic harvester compared to other established solid-state technologies.

### Power Density Superiority
The TEH achieved a power density of $366 \text{ mW/cm}^3$ at a temperature difference of approximately $66 \text{ K}$ ($T_{\text{hot}} = 73.7 \text{ }^{\circ}\text{C}$, $T_{\text{cold}} = 7.5 \text{ }^{\circ}\text{C}$). As shown in {fig:benchmarking-power-density-and-cost}, this value is nearly double that of the next-best thermoelastic device and significantly higher than modern Thermomagnetic Generators (TMGs) and Pyroelectric Generators (PEGs). Notably, it also surpasses the power density of top-performing TEGs ($205 \text{ mW/cm}^3$) in the same low-temperature range.

### Economic and Material Viability
Beyond raw power, the researchers assessed the power-to-material-cost ratio. The TEH reached $4.9 \text{ W/€}$, outperforming the benchmarked TEG ($2.78 \text{ W/€}$). Because NiTi is a widely used material in medical and engineering fields, its supply chain is mature, and it does not rely on the toxic or scarce elements often found in high-performance thermoelectric materials.

## Future Outlook

This research provides a clear roadmap for the development of thermoelastic harvesting. By optimizing system design—specifically through transversal flow and protagonist-antagonist architectures—the technology has reached a point where it can effectively compete with thermoelectric generators for waste heat recovery below $100 \text{ }^{\circ}\text{C}$.

The study identifies heat exchange engineering as the most critical area for future improvement. Reducing the volume of "dead water" that flows through the system without interacting with the wires could dramatically increase the system-level efficiency $\eta_{\text{sys}}$. Furthermore, the results suggest a new target for material scientists: developing SMA compositions specifically for harvesting, where the goal is to maximize the area of the stress-strain cycle while minimizing the heat required to trigger the phase change. This differs from traditional SMA applications like actuators, opening a new pathway for functional material design.
