## Introduction to LHS 1140 b and the Search for Helium

The exoplanet LHS 1140 b has emerged as a high-priority target for atmospheric characterization. Located approximately 40 light-years away, it orbits a quiet mid-M dwarf star within its habitable zone. Initial assessments of the planet suggested it might be a terrestrial, Earth-like world. However, refined measurements of its mass, approximately $5.6 M_{\oplus}$, and radius, approximately $1.7 R_{\oplus}$, suggest a different nature: either a "water world" with a significant volatile layer or a temperate "mini-Neptune" retaining a low-mass hydrogen/helium (H/He) envelope.

One of the most effective ways to distinguish between these atmospheric compositions is to look for evidence of atmospheric escape. Because LHS 1140 b is subject to high-energy radiation from its host star, its upper atmosphere can be heated, causing gasses to escape into space. Metastable helium, which produces a distinct absorption triplet at $1.083 \mu\text{m}$, serves as a sensitive probe for this process. Detecting this signature allows researchers to infer the presence of an extended atmosphere and estimate the rate at which the planet is losing mass.

Recent observations of LHS 1140 b have yielded conflicting results. In 2024, a study using the high-resolution WINERED spectrograph on the Magellan Clay Telescope reported a detection of escaping helium. Curiously, the same team observed the planet again in 2025 and found no such signal. This discrepancy suggested that the atmospheric escape from LHS 1140 b might be highly variable, potentially driven by changes in the host star’s X-ray and ultraviolet (XUV) output. The research presented here aims to resolve this mystery by analyzing four archival transits of LHS 1140 b observed with the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST).

## Observational Strategy with JWST NIRISS

To search for the helium signature, the study utilized data from the Near-InfraRed Imager and Slitless Spectrograph (NIRISS) onboard JWST. Observations were performed in the Single Object Slitless Spectroscopy (SOSS) mode, which is specifically designed for high-precision time-series observations of exoplanet transits. NIRISS/SOSS covers a broad wavelength range from $0.85\text{--}2.85 \mu\text{m}$, which includes the critical helium triplet at $1.083 \mu\text{m}$.

The dataset analyzed includes four transits of LHS 1140 b spanning from December 2023 to July 2026. Additionally, a single transit of LHS 1140 c, a smaller interior planet in the same system, was included to help characterize the host star. While JWST has a lower spectral resolution than ground-based instruments like WINERED, its position in space grants it extraordinary photometric stability. This stability allows for the detection of subtle spectral features that might be obscured by Earth's atmosphere or instrumental noise in ground-based data.

## Data Processing and Systematic Noise Removal

The raw data from JWST were processed using a specialized pipeline called `exoTEDRF`. This pipeline handles the complex task of converting raw pixel counts into cleaned, calibrated spectra. Key steps included the correction of $1/f$ noise (a common form of electronic noise), identifying and removing cosmic rays (outliers), and performing background subtraction.

A particularly challenging aspect of analyzing transit data for planets orbiting M dwarfs is the presence of stellar heterogeneities, such as starspots and faculae. If a planet crosses a dark spot or a bright region on the stellar surface during its transit, it can create "bumps" or "dips" in the light curve that mimic atmospheric signatures. The researchers identified such events in two of the four transits (Visits 2 and 4). These spot crossings were carefully modeled using Gaussian functions to ensure they did not bias the final transmission spectra. 

The light curve fitting was performed using the `exoUPRF` package. For each transit, a "white" light curve was first generated by summing the total flux across the entire SOSS spectral range. This provided precise constraints on the orbital parameters, such as the mid-transit time $T_0$, the scaled semi-major axis $a/R_s$, and the planet-to-star radius ratio $R_p/R_*$.

## Searching for the Helium Signature

The core of the investigation involved examining the transmission spectrum of LHS 1140 b specifically in the $1.04\text{--}1.12 \mu\text{m}$ range. The researchers used a Bayesian model comparison to evaluate three different hypotheses for each transit:

1.  **Flat Continuum (Null Hypothesis):** A model where the planet's transit depth is constant across the wavelength range, suggesting no detectable spectral features.
2.  **Free Gaussian Helium Line:** A model where a Gaussian absorption feature is added at $1.083 \mu\text{m}$, with the amplitude of the absorption allowed to vary.
3.  **Fixed Gaussian Helium Line:** A model where the helium absorption amplitude and width are fixed to the values reported in the 2024 WINERED detection.

The Gaussian model for the helium line was defined as:
$$ \delta(\lambda) = A \exp\left( -\frac{(\lambda - \lambda_0)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right) $$
where $A$ is the amplitude of the absorption and $\lambda_0$ is the central wavelength ($1.083 \mu\text{m}$).

The results across all four transits were consistent: none of the observations showed evidence of helium absorption. In all cases, the flat continuum model was statistically preferred over the free-amplitude model. Furthermore, the model fixed to the 2024 detection was strongly rejected, with evidence against it reaching a confidence level up to $5.1\sigma$ in the best case.

To quantify what JWST *could* have detected, the researchers performed injection-recovery tests. They added simulated helium signals of various strengths into the data and determined the minimum amplitude required for a confident detection (defined as a Bayes factor threshold of $$ \ln \mathcal{B} > 4.61 $$, equivalent to a $3.5\sigma$ detection). The derived upper limits for the helium line amplitude ranged from 350 to 550 parts per million (ppm). These limits are significantly lower than the nearly 600 ppm signal reported in the 2024 ground-based detection.

## Characterizing the Host Star LHS 1140

Beyond the search for helium, the researchers investigated the stability of the host star itself. Using the absolute out-of-transit stellar spectra, they employed the `exoTHOMF` package to fit multi-component stellar atmosphere models. These models accounted for the star's background photosphere as well as surface heterogeneities.

The best-fitting model for LHS 1140 required three components:
*   A background photosphere with temperature $T_{phot}$ approximately 2900 K.
*   A cold spot component $T_{cold}$ at least 500 K cooler than the photosphere, covering about 10% of the surface ($f_{cold}$).
*   A hot faculae component $T_{hot}$ approximately 3000 K warmer than the photosphere, covering about 7% of the surface ($f_{hot}$).

Crucially, as shown in {fig:stellar-spectra}, the inferred parameters for these components remained remarkably stable over the 2.5-year observation period. This suggests that the broadband stellar properties of LHS 1140 do not change significantly on these timescales. However, this does not rule out variability in the XUV radiation, which originates in the stellar corona and chromosphere rather than the photosphere, and is the primary driver of atmospheric escape.

## Implications for Atmospheric Escape

The strict non-detection of helium in all four JWST transits, combined with the conflicting results from ground-based observations, points toward a highly dynamic environment for LHS 1140 b. There are several possible interpretations of these results.

First, the absence of helium might indicate that the planet does not have a helium-rich atmosphere at all. If the planet is a water world with a high mean molecular weight atmosphere (dominated by water vapor or nitrogen), the scale height of the atmosphere would be small, making absorption features difficult to detect. However, this would not explain the previous 2024 detection.

Second, and more likely, the atmospheric escape from LHS 1140 b may be intermittent. M dwarf stars are known to be active, and while LHS 1140 is relatively "quiet," it may still undergo periods of increased high-energy emission. If the helium escape is sensitive to the stellar XUV flux at the time of transit, a detection could be possible during a period of high activity, while a non-detection would follow during a quiescent phase. The consistency of the JWST non-detections over 2.5 years suggests that the state required for strong helium escape may be relatively rare.

Finally, this work demonstrates that JWST NIRISS can achieve sensitivities to narrow spectral lines comparable to high-resolution ground-based spectrographs. The ability to set upper limits at the level of a few hundred ppm highlights JWST's utility in studying the upper atmospheres of temperate exoplanets. As more transits are observed, researchers will continue to build a more complete picture of the "cosmic shoreline"—the boundary between planets that can retain their atmospheres and those that are stripped bare by their stars. For now, LHS 1140 b remains a complex puzzle, teetering on that very edge.
