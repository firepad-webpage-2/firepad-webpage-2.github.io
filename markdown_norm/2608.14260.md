The evolution of wireless communication is shifting from the traditional transmission of exact bitstreams toward a paradigm that prioritizes the delivery of meaning. While conventional systems operate under Shannon's technical framework, seeking to reconstruct pixels with high fidelity, semantic communication (SC) focuses on extracting and transmitting the underlying essence of data. However, most existing SC systems treat all receivers as identical, ignoring the subjective nature of "meaning." This research introduces a Personalized Digital Semantic Communication (PDSC) framework that adapts image transmission to the specific preferences of an individual receiver, leveraging advanced vision-language models (VLMs) and latent diffusion models (LDMs).

## Introduction to Personalized Semantic Communication

In standard communication systems, a source image is compressed into bits, transmitted over a noisy channel, and reconstructed at the receiver. The goal is typically to minimize pixel-wise distortion. In semantic communication, the goal shifts to maintaining semantic consistency. Yet, the relevance of visual information is often receiver-dependent. For instance, if two users look at the same movie poster, one might care about the lead actors (action movie preference), while another might focus on the artistic style (animation preference).

Traditional SC systems are "user-agnostic," employing a single semantic representation for all receivers. This leads to inefficiencies where bandwidth is spent on details the receiver does not find useful. The PDSC framework addresses this by incorporating a receiver's historical interaction set $H_u = \{x_1, \dots, x_N\}$, representing their long-term visual preferences. By conditioning the communication process on these preferences, the system can achieve higher perceived quality and task relevance even at extremely low bitrates.

## Framework Architecture: From Vision-Language to Bitstreams

The PDSC framework consists of three primary stages: personalized semantic encoding, digital transmission through vector quantization, and generative semantic decoding.

### Personalized Semantic Encoder
The transmitter uses a VLM-based encoder to extract three distinct types of semantic tokens from the source image $X$, conditioned on the receiver's history $H_u$:
1.  **Preference Token ($z_p$):** Captures the specific attributes of the source image that align with the receiver’s historical interests. It is generated by querying the historical images with the source features.
2.  **Visual-Semantic Tokens ($z_v$):** Preserve the structural layout and fundamental visual components of the source image $X$.
3.  **Text-Semantic Tokens ($z_t$):** Provide high-level linguistic descriptions (e.g., captions) to guide the reconstruction process.

These tokens are combined into a joint representation $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$, where $L$ is the number of tokens and $d$ is the embedding dimension.

### Vector-Quantized Digital Interface
To maintain compatibility with current digital communication infrastructures, the continuous tokens in $Z$ must be converted into bits. The framework employs a Vector Quantization (VQ) interface. A learnable codebook $C = \{c_1, \dots, c_K\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ is maintained at both the transmitter and receiver. Each token $z_j$ is mapped to its nearest neighbor in the codebook:

$$k_j = \arg \min_{i} \|z_j - c_i\|_2$$

The resulting indices $k = \{k_1, \dots, k_L\}$ are then source-encoded into a bitstream of length $R_s = L\lceil\log_2 K\rceil$. This discrete representation is significantly more compact than raw pixel data, allowing for transmission over highly constrained channels.

### Diffusion-Based Semantic Decoder
At the receiver, the indices are recovered as $\hat{k}$, and the corresponding codewords $\hat{z}_j$ are retrieved from the codebook. These tokens serve as conditioning signals for a Latent Diffusion Model (LDM). Unlike traditional decoders that attempt to reverse a mathematical transform, this generative decoder uses the tokens to guide a denoising process, synthesizing a personalized image $\hat{X}$ that reflects both the source semantics and the user's preferences.

## Personalized Semantic Distortion and Optimization

A core contribution of this work is the mathematical definition of personalized semantic distortion, which allows the system to balance fidelity to the original image with alignment to the receiver's preferences.

The total distortion $D(X, \hat{X}, H_u)$ is defined as:

$$D(X, \hat{X}, H_u) = \alpha L_{sem} + (1 - \alpha) L_{pref}$$

In this equation, $\alpha$ is a weighting factor that controls the trade-off.

### Source-Semantic Fidelity
The fidelity term $L_{sem}$ measures how well the reconstructed image preserves the high-level meaning of the original. This is calculated using the cosine distance between the embeddings generated by a pretrained VLM image encoder $\Phi_I(\cdot)$:

$$L_{sem} = 1 - \frac{\Phi_I(X) \cdot \Phi_I(\hat{X})}{\|\Phi_I(X)\| \|\Phi_I(\hat{X})\|}$$

### User-Preference Alignment
The alignment term $L_{pref}$ measures the consistency between the reconstructed image and the receiver's history $H_u$. Since not all historical images are equally relevant to a specific source image, the framework uses a weighted approach:

$$L_{pref} = \sum_{i=1}^N w_i (1 - \cos(\Phi_I(\hat{X}), \Phi_I(x_i)))$$

The weights $w_i$ are calculated based on the relevance of each historical image $x_i \in H_u$ to the current source $X$:

$$w_i = \frac{\exp(\cos(\Phi_I(X), \Phi_I(x_i)) / \tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^N \exp(\cos(\Phi_I(X), \Phi_I(x_j)) / \tau)}$$

This ensures that the reconstruction is influenced most strongly by the receiver's preferences that are actually pertinent to the current content.

## Two-Stage Learning and Implementation

Training the PDSC framework requires aligning the discrete codebook with the generative capabilities of the diffusion model. The authors propose a two-stage training strategy.

**Stage I: Semantic Token Alignment.** In this stage, the diffusion backbone and visual tokenizer are frozen. The goal is to train the semantic encoder and the VQ codebook so that the discrete codewords can effectively prompt the diffusion prior. The loss function includes a diffusion denoising loss $L_{diff}$ and a VQ loss $L_{vq}$:

$$L_I = L_{diff} + \lambda_{vq}L_{vq} + \lambda_u L_u$$

The term $L_u$ is an entropy-based regularizer that prevents "codebook collapse," where only a few codewords are used, by encouraging a uniform distribution of index usage.

**Stage II: Personalization Adaptation.** Here, the system is fine-tuned to optimize for the personalized distortion metric. The encoder, codebook, and the diffusion model's conditioning layers are trained jointly:

$$L_{II} = L_{diff} + \lambda_{vq}L_{vq} + \lambda_u L_u + \lambda_s D(X, \hat{X}, H_u)$$

The VQ loss itself is defined using a straight-through estimator to allow gradients to flow through the non-differentiable quantization step:

$$L_{vq} = \|\text{sg}[Z] - \hat{Z}\|_2^2 + \beta\|Z - \text{sg}[\hat{Z}]\|_2^2$$

where $\text{sg}[\cdot]$ denotes the stop-gradient operator.

## Experimental Analysis and Performance

The PDSC framework was evaluated using a dataset of movie posters and user ratings from MovieLens. It was compared against three baseline methods: the traditional BPG+LDPC separation-based scheme, and two state-of-the-art semantic communication models, CDDM and MoS.

### Robustness to Channel Noise
Communication reliability was tested across a range of Signal-to-Noise Ratios (SNRs). PDSC showed remarkable robustness, particularly in low-SNR environments. At $0 \text{ dB}$, PDSC achieved a 45.8% improvement in the CLIP Image Score (CIS), which measures preference alignment, compared to the best-performing baseline, MoS. This suggests that by focusing on high-level personalized tokens, the system can successfully convey essential meaning even when the channel is extremely noisy.

### Rate-Distortion Efficiency
When operating at a fixed SNR of $20 \text{ dB}$, the framework's efficiency was tested across different bitrates by varying the codebook size $K$. With $L=21$ tokens and a codebook size of $K=1024$, the total semantic rate is 210 bits per image. Under these conditions, PDSC consistently outperformed user-agnostic models. Even at very low rates (e.g., 147 bits/image), PDSC maintained high semantic consistency and preference alignment, whereas traditional methods began to suffer from severe visual artifacts or total reconstruction failure.

Averaged across various rates, PDSC provided a 23.1% higher CIS and a 6.7% higher CLIP Score (CS, measuring source consistency) than MoS. This performance gap highlights the benefit of allocating the bit budget to receiver-aware semantics rather than attempting to reconstruct generic visual details.

## Conclusion and Future Directions

The Personalized Digital Semantic Communication framework represents a significant step toward receiver-aware wireless systems. By integrating VLMs and diffusion models, the system demonstrates that it is possible to transmit images in a way that is both bandwidth-efficient and tailored to individual needs. The introduction of a personalized rate-distortion objective provides a principled way to optimize these systems.

Future research may explore how to adapt these preferences dynamically in real-time as user interests evolve. Additionally, scaling the framework to video transmission or multi-user environments presents exciting challenges for extending personalized semantic communication to a broader range of multimedia services. By moving the focus from "what is transmitted" to "who is receiving," this approach paves the way for more intelligent and efficient next-generation communication networks.
