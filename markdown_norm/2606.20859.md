## The Challenge of Non-Stationary Data Streams

In modern machine learning, many algorithms rely on the assumption of exchangeability. Essentially, this means that the specific order in which data points arrive should not influence their joint probability distribution. When data is exchangeable, the future looks statistically like the past. However, real-world data streams—such as financial markets, sensor readings, or user behavior—are frequently non-stationary. Distributional shifts, where the underlying patterns change over time, can lead to invalid predictions and model failure.

To detect these shifts in real-time, researchers use conformal test martingales. These are mathematical processes that act as a "betting" mechanism against the null hypothesis of exchangeability. If the assumption holds, the wealth of the martingale remains stable. If the assumption is violated, the wealth grows, providing a signal that the data distribution has shifted.

A popular tool in this domain is the Simple Jumper martingale. While computationally efficient, the Simple Jumper is limited by its linear betting function. It is highly effective at detecting "location shifts"—changes in the average value of a data stream—but it is largely blind to shifts in higher-order moments, such as changes in variance (spread), skewness (asymmetry), or kurtosis (tailedness). The research by Szabadváry introduces Legendre Jumper Martingales to bridge this gap, allowing for the detection of complex distributional shifts by betting on multiple moments simultaneously.

## Martingales and Conformal Prediction

The framework for this work is situated within conformal prediction. When a model produces a sequence of p-values $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n$ under the assumption of exchangeability, these p-values are independent and uniformly distributed on $[0, 1]$. A conformal test martingale $S_n$ is constructed as:

$$
S_n = \prod_{i=1}^n f_i(p_i)
$$

where each $f_i$ is a non-negative betting function that integrates to 1:

$$
\int_0^1 f_i(p) dp = 1
$$

By Ville’s inequality, the probability that the martingale ever exceeds a threshold $1/\alpha$ is bounded:

$$
P( \max_{1 \leq i \leq n} S_i \geq \frac{1}{\alpha} ) \leq \alpha
$$

If the martingale grows significantly, it provides statistical evidence that the uniform distribution of p-values—and thus the exchangeability of the data—has been violated. The core innovation of this paper lies in how these betting functions $f_i$ are constructed using Legendre polynomials.

## Leveraging Legendre Polynomials for Moment Detection

To detect departures from uniformity across different moments, the author draws inspiration from Neyman's smooth test, a classical statistical method. The method utilizes shifted Legendre polynomials, denoted $\tilde{P}_k(x)$. These polynomials are orthogonal on the interval $[0, 1]$ with respect to the uniform weight function. This orthogonality property is vital because it ensures that for any degree $k \geq 1$:

$$
\int_0^1 \tilde{P}_k(x) dx = 0
$$

This property allows researchers to build valid betting functions. The Simple Legendre Jumper (SLJ) defines its betting function as:

$$
f^{(k)}_\epsilon(p) = 1 + \epsilon \tilde{P}_k(p)
$$

where $k$ is the polynomial degree and $\epsilon$ is a parameter representing the "bet." By choosing different values of $k$, the martingale can be tuned to specific moments:
- $k=1$ corresponds to a linear shift, targeting the mean (location).
- $k=2$ corresponds to a quadratic shift, targeting the variance.
- $k=3$ corresponds to a cubic shift, targeting skewness.

While a single SLJ can detect a specific type of shift, real-world data often exhibits complex changes involving multiple moments simultaneously. 

## The Product Legendre Jumper and the Jumping Tax

To capture these complex shifts, the paper introduces the Product Legendre Jumper (PLJ). The PLJ uses a combined betting function that incorporates a set of polynomial degrees $K$:

$$
f^K_\epsilon(p) = \frac{\prod_{k \in K} (1 + \epsilon_k \tilde{P}_k(p))}{Z(\epsilon)}
$$

Here, $\epsilon$ is a vector of parameters, one for each degree $k$. The term $Z(\epsilon)$ is a normalization constant defined as:

$$
Z(\epsilon) = \int_0^1 \prod_{k \in K} (1 + \epsilon_k \tilde{P}_k(p)) dp
$$

The PLJ adapts to the data by treating the parameter vector $\epsilon$ as a state in a Markov chain. At each step, the system either stays in its current state (with probability $1-J$) or "jumps" to a new state (with probability $J$). 

However, this approach faces a significant hurdle known as the "jumping tax." As the number of moments $|K|$ increases, the size of the state space grows exponentially. For a grid of $g$ possible values for each $\epsilon_k$, the total number of states is $g^{|K|}$. This exponential growth dilutes the martingale's wealth because the starting capital must be distributed across all possible states. Consequently, the PLJ becomes computationally prohibitive and statistically less efficient as more moments are added.

## Computational Efficiency via the Variational Legendre Jumper

To overcome the jumping tax, the author proposes the Variational Legendre Jumper (VLJ). This method is inspired by mean-field approximations used in variational inference. Instead of managing a single Markov chain over a massive joint state space, the VLJ treats each polynomial degree independently.

The VLJ runs $|K|$ separate instances of the Simple Legendre Jumper. Each sub-jumper maintains its own wealth and independently adapts its specific parameter $\epsilon_k$. At every time step $n$, each sub-jumper provides a "consensus" parameter $\bar{\epsilon}_k$, which is the average of its possible parameter values weighted by their current wealth. The global martingale then uses these consensus values in a single normalized product betting function:

$$
S_n = S_{n-1} \cdot f^K_{\bar{\epsilon}}(p_n)
$$

This factorization reduces the computational complexity from $O(g^{|K|})$ to $O(|K|g)$. As shown in Figure 9, this can reduce the wall-clock processing time from nearly a minute to less than a second for a set of five polynomial degrees, without sacrificing significant statistical power.

## Adapting to Unknown Timescales: Composite Martingales

Another practical challenge in online testing is the "jumping rate" $J$. This parameter determines how quickly the martingale adapts to changes. A high $J$ allows for rapid adaptation to abrupt shifts but causes the wealth to decay quickly if no shift occurs. Conversely, a low $J$ is better for gradual shifts but slow to react to sudden changes.

Since the timescale of a potential shift is usually unknown, the paper proposes the Composite Legendre Jumper. This is an arithmetic average of several martingales, each using a different jumping rate from a grid, such as $J \in \{10^{-4}, 10^{-3}, 10^{-2}, 10^{-1}, 1\}$. 

This composite approach provides two major benefits. First, it automatically adapts to the shift's timescale by naturally giving more weight to the sub-martingale that is performing best. Second, by including $J=1$ (which represents a "reset" at every step), the martingale maintains a "wealth floor" of $1/|J_{grid}|$. This prevents the martingale from losing too much capital during periods of stability, ensuring it remains ready to detect a shift when one eventually occurs.

## Experimental Results on Real-World Data

The proposed methods were tested using the Wine Quality dataset. A classifier was trained to predict wine quality, and the resulting p-values were monitored for exchangeability violations across different data orderings.

*{fig:performance-on-wine-quality-dataset}: Evolution of martingale wealth ($\log_{10} M_n$) under different data orderings. The multi-moment methods (PLJ and VLJ) consistently outperform single-moment methods (SLJ) when distributional shifts occur.*

The experiments revealed several key insights:
1. **Multi-moment superiority:** In cases like the "Red $\to$ White" wine shift, the PLJ and VLJ (using $K=\{1, 2, 3\}$) accumulated significantly more wealth than the standard Simple Jumper (which only uses $k=1$). This demonstrates that real-world shifts often involve more than just a change in the mean.
2. **VLJ Accuracy:** The Variational Legendre Jumper performed almost identically to the more complex Product Legendre Jumper, confirming that the mean-field approximation is highly effective in this context.
3. **Composite Robustness:** Under the "Original order" of the dataset, which contained abrupt shifts, the Composite versions reached much higher wealth levels than fixed-rate martingales, proving their ability to adapt to unknown shift speeds.

## Conclusion and Significance

The Legendre Jumper Martingale framework provides a comprehensive set of tools for monitoring the validity of online machine learning models. By moving beyond simple linear betting, these martingales can detect a wide variety of distributional changes that would otherwise go unnoticed. The introduction of the Variational Legendre Jumper ensures that this increased sensitivity does not come at the cost of high computational overhead, making it suitable for high-speed data streams. 

Ultimately, this work bridges classical statistical moment-testing with modern sequential analysis. For practitioners, the Composite VLJ offers a robust, "set-and-forget" solution for detecting non-exchangeability, ensuring that models remain reliable even as the world around them changes.
