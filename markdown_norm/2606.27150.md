In the world of actuarial science, reinsurance serves as "insurance for insurance companies." Traditionally, models analyzing how insurers manage their risk treat the cost of reinsurance—the premium—as a fixed, external value. However, in reality, the price of reinsurance is a strategic decision made by a reinsurer who must anticipate how a market of competing insurers will react.

The paper **"Endogenous Reinsurance Pricing in Large Competitive Insurance Markets: Finite-Player and Mean Field Analysis"** by Ruimeng Hu and Byungdoo Kong addresses this gap. The researchers develop a framework where the reinsurance premium is determined "endogenously"—meaning it is solved as part of a game between a single large reinsurer and a population of many heterogeneous insurers. By incorporating "relative performance concerns," where insurers care not just about their own wealth but also about how they compare to their peers, the authors uncover complex market dynamics, including a "spillover effect" that influences how much risk is kept or transferred.

## The Market Structure: Leaders and Followers

The authors model the interaction as a **Stackelberg game**. In this hierarchy, the reinsurer acts as the "leader." It first announces a premium rate $p$ and its own investment strategy. The insurers, acting as "followers," observe this price and then engage in a **Nash game** among themselves to decide how much risk to retain ($q_i$) and how to invest their capital ($\pi_i$).

The market is characterized by several layers of complexity:
1.  **Heterogeneity**: Insurers are not identical. They differ in their initial wealth, their tolerance for risk, and their sensitivity to the performance of others.
2.  **Stochastic Risks**: The wealth of every player is influenced by both common risks (affecting the whole market, like a natural disaster) and idiosyncratic risks (specific to one insurer).
3.  **Relative Performance Concerns**: An insurer's utility is based on their terminal wealth $X_{i,T}$ relative to the average wealth of the population $\bar{X}_T$. This introduces competition; if everyone else is doing well, an insurer feels "poorer" even if their absolute wealth remains constant.

The goal of each insurer $i$ is to maximize their expected utility, expressed through an exponential utility function:

$$
E\left[-e^{-\frac{1}{\delta_i}(X_{i,T} - \theta_i \bar{X}_T)}\right]
$$

where $\delta_i$ is the risk tolerance and $\theta_i$ represents the intensity of the relative performance concern.

## The Spillover Mechanism

One of the paper's primary contributions is the identification of a "common-insurance exposure spillover." When insurers care about relative performance ($\theta_i > 0$), their decisions become coupled. 

If the reinsurer raises the premium $p$, some insurers will choose to retain more risk to save on costs. Because these insurers now hold more risk, the average wealth of the population $\bar{X}_T$ becomes more volatile. To keep up with this average and avoid falling behind in the "competition," other insurers are incentivized to retain more risk as well, even if the direct actuarial cost of reinsurance might otherwise suggest they should transfer it.

The authors show that the equilibrium retention level for a given premium $p$ is determined by a unique scalar fixed-point equation for the "average common-insurance exposure" of the population, denoted as $\hat{V}_N(p)$. This single variable captures the aggregate risk-taking behavior of the entire market.



## Threshold Structure and Computational Efficiency

A significant challenge in solving for the optimal premium is that as $p$ changes, different insurers switch between three states:
1.  **Full Cession ($q_i = 0$)**: The premium is low enough that the insurer transfers all risk to the reinsurer.
2.  **Partial Retention ($0 < q_i < 1$)**: The insurer keeps some risk and transfers some.
3.  **Full Retention ($q_i = 1$)**: The premium is so high that the insurer keeps all the risk.

In a market with $N$ insurers, there are $3^N$ possible configurations of these states. Searching through all of them to find the reinsurer's best premium would be computationally impossible for large $N$. 

The authors overcome this by proving that individual retention responses $\hat{q}_i(p)$ are continuous and monotonically increasing with respect to the premium $p$. This allows them to develop a **threshold continuation procedure**. Instead of checking every combination, the algorithm identifies the specific premium values where insurers transition between states. This reduces the computational complexity from an exponential $O(3^N)$ to a quadratic $O(N^2)$, making it feasible to model markets with hundreds of participants.

## Scaling to the Mean Field

When the number of insurers $N$ becomes extremely large, the market can be modeled as a **Mean Field Game (MFG)**. In this limit, individual "idiosyncratic" risks (the accidents specific to one person) are diversified away, leaving only the "common" risks.

The authors provide a rigorous proof of **forward convergence**. They show that as the number of insurers $N \to \infty$, the finite-player equilibrium premium and the insurers' responses converge to the values predicted by the mean field model.

This convergence is significant because it justifies using the mathematically simpler mean field equations to approximate very large, but finite, insurance markets. The paper handles a particularly difficult case where the mean field equilibrium might not be unique; they prove that even in such cases, any sequence of finite-player equilibria will eventually cluster around the set of mean field equilibria.

## Numerical Findings and Non-Uniqueness

The numerical simulations in the paper reveal several interesting economic properties:

*   **Impact of Competition**: High relative performance concerns ($\theta$) can lead insurers to retain risk even when reinsurance is "actuarially favorable" (i.e., when the premium is lower than the expected loss). This happens because insurers are more afraid of underperforming the market average than they are of the risk itself.
*   **Multiple Equilibria**: The reinsurer's objective function can have multiple peaks. This means there might be two different "optimal" premiums that provide the same utility to the reinsurer but lead to vastly different market behaviors. In one equilibrium, the reinsurer might set a high price and accept less business; in another, a lower price attracts much more risk.

## Conclusion

By bridging the gap between strategic reinsurance pricing and competitive insurer dynamics, Hu and Kong provide a more realistic model of modern insurance markets. Their work shows that reinsurance pricing is not just a calculation of loss probabilities, but a complex game where the "average" behavior of the market exerts a powerful influence on individual decisions.

The computational methods developed—specifically the $O(N^2)$ algorithm—and the rigorous mean field convergence theory provide researchers and practitioners with robust tools to analyze these interactions. Future research might build on this by considering competition between multiple reinsurers or more complex, non-proportional reinsurance treaties.

In summary, the paper moves the field toward a more "endogenous" understanding of risk management, where prices and behaviors are inextricably linked through the competitive pressure to perform relative to one's peers.
