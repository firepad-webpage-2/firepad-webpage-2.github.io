## The Challenge of Circuit Representation in Large Language Models

The application of Large Language Models (LLMs) to integrated circuit (IC) design has emerged as a significant area of research within Electronic Design Automation (EDA). These models are being leveraged to translate high-level natural language specifications into hardware descriptions, assist in analog circuit optimization, and facilitate the generation of schematics. Central to these workflows is the SPICE netlist, a textual yet highly structured representation of electronic circuits. A SPICE netlist describes components (transistors, resistors, capacitors) and their interconnections (nodes), effectively encoding a mathematical graph within a text file.

![introduction-to-llm-based-circuit-design-workflows-and-the-role-of-spice-netlists](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12197v1/Fig/intro.png)
*{fig:introduction-to-llm-based-circuit-design-workflows-and-the-role-of-spice-netlists}: SPICE netlists serve as the fundamental representation layer for various LLM-driven circuit design tasks, including generation, optimization, and layout interpretation.*

While LLMs demonstrate impressive capabilities in general-purpose programming languages like Python or C++, their reliability in handling domain-specific hardware artifacts like SPICE netlists remains a concern. Previous studies have noted that LLMs often struggle with topology-heavy reasoning—tasks that require understanding how components are connected rather than just identifying keywords. However, most existing evaluations focus on end-to-end design success (e.g., "does the circuit simulate correctly?"). This makes it difficult to determine whether a failure is due to a flaw in high-level design logic or a simple corruption of the netlist structure during the generation or editing process. The research presented here addresses this gap by introducing NetlistBench, a benchmark designed to isolate and quantify the reliability of LLMs in the elementary recognition and manipulation of SPICE netlists.

## Defining the NetlistBench Framework

NetlistBench is designed as a structure-verified benchmark, meaning it evaluates the output of an LLM based on the underlying circuit connectivity rather than simple string matching. The benchmark consists of 2,342 test cases derived from two primary sources: AnalogGenie, which provides flat CMOS analog netlists, and ALIGN, which provides hierarchical netlists containing subcircuit definitions.

The construction of the benchmark follows a deterministic, template-driven pipeline to ensure that every task has an unambiguous ground truth. Each evaluation instance is represented as a triplet:

$$I_i = (N^{(i)}_{\text{src}}, T^{(i)}_{\text{inst}}, Y^{(i)}_{\text{target}})$$

In this structure, $N^{(i)}_{\text{src}}$ is the source SPICE netlist, $T^{(i)}_{\text{inst}}$ is the natural language instruction provided to the model, and $Y^{(i)}_{\text{target}}$ is the expected output. For recognition tasks, the target might be a JSON object containing specific circuit attributes, while for manipulation tasks, it is the modified netlist itself.

![netlistbench-evaluation-pipeline-and-task-construction](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12197v1/Fig/pipeline_last3.png)
*{fig:netlistbench-evaluation-pipeline-and-task-construction}: The NetlistBench pipeline uses a deterministic generator to create task instances and a structure-aware oracle to verify model outputs against canonical circuit representations.*

One of the most critical aspects of this methodology is the "Structure-Aware Evaluation Oracle." In standard code generation benchmarks, models are often evaluated using text-based metrics like BLEU or simple exact matching. However, in SPICE netlists, there are many ways to represent the same circuit that are functionally identical but textually different (e.g., reordering lines or using different units like $1k = 1000$). The NetlistBench oracle parses both the LLM output and the target netlist into a Canonical Intermediate Representation (IR). This IR captures the essential structural properties: device names, device types, the specific order of terminal nodes, and numerical parameters. An LLM output only passes the evaluation if its canonical IR exactly matches the target IR, ensuring that even subtle, unintended changes to the circuit connectivity are detected as failures.

## Core Modalities: Recognition and Manipulation

The benchmark categorizes LLM tasks into two primary modalities: Recognition and Manipulation. These reflect the fundamental operations required for an LLM to serve as a reliable assistant in a design flow.

### Recognition Modality
Recognition tasks test the model's ability to "read" a netlist and extract its underlying properties. This includes:
- **Attribute Extraction:** Identifying specific device parameters or port interfaces.
- **Relational Queries:** Determining which devices are connected to a specific node or identifying the neighbors of a particular component.
- **Structural Equivalence:** Comparing two different netlists to determine if they represent the same circuit topology, regardless of component naming or statement ordering.

### Manipulation Modality
Manipulation tasks test the model's ability to "edit" a netlist while preserving the integrity of the rest of the design. These are further divided by complexity:
- **Single-Edit Tasks:** Applying a localized change, such as replacing a specific transistor, changing a resistor's value, or reconnecting a single terminal.
- **Compound Tasks:** Executing a sequence of dependent edits (e.g., "add a capacitor, connect it to node A, and then change the width of the neighboring transistor"). These tasks test the model's ability to maintain a consistent state over a longer horizon.
- **Hierarchical Tasks:** Modifying components inside a subcircuit definition or changing how a subcircuit interface interacts with the top-level design.

## Experimental Insights: The Structure-Preservation Bottleneck

The researchers evaluated several state-of-the-art LLMs, including models from the Claude, GPT, Gemini, DeepSeek, and Qwen families. The results revealed a significant "reliability gap" between simple text-based modifications and tasks requiring structural understanding.

### Local vs. Structural Reliability
Models generally performed well on tasks that involved localized text substitution. For instance, parameter editing and device removal tasks often saw pass rates near $100\%$. These tasks are relatively easy because they do not require the model to reason about the global connectivity graph; they only require finding a specific line and modifying it.

However, performance dropped sharply for tasks involving connectivity. Reconnecting terminals, adding new devices, or swapping subcircuit ports showed much higher failure rates. In these cases, the LLMs frequently introduced unintended changes—such as accidentally renaming a node elsewhere in the file or failing to bind a new device to the correct terminals. This suggests that LLMs do not "see" the circuit as a graph but rather as a sequence of tokens, making it difficult for them to maintain global structural integrity during an edit.

### The Decay of Compound Task Performance
One of the most striking findings was the degradation of performance as the number of required edits increased. While a model might have a $70\%$ to $80\%$ success rate for a 3-step compound edit, that accuracy often plummeted to $30\%$ or even near zero as the sequence length reached 15 steps.

![reliability-degradation-in-long-horizon-compound-tasks](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12197v1/Fig/compound_combined.png)
*{fig:reliability-degradation-in-long-horizon-compound-tasks}: LLM reliability significantly decreases as the number of dependent edits increases, indicating that errors compound over longer sequences of operations.*

This "compounding error" effect is a major obstacle for automated design. If an LLM-based agent is tasked with a complex optimization that requires dozens of small netlist changes, the probability of the model corrupting the netlist at some point in the sequence becomes extremely high.

## Mitigations: Reasoning and Representations

The study also explored whether specific techniques could mitigate these reliability issues, specifically focusing on "thinking" (reasoning) modes and alternative data representations.

### The Impact of Native Reasoning
The researchers compared standard models with models using "thinking" or reasoning modes (such as DeepSeek-V4 with its native thinking enabled). Enabling reasoning provided a substantial boost in pass rates. For example, DeepSeek's overall pass rate improved from $55\%$ to $81\%$ when reasoning was enabled. Similarly, Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting helped bridge the gap.

Statistical analysis showed these improvements were highly significant ($p < 10^{-6}$). The reasoning modes appeared to help the models better track dependencies and plan the structural changes before generating the final text. However, even with reasoning, the models still struggled with the longest compound tasks and complex hierarchical modifications, indicating that reasoning is a partial solution rather than a complete cure for the structure-preservation bottleneck.

### Alternative Representations (PySpice)
The paper also investigated whether representing the circuit in PySpice (a Python-based object-oriented netlist format) would be easier for LLMs than standard SPICE text. The hypothesis was that the explicit object-oriented structure of PySpice might align better with the LLM's training on general code. The results were mixed: while one model showed improvement ($p < 0.001$), another showed no significant change ($p = 0.51$). This suggests that the difficulty lies in the underlying graph logic of the circuit itself, rather than just the specific syntax of the SPICE language.

## Implications for the Future of AI in EDA

NetlistBench provides a clear quantification of the current limitations of LLMs in the hardware design domain. The core takeaway is that LLMs are currently not reliable enough to be treated as standalone, unverified netlist editors. The "structure-preservation bottleneck" means that even small, correct-looking edits can introduce catastrophic failures in the underlying circuit connectivity.

For the research community and industry practitioners, this work suggests several paths forward:
1.  **Decomposed Workflows:** Rather than asking an LLM to perform a complex series of edits in one go, workflows should break tasks into atomic, verifiable steps.
2.  **External Verification Oracles:** EDA tools must incorporate structure-aware verifiers—similar to the oracle used in NetlistBench—to check every LLM-generated netlist for unintended structural drift before it reaches simulation or layout.
3.  **Circuit-Specific Models:** There is a need for models that are either fine-tuned on graph-based circuit representations or architectures that can explicitly reason about connectivity rather than treating it as incidental text.

By isolating representation-level reliability from high-level design intelligence, NetlistBench offers a rigorous framework for tracking the progress of AI as it moves toward becoming a truly trustworthy partner in integrated circuit design.
