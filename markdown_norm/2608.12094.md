The problem of sphere packing—finding the densest way to arrange non-overlapping spheres in space—has long fascinated mathematicians. While the solution for three-dimensional space was conjectured by Kepler in 1611 and proven only recently, the problem becomes increasingly complex and structurally significant in higher dimensions. In 24-dimensional space, the Leech lattice stands as a uniquely dense and symmetric arrangement. In 2017, a team of researchers proved that the Leech lattice provides the optimal sphere packing in $\mathbb{R}^{24}$, building upon a linear programming framework established years earlier.

Despite this major result, a specific technical detail remained unresolved: the explicit construction and characterization of an "auxiliary function" that directly accounts for the 196,560 shortest vectors in the Leech lattice. This requirement, known as the 196560 auxiliary-function conjecture, was formulated by Henry Cohn and Abhinav Kumar in 2009. The recent work by Yutong Zhang and Yaoran Yang provides a rigorous analytical proof of this conjecture, utilizing the advanced framework of Fourier interpolation to construct and verify the properties of this function.

## The Linear Programming Method and Auxiliary Functions

The modern approach to sphere packing in high dimensions relies on a method developed by Henry Cohn and Noam Elkies in 2003. This method translates the geometric problem into a search for specific real-valued functions, known as auxiliary functions. If one can find a radial Schwartz function $f$ that satisfies certain conditions on its values and its Fourier transform $\hat{f}$, one can establish an upper bound on the density of any sphere packing in that dimension.

For the Leech lattice to be proven optimal, the method requires a function $f$ that is non-positive beyond a certain radius and whose Fourier transform is non-negative everywhere. Furthermore, for the bound to be "sharp"—meaning it exactly matches the density of the Leech lattice—the function and its Fourier transform must vanish at the distances corresponding to the lengths of all vectors in the lattice.

In 2017, such a function was constructed to prove the optimality of the Leech lattice. However, the 2009 conjecture by Cohn and Kumar focused on a slightly different, more specific auxiliary function $g: \mathbb{R}^{24} \to \mathbb{R}$. This function $g$ was conjectured to exist with the following properties:
1. $g(r) \leq 0$ for $r \geq \sqrt{6}$
2. $\hat{g}(r) \geq 0$ for $r \geq 0$
3. $g(2) > 0$
4. The numerical identity:
$$\frac{\hat{g}(0) - g(0)}{g(2)} = 196560$$

The constant 196,560 is not arbitrary; it is the "kissing number" of the Leech lattice, representing the number of shortest non-zero vectors (which have a squared length of 4, or radius $r=2$). Proving the existence of such a function $g$ provides a direct link between the linear programming bounds and the specific arithmetic structure of the Leech lattice.

## Fourier Interpolation and Basis Functions

The foundation of Zhang and Yang’s proof lies in the theory of Fourier interpolation, specifically a framework developed in 2022 by Cohn, Kumar, Miller, Radchenko, and Viazovska. This framework allows for the construction of radial Schwartz functions that take prescribed values and derivatives at specific points (roots of integers).

In 24 dimensions, the interpolation points are at radii $\sqrt{2n}$ for integers $n$. The researchers utilize a specific set of basis functions, $a_2$ and $b_2$, which are tailored to the $n=2$ case (the shell of shortest vectors). These basis functions have very precise properties: $a_2$ is defined such that $a_2(2)=1$ and it (along with its derivatives) vanishes at other lattice points, while $b_2$ vanishes at $r=2$ but has a specific derivative there.

These functions are constructed using modular forms, which are highly symmetric complex functions. The kernels used to generate $a_2$ and $b_2$ involve modular forms like the Eisenstein series $E_{12}$ and the Ramanujan delta function $\Delta$. By performing meticulous algebraic manipulations on the $q$-expansions (power series representations) of these modular forms, the authors can extract the exact coefficients needed to define the basis functions.

## Constructing the Auxiliary Function

To solve the conjecture, the authors propose a candidate function $g_C$ from an affine family:
$$g_C = a_2 - C b_2$$
Here, $C$ is a real parameter. Because of the interpolation properties of $a_2$ and $b_2$, the condition $g_C(2) = 1$ is automatically satisfied for any $C$, since $a_2(2)=1$ and $b_2(2)=0$. 

Furthermore, the construction ensures that $g_C$ and its Fourier transform $\hat{g}_C$ vanish at almost all necessary points. Specifically:
- $g_C(\sqrt{2n}) = 0$ and $g'_C(\sqrt{2n}) = 0$ for $n \geq 3$.
- $\hat{g}_C(\sqrt{2n}) = 0$ and $\hat{g}'_C(\sqrt{2n}) = 0$ for $n \geq 2$.

The central challenge is finding a value of $C$ that satisfies the sign conditions: $g_C(r) \leq 0$ for $r \geq \sqrt{6}$ and $\hat{g}_C(r) \geq 0$ for $r \geq 0$. The researchers identify the required $C$ by analyzing the ratios:
- $R(r) = a_2(r)/b_2(r)$
- $\hat{R}(r) = \hat{a}_2(r)/\hat{b}_2(r)$

By defining $C^*$ as the maximum value these ratios take over their respective domains, any $C \geq C^*$ will yield a function $g_C$ that satisfies the conjecture.

## Asymptotic Analysis and Sign Conditions

Proving that a valid $C^*$ exists—and that the sign conditions hold for all $r$ up to infinity—requires sophisticated asymptotic analysis. As the radius $r$ becomes very large, the behavior of the basis functions must be controlled. 

The authors employ a "Laplace-Bessel transfer" method. This involves looking at the behavior of the modular forms as the parameter $t \to 0^+$. In this limit, the modular forms are analyzed at their "S-cusp" (using the transformation $z \to -1/z$), where their behavior is dominated by exponential terms like $e^{-\pi/t}$. These modular asymptotics are then translated into the spatial domain ($r \to \infty$) using integral representations.

A key result of this analysis is that both ratios, $R(r)$ and $\hat{R}(r)$, converge to a common finite limit $\rho$ as $r \to \infty$. The authors calculate this limit exactly:
$$\rho = \frac{43 + 240 \log 2}{15}$$
To ensure that $C$ can be chosen to satisfy the sign conditions globally, the authors perform a refined analysis of the "first exponential correction." They prove that $R(r)$ approaches the limit $\rho$ from above, while $\hat{R}(r)$ approaches it from below. Specifically, they find a correction term of the form $\kappa e^{-2\pi(\sqrt{2}-1)r}$ where $\kappa > 0$. This determines that $C^* > \rho$, ensuring that $C^*$ is a finite, well-defined threshold.

Importantly, the authors maintain total analytical rigor throughout this process. They avoid numerical approximations or computer-assisted proofs for inequalities, relying instead on exact algebraic identities and elementary bounds such as $\pi < 22/7$ and $\log 2 < 7/10$.

## Recovery of Leech Shell Coefficients

Beyond the primary conjecture, the research demonstrates that the Fourier interpolation basis can be used to recover information about the Leech lattice structure itself. The number of vectors in a specific "shell" of the lattice (vectors of a fixed length) is related to the values of the interpolation functions at the origin.

The authors show that for any integer $m \geq 2$, the number of vectors $N_m$ in the Leech lattice with squared length $2m$ is given by:
$$N_m = \hat{a}_m(0) - a_m(0)$$
This relationship allows them to derive a formula for $N_m$ in terms of classical number-theoretic functions:
$$N_m = \frac{65520}{691}(\sigma_{11}(m) - \tau(m))$$
In this expression, $\sigma_{11}(m)$ represents the sum of the 11th powers of the divisors of $m$, and $\tau(m)$ is the Ramanujan tau function. This identity matches known results from the theory of modular forms, confirming that the Fourier interpolation framework is consistent with and reinforces the existing arithmetic understanding of the Leech lattice.

## Significance of the Work

The proof of the 196560 auxiliary-function conjecture completes a significant chapter in the study of the Leech lattice. While the optimality of the lattice for sphere packing was already established, this work provides the specific function that explains the lattice's density through its shortest vectors. 

The research highlights the maturity of the Fourier interpolation framework. By showing how to construct functions with precise sign properties and asymptotic limits, Zhang and Yang provide a methodology that could be applied to other exceptional structures in mathematics and physics. The transition from existence proofs to explicit, analytically verified constructions marks a step forward in the precision with which we can describe the relationship between geometry, modular forms, and optimal packing.
