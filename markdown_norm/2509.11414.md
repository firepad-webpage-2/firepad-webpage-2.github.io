## Introduction

Large multilingual language models have become increasingly capable, with recent models like Llama 3.1 and Qwen 2.5 supporting dozens of languages. However, a fundamental challenge remains: these models are trained on a fixed set of languages, and expanding their linguistic repertoire typically requires expensive full retraining. This paper addresses a critical real-world problem in multilingual NLP - how to continually add new languages to existing multilingual language models without access to the original training data.

![Overview of Methods](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures/2509.11414v1/img-0.jpeg)
*{fig:overview-of-methods}: Overview of the proposed LayRA method and sequential language addition strategies. LayRA applies LoRA adapters to selected layers (first 10 and final 2), while the sequential methods show different approaches for adding multiple languages over time.*

The research introduces Layer-Selective LoRA (LayRA), a parameter-efficient approach that selectively applies Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to specific transformer layers based on insights about how multilingual models process different languages. This work is particularly significant given that pre-training data for state-of-the-art models is often proprietary and unavailable, making traditional experience replay methods infeasible.

## Problem Formulation and Methodology

The authors formalize continual language learning as follows: given a pre-trained multilingual model $\theta_N$ supporting $N$ languages, the goal is to create $\theta_{N+n}$ that supports $n$ additional languages while maintaining performance on the original languages. Crucially, no access to the original pre-training data is assumed - a realistic constraint in practice.

The paper evaluates several baseline approaches:
- **Full CPT**: Naive continued pre-training on all parameters
- **LoRA CPT**: Applying LoRA adapters to all transformer layers
- **Layer-Selective Full CPT**: Fine-tuning only selected layers without parameter efficiency

### Layer-Selective LoRA (LayRA)

LayRA combines two key insights. First, LoRA has been shown to "learn less and forget less," providing better retention of previous knowledge. Second, multilingual transformers exhibit layer specialization:
- **Early layers**: Encode input in the source language
- **Middle layers**: Perform reasoning, often in a dominant language like English  
- **Final layers**: Generate output in the target language

Based on this understanding, LayRA applies LoRA adapters only to layers responsible for non-English processing (first 10 and final 2 layers, plus embedding and language modeling head) while freezing the middle layers that handle English-based reasoning.

### Sequential Learning Strategies

For adding multiple languages over time, two approaches are proposed:

**LayRA-series**: Applies LayRA iteratively, where each new set of languages is learned from the previously adapted model using model arithmetic:

$$
\theta_{N+n} = \theta_N + \lambda(\phi_{N+n} - \theta_N)
$$

where $\phi_{N+n}$ represents the adapted parameters and $\lambda$ controls the interpolation strength.

**LayRA-merge**: Adapts each new language set independently from the original model, then merges the specialized models:

$$
\theta^{merge}_{N+n+n'} = \mu\theta_{N+n} + (1-\mu)\theta_{N+n'}
$$

### Instruction-Following Transfer

To enable instruction-following capabilities in new languages without instruction-tuning data, the authors use model arithmetic by computing a "language vector" and adding it to an existing instruction-tuned model:

$$
\theta^{it}_{N+n} = \gamma(\theta_{N+n} - \theta_N) + \theta^{it}_N
$$

## Experimental Setup and Evaluation

The experiments use Llama 3.1 8B and Qwen 2.5 7B as base models, adding three typologically diverse languages:
- **Galician**: Mid-resource, Latin script, Romance language (related to Spanish/Portuguese)
- **Swahili**: Low-resource, Latin script, Bantu language (unrelated to existing languages)
- **Urdu**: Low-resource, Perso-Arabic script, Indo-Aryan (related to Hindi but different script)

Evaluation encompasses both pre-trained models (XNLI, PAWS-X, XCOPA, XStoryCloze) and instruction-tuned models (XNLI, MGSM, MMLU-Lite), measuring both retention (performance on original languages) and learning (performance on new languages).

## Key Findings

### LayRA's Superior Performance

LayRA consistently demonstrates the best retention-learning trade-off across all experimental conditions. The method significantly outperforms LoRA CPT in retention while remaining competitive in learning new languages.

![Learning vs Retention Trade-offs](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures/2509.11414v1/img-1.jpeg)
*{fig:learning-vs-retention-trade-offs}: Learning vs retention trade-offs for Swahili and Urdu adaptation using Llama 3.1. LayRA (red) shows superior retention compared to other methods while maintaining competitive learning performance.*

The results show LayRA provides over 1 point average improvement in 12 out of 19 experimental groups. Notably, LayRA exhibits the least catastrophic forgetting in English, supporting the hypothesis that preserving middle-layer reasoning capabilities is crucial.

### Impact of Language Characteristics

The research reveals significant effects of language typology:

**Positive Transfer**: Galician, being typologically similar to Spanish and Portuguese already in Llama 3.1, shows the strongest performance with LayRA. The model even demonstrates bidirectional transfer, sometimes improving English performance.

**Script Compatibility**: Swahili, despite being unrelated to existing languages, adapts well due to its Latin script and English loanwords. However, Urdu shows the poorest performance with Llama 3.1 due to Perso-Arabic script incompatibility with the tokenizer, leading to over-fragmentation. Interestingly, Qwen 2.5, which supports Arabic script, shows much better Urdu retention.

### Sequential Learning Results

LayRA-merge significantly outperforms LayRA-series for multi-stage adaptation, often matching or exceeding the "parallel" upper bound where all languages are added simultaneously. This demonstrates the effectiveness of independent adaptation followed by merging over iterative sequential learning.

## Significance and Implications

This research addresses a critical gap between theoretical continual learning research and practical multilingual NLP deployment. By developing replay-free methods that work without access to proprietary training data, the work democratizes the ability to expand multilingual language model capabilities.

The findings have several important implications:

**Practical Deployment**: Organizations can now extend existing multilingual models to support additional languages without requiring access to original training corpora or expensive full retraining.

**Low-Resource Language Support**: The ability to transfer instruction-following capabilities without explicit instruction-tuning data makes powerful language models more accessible to speakers of low-resource languages.

**Architectural Insights**: The validation of layer specialization in multilingual transformers provides valuable guidance for future model architectures and adaptation strategies.

**Tokenization Challenges**: The significant impact of script compatibility on adaptation success highlights the need for more universal tokenization approaches or vocabulary expansion techniques.

The research establishes a foundation for sustainable multilingual AI development, enabling models to evolve with changing linguistic needs while maintaining their existing capabilities. This is particularly crucial as the global AI community seeks to make language technologies more inclusive and accessible across the world's diverse linguistic landscape.
