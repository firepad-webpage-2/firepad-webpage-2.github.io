## The Shift Toward Optimal Biological Doses in Precision Oncology

Early-phase oncology trials have historically operated under the assumption that "more is better." This assumption led to the identification of the Maximum Tolerated Dose (MTD)—the highest dose of a drug that does not cause unacceptable side effects. For traditional cytotoxic chemotherapies, which kill rapidly dividing cells, efficacy typically increases alongside toxicity. However, the rise of targeted therapies and immunotherapies has complicated this relationship. These modern agents often exhibit a plateau in efficacy, where increasing the dose beyond a certain point does not improve the therapeutic effect but continues to increase risk. In some cases, efficacy may even decline at very high doses due to biological saturation or immune exhaustion.

Consequently, drug development is shifting toward identifying the Optimal Biological Dose (OBD), which balances safety and efficacy to provide the best benefit-risk profile for patients. This shift is supported by regulatory initiatives like Project Optimus from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), which emphasizes dose optimization earlier in the clinical development process. A critical challenge arises when a drug is tested in a basket trial—a design where a single treatment is evaluated across multiple patient subgroups (subtrials) defined by specific biomarkers or cancer types. Because these subgroups may respond differently to the same drug, a "one-size-fits-all" dose may be suboptimal or unsafe for certain populations.

The research presented here introduces a Bayesian Phase I/II basket design named DF-EXNEX. This design aims to identify subtrial-specific OBDs by integrating toxicity and efficacy data sequentially throughout the trial. The core of this approach is a robust information-borrowing mechanism that allows the model to share data between similar subtrials to increase efficiency, while maintaining the flexibility to treat distinct subtrials independently if their response patterns differ.

## Modeling Toxicity and Efficacy Simultaneously

To identify an OBD, a statistical model must account for both the probability of toxicity and the expected level of efficacy at each dose. The DF-EXNEX design uses two separate but related models to capture these dynamics across $K$ subtrials and $D$ ordered dose levels.

For safety, the probability of dose-limiting toxicity $p_{j,k}$ at dose level $j$ in subtrial $k$ is modeled using a monotone logistic regression:

$$\text{logit}(p_{j,k}) = \alpha_k + \exp(\beta_k) \log(d_j / d_{\text{ref}})$$

In this equation, $d_j$ is the dose value, $d_{\text{ref}}$ is a reference dose, and $\alpha_k$ and $\beta_k$ are parameters representing the baseline toxicity and the rate of increase, respectively. The use of $\exp(\beta_k)$ ensures that the toxicity probability always increases with the dose, which is a standard safety assumption in clinical trials.

For efficacy, the design assumes that the relationship between dose and response might be non-linear. Instead of a strictly increasing model, it employs a quadratic dose-response curve to capture potential plateaus or unimodal (bell-shaped) patterns. The mean efficacy $\mu_{j,k}$ is defined as:

$$\mu_{j,k} = a_k + b_k \log(d_j / d_{\text{ref}}) + c_k (\log(d_j / d_{\text{ref}}))^2$$

Here, $\theta^{(E)}_k = (a_k, b_k, c_k)$ represents the subtrial-specific parameters. This flexibility is essential for targeted therapies where the most effective dose might not be the highest dose. By modeling efficacy as a continuous variable rather than a binary "yes/no" response, the design can extract more information from smaller patient cohorts.

## Robust Information Borrowing with EXNEX

One of the most significant hurdles in basket trials is determining how much information to "borrow" between subtrials. If two cancer types are biologically similar, data from one can help refine the dose recommendations for the other. However, if they are different, forced borrowing (pooling data) can lead to incorrect dose selections that harm patients.

The DF-EXNEX design addresses this using the exchangeability–non-exchangeability (EXNEX) framework. For each subtrial $k$, the model considers three possible scenarios for the relationship between its parameters and those of the other subtrials:

1.  **Full Exchangeability ($F_1$):** The subtrial's parameters are assumed to come from a common distribution shared by all subtrials. This allows for maximum information sharing.
2.  **Partial Exchangeability ($F_2$):** Only certain characteristics, such as the shape of the dose-response curve ($\beta_k$), are shared, while the baseline levels ($\alpha_k$) remain independent. This is useful when subtrials have different baseline risks but similar drug sensitivities.
3.  **Non-Exchangeability ($F_3$):** The subtrial is analyzed independently using its own data. This acts as a "safety valve," preventing the model from being misled by data from dissimilar subtrials.

The model assigns a prior weight $\omega_h$ to each component (typically $1/3$ each), and as data $D_c$ accumulates, the posterior distribution updates these weights. If the observed outcomes in two subtrials look similar, the model naturally shifts more weight toward exchangeability, increasing the precision of the estimates. If they diverge, it shifts toward non-exchangeability.

## Utility-Based Dose Selection

In every step of the sequential trial, the design must decide which dose to give to the next cohort of patients. This is done by calculating an admissible set of doses and then selecting the one with the highest "utility."

A dose level $j$ is considered admissible for subtrial $k$ only if it meets two criteria:
-   **Safety:** The posterior probability that the toxicity rate $p_{j,k}$ is below an acceptable limit $p^*_T$ must be high: $\text{Pr}(p_{j,k} < p^*_T \mid D_c) > \epsilon_1$.
-   **Futility:** The posterior probability that the mean efficacy $\mu_{j,k}$ exceeds a minimum threshold $\mu^*_E$ must also be high: $\text{Pr}(\mu_{j,k} > \mu^*_E \mid D_c) > \epsilon_2$.

Doses that are too toxic or appear ineffective are excluded. Among the remaining admissible doses, the design selects the one that maximizes a utility function:

$$U_k(j) = \lambda_T (1 - p_{j,k}) + \lambda_E g(\mu_{j,k})$$

The utility $U_k(j)$ is a weighted sum where $\lambda_T$ and $\lambda_E$ represent the importance of safety and efficacy, respectively. The function $g(\mu_{j,k})$ transforms the continuous efficacy mean into a 0-to-1 scale comparable to the toxicity probability. This allows the clinician to explicitly define the trade-off between the risk of side effects and the benefit of treatment.

## Simulation Results and Performance

The researchers conducted an extensive simulation study covering 70 different scenarios to evaluate how the DF-EXNEX design performs compared to a standard design that does not borrow information (the NEX design). The simulations modeled a basket trial with 4 subtrials and 5 dose levels, using 30 patients per subtrial.

### Improved Accuracy and Safety
Across the 70 scenarios, the DF-EXNEX design showed a clear advantage. The geometric mean Proportion of Correct Selection (PCS)—a measure of how often the model correctly identifies the true OBD—increased by an average of 0.070 over the non-borrowing design. In 67 out of 70 scenarios, the robust borrowing design performed better.

Furthermore, the design proved safer. In scenarios where low doses were the only safe options, the Proportion of Overly Toxic Selection (PTS) dropped significantly from 0.115 to 0.036. This indicates that borrowing information helps the model recognize toxicity signals more quickly, protecting patients from exposure to dangerous dose levels.

### The Impact of Subtrial Similarity
As expected, the benefit of borrowing is most pronounced when the subtrials are similar. Figure 3 illustrates that when all subtrials share the same true OBD (similarity score = 1.00), the gain in selection accuracy is highest. However, the design remains robust even when subtrials are completely distinct (similarity score = 0.00), still providing a modest improvement over independent analysis by stabilizing the models.

In cases where subtrials were "partially" similar (e.g., two subtrials share an OBD and the other two have different ones), the EXNEX mechanism successfully identified these relationships. While the model sometimes suggested "compromise" doses in highly heterogeneous settings, these doses were typically near the true OBD and remained clinically acceptable.

## Conclusion and Future Directions

The DF-EXNEX design provides a structured, Bayesian framework for navigating the complexities of modern dose-finding. By moving beyond the MTD and incorporating both toxicity and efficacy into a single decision-making tool, it offers a pathway for more precise drug development. Its primary contribution lies in the robust EXNEX borrowing mechanism, which allows for statistical efficiency without the risks associated with naive data pooling.

The findings suggest that such designs can reduce the number of patients exposed to toxic doses and decrease the likelihood of stopping a trial early due to limited data in a single subgroup. Future iterations of this work may look to extend the model to handle different types of efficacy data (such as time-to-event outcomes) or to adapt to combination therapies where multiple drugs are titrated simultaneously. As precision medicine continues to evolve, adaptive designs like DF-EXNEX will be essential for ensuring that every patient subgroup receives the most effective and safest dose possible.
