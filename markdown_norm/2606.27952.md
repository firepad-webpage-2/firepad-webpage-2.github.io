## Understanding Multistate Processes and Occupation Probabilities

In fields such as medicine, demography, and economics, researchers often study individuals as they move through various stages or "states" over time. A patient might move from being "Healthy" to having an "Illness," and eventually to "Death." Multistate models (MSM) provide a formal mathematical framework for describing these transitions. A fundamental measure in these models is the occupation probability: the likelihood that an individual is in a specific state at a particular time point. For instance, in a clinical trial, one might want to know the probability that a patient is in a state of remission three years after starting a new treatment.

The solid lines represent the expected probabilities, while shaded areas indicate quantiles of the underlying distribution.*

Standard approaches to estimating these probabilities often rely on the "Markov property." This assumption posits that the probability of moving to a future state depends only on the current state and not on the history of how the individual reached that state. However, real-world processes are frequently non-Markovian; a patient's risk of disease progression may depend heavily on how long they have been ill or how many times they have relapsed in the past. When these historical dependencies are ignored, statistical estimates can become biased.

## Challenges of Coarsened Data in Longitudinal Studies

Estimating occupation probabilities is further complicated by the fact that we rarely observe the complete "latent" history of every individual. Data is usually "coarsened" through two primary mechanisms:

1.  **Baseline Exposure (Treatment Assignment):** In observational studies, the treatment an individual receives ($A \in \{0,1\}$) is not randomized but may be influenced by baseline characteristics like age or previous health status.
2.  **Right-Censoring:** Individuals may drop out of a study or the study may end before an event (like death) occurs. This censoring time $C$ prevents us from seeing the full path of the multistate process.

A critical challenge arises when the reason an individual is censored is related to their health status. If patients with worsening symptoms are more likely to drop out, the remaining observed data is no longer representative of the whole population. Existing methods often assume that censoring is independent of the process, but this is rarely true in complex longitudinal settings where censoring can depend on the evolving history of an individual’s state and time-varying covariates.

## Identifying Occupation Probabilities under Coarsening at Random

To address these challenges, the authors utilize the framework of Coarsening at Random (CAR). Under CAR, the probability of being censored at any given time depends only on the information observed up to that point—such as the sequence of states visited and recorded covariates—and not on future unobserved transitions.

The researchers define a full-data process for each hypothetical treatment intervention, denoted by $\bar{\mathcal{X}} = (\bar{\mathcal{X}}_0, \bar{\mathcal{X}}_1)$. Each process includes the multistate sequence $Z_a(t)$, time-dependent covariates $L_a(t)$, and baseline covariates $W$. The target parameter is the occupation probability:

$$\psi_{t_0, j}^{\bar{\mathcal{X}}}(F) := E_F[1\{Z_a(\tau_a \wedge t_0) = j\}]$$

To ensure this parameter can be estimated from observed data, three key assumptions are required:
*   **Consistency:** The observed data for treated individuals actually reflects the counterfactual "treated" process.
*   **Positivity:** For every possible set of patient characteristics, there is a non-zero probability of receiving either treatment and remaining uncensored ($pr(A=a|W)pr(C_a > t|\bar{\mathcal{X}}_a) > 0$).
*   **Exchangeability (CAR):** This assumes that, given the observed history, the treatment and censoring mechanisms are independent of the potential future outcomes.

Under these assumptions, the authors show that the target parameter is identified by an observed data estimand based on inverse probability weighting. This formula essentially adjusts the contribution of each observed individual to account for the probability that they were selected for treatment and remained uncensored.

## Deriving Efficient and Robust Estimators

While inverse probability weighting (IPW) provides a way to identify the target parameter, basic IPW estimators are often inefficient (having high variance) and sensitive to the correct specification of the weights. To improve upon this, the authors derive the Efficient Influence Function (EIF) for the occupation probability.

The EIF is a mathematical tool that describes how a statistical estimator changes in response to small perturbations in the underlying data distribution. It serves as the basis for creating "Augmented" Inverse Probability Weighted (AIPW) estimators. The researchers follow a three-step procedure to derive the EIF:
1.  Derive the score for the full data mapping.
2.  Map this score to the observed data space using weighting.
3.  Subtract the orthogonal projections of this score onto the spaces representing the treatment assignment and censoring mechanisms.

The resulting influence function is:

$$\text{IF}_{\psi_O} := \text{IF}_0 - \text{IF}_\pi - \text{IF}_G$$

Here, $\text{IF}_\pi$ accounts for the propensity score (the probability of treatment), and $\text{IF}_G$ accounts for the censoring mechanism. By incorporating these "augmentation" terms, the resulting estimators can use information from all participants—including those who were censored—by modeling their expected outcomes based on their observed history.

## The Hierarchy of AIPW Estimators

The paper introduces five different estimators to evaluate their performance:
*   **$\hat{\psi}_{0n}$ (IPW):** A standard weighted estimator.
*   **$\hat{\psi}_{\nu n}$:** An AIPW estimator augmented only by the propensity score term.
*   **$\hat{\psi}_{\mu n}$:** A fully augmented "one-step" estimator that uses the full EIF. This estimator is **doubly robust**, meaning it remains consistent even if one part of the statistical model (either the weighting part or the outcome modeling part) is incorrectly specified.
*   **$\hat{\psi}_{\nu,mod n}$ and $\hat{\psi}_{\mu,mod n}$ (Modified AIPW):** These versions use a regression technique (Ordinary Least Squares) to estimate the coefficients for the augmentation terms. This modification is designed to maintain efficiency and improve stability even when the models for treatment or censoring are slightly off.

The inclusion of the $Q(a, W)$ and $\eta(u, \bar{\mathcal{X}}(u), a)$ nuisance parameters—which represent the conditional expectation of the outcome given baseline and time-varying history—allows the AIPW estimators to "correct" for the bias introduced by the weights.

## Validating Performance via Simulation

The authors tested these estimators using a simulated three-state illness-death model where transitions were intentionally non-linear and path-dependent. In this scenario, individuals could move from "Healthy" to "Illness" and back, or transition to "Death" from either state.

The simulation results confirmed several theoretical predictions:
*   **Efficiency:** The fully augmented estimator $\hat{\psi}_{\mu n}$ consistently showed the lowest root mean square error (RMSE), meaning it was more precise than the standard IPW estimator.
*   **Double Robustness:** When the authors intentionally used a biased model for the propensity score (omitting important covariates), the standard IPW estimator failed significantly. However, the AIPW estimators maintained near-zero bias because the augmentation terms "compensated" for the incorrect weights.
*   **Confidence Intervals:** The $\hat{\psi}_{\mu n}$ estimator provided 95% confidence intervals that actually contained the true value approximately 95% of the time, validating its asymptotic properties.

## Implications for Complex Longitudinal Modeling

The significance of this work lies in its ability to handle "real-world" complexity without resorting to oversimplified assumptions. By moving away from the Markov requirement, the proposed estimators allow researchers to model systems where the past truly matters.

Key takeaways for practitioners include:
1.  **Robustness to Censoring:** The methods handle situations where censoring is "informative" (driven by the patient's history), which is a common but often ignored problem in clinical research.
2.  **Machine Learning Compatibility:** The mathematical structure of the one-step estimator ($\hat{\psi}_{\mu n}$) is compatible with machine learning tools. Because it is $\sqrt{n}$-consistent even if nuisance parameters converge slowly, researchers can use complex, non-parametric models to estimate the propensity scores and outcome expectations.
3.  **Broad Scope:** The framework is not limited to simple survival models; it applies to any multistate process with any number of states, including recurrent events and competing risks.

In summary, this research provides a statistically rigorous path for estimating occupation probabilities that is both efficient and resilient to model misspecification, offering a more reliable toolkit for analyzing how individuals progress through complex life and health stages.
