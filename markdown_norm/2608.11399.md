The Cox proportional hazards model is a cornerstone of modern survival analysis. Since its introduction in 1972, it has become the standard tool for researchers across medicine, public health, and the social sciences to examine how specific factors—covariates—influence the time until an event occurs, such as death or disease recurrence. While the model's theoretical foundation was built on the assumption of continuous time, where no two individuals experience an event at the exact same moment, real-world data often tells a different story. In practice, events are frequently recorded in discrete intervals (e.g., months or years) or at scheduled follow-up visits, leading to "tied" failure times where multiple subjects have the same recorded event time.

Despite the prevalence of ties, the formal statistical theory establishing the efficiency of estimators in these scenarios has remained incomplete for decades. This paper addresses this gap by deriving the semiparametric efficiency bound for the Cox regression coefficient when the failure distribution is general—meaning it can be continuous, discrete, or a mix of both. The research rigorously proves that the original "exact" estimator proposed by Cox for tied data is indeed efficient and introduces a new, computationally faster alternative that achieves the same statistical performance.

## The Cox Model with Ties

The traditional Cox model relates the hazard function of an individual to a set of baseline covariates $L$ through a regression coefficient $\beta$. Under the assumption of continuity, the hazard for an individual with covariates $L$ is expressed as the baseline hazard multiplied by the exponentiated linear predictor, $\exp(L^\top \beta)$. However, when ties are possible, the relationship must be defined more broadly to accommodate mass points in the failure distribution.

In this work, the author utilizes a generalized formulation of the Cox model. For any failure distribution, the increment of the conditional cumulative hazard at time $u$ for an individual with covariates $L$, denoted as $\delta \Lambda^{\#}_T(u; L)$, is related to the baseline increment $\delta \Lambda^{\#}_T(u; 0)$ by the following relationship:

$$\delta \Lambda^{\#}_T(u; \ell) = \frac{\delta \Lambda^{\#}_T(u; 0) \exp(\ell^\top \beta)}{1 - \delta \Lambda^{\#}_T(u; 0) + \delta \Lambda^{\#}_T(u; 0) \exp(\ell^\top \beta)}$$

This formulation is elegant because it unifies two distinct statistical models. When the time distribution is continuous, the increments $\delta \Lambda^{\#}_T(u; 0)$ are infinitesimal, and the expression simplifies to the standard proportional hazards model. Conversely, if the time distribution is purely discrete, this expression corresponds to a proportional odds model for the discrete hazards. By using this generalized form, the researcher ensures that the subsequent efficiency results apply to any observed failure-time data, regardless of how "tied" it might be.

The observed data for each of $n$ individuals consist of the triplet $(L, X, \Delta)$, where $L$ represents baseline covariates, $X$ is the time at-risk, and $\Delta$ is the failure indicator. To handle the intricacies of censoring and failures in this general setting, the author defines several stochastic processes. These include the standard at-risk process $Y(u) = I(X \geq u)$, which tracks whether an individual is still being observed at time $u$, and a modified process $Y^{\dagger}(u) = I\{N_C(u-) = 0, N_T(u) = 0\}$, which explicitly accounts for the timing of censoring relative to failure.

## Semiparametric Efficiency and the Influence Function

A central goal of semiparametric theory is to determine the "best" possible performance an estimator can achieve. This is quantified by the semiparametric efficiency bound, which represents the minimum variance any regular, asymptotically linear estimator can have. To find this bound, researchers identify the Efficient Influence Function (EIF).

The author begins by characterizing the nuisance tangent space—the set of all possible scores generated by "nuisance" parameters that are not of primary interest, such as the baseline hazard and the distribution of covariates. In the Cox model, the primary parameter is $\beta$, while the baseline hazard $\Lambda^{\#}_T(\cdot; 0)$ and the censoring mechanism are nuisances. By applying martingale theory, specifically identifying the martingales associated with the failure process $M^{\#}_T(t; L)$ and the censoring process $M^{\dagger}_C(t; L)$, the researcher isolates the component of the data that contains information about $\beta$.

The resulting Efficient Influence Function for $\beta$ is derived as the projection of the score function onto the orthogonal complement of the nuisance tangent space. This derivation leads to a general efficient score:

$$S_{\text{eff}}(\beta, \Lambda) = \int \left[ L - \frac{E[L \zeta(L, u, \beta, \Lambda) Y(u)]}{E[\zeta(L, u, \beta, \Lambda) Y(u)]} \right] dM^{\#}_T(u; L)$$

where the weighting term $\zeta$ is defined as:

$$\zeta(L, u, \beta, \Lambda) = \frac{\exp(L^\top \beta)}{1 - \delta \Lambda^{\#}_T(u; 0) + \delta \Lambda^{\#}_T(u; 0) \exp(L^\top \beta)}$$

This result is significant because it provides a universal benchmark for efficiency that does not rely on the assumption that ties are absent. It shows that an efficient estimator must correctly weight the contributions of both continuous-time failures and discrete-time mass points.

## The Asymptotic Efficiency of the Exact Cox Estimator

Since 1972, the "exact" partial likelihood estimator, $\hat{\beta}_{\text{Cox}}$, has been the gold standard for handling ties. This estimator considers all possible orderings of tied events that could have occurred if the data had been recorded in continuous time. While intuitively appealing, proving that this estimator actually reaches the theoretical efficiency bound has been a "major outstanding problem" for over 50 years.

The author provides a rigorous proof of this efficiency. By assuming that the set of population failure mass points $J(P_0)$ is finite, the researcher demonstrates that $\hat{\beta}_{\text{Cox}}$ is consistent and has an influence function that matches the EIF derived earlier. The proof leverages connections between the exact partial likelihood and rejective sampling theory. Specifically, the probability of observing a specific set of failures among those at risk at a tied time point can be viewed through the lens of conditional logistic regression, where the "exact" calculation effectively manages the combinatorial complexity of the ties.

This finding confirms that Cox’s 1972 proposal was not just a heuristic approximation but a theoretically optimal method for the general Cox model. However, the exact estimator comes with a high computational cost. When the number of tied events at a single time point $m$ is large, the number of permutations becomes enormous. Even with dynamic programming, evaluating the score function has a computational complexity of $\mathcal{O}(m^2)$, which can lead to numerical instability or excessive runtimes in large datasets.

## A Computationally Efficient Alternative

Recognizing the limitations of the exact estimator, the researcher proposes a new estimator, $\tilde{\beta}_n$, designed to be both efficient and fast. The key to this new approach lies in how the baseline cumulative hazard is handled. 

Instead of dealing with the permutations of ties directly, the proposed method uses a "profiled" estimator for the baseline increments $\tilde{\Lambda}^{\#}_{T,n}(\cdot; 0, \beta)$. For each time $u$ where failures occur, the estimator solves an equation to find the baseline hazard increment that balances the observed failures with the expected failures under the current model. When this profiled baseline hazard is plugged back into the efficient score equation, many terms cancel out, resulting in a simplified estimating equation for $\beta$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \int \left[ L_i - \frac{\sum_{j=1}^n L_j Y_j(u) \tilde{\zeta}(L_j, u, \beta, \Lambda)}{\sum_{j=1}^n Y_j(u) \tilde{\zeta}(L_j, u, \beta, \Lambda)} \right] dN_{T,i}(u) = 0$$

The computational complexity of evaluating this score is only $\mathcal{O}(m)$ for a set of $m$ ties, a significant improvement over the $\mathcal{O}(m^2)$ of the exact method. In large-scale genomic or clinical studies where ties are pervasive, this difference can transform a calculation from one that takes hours into one that takes seconds. The author proves that this estimator is "locally efficient," meaning it achieves the semiparametric efficiency bound when the model is correctly specified.

## Empirical Performance and Practical Impact

To validate the theoretical results, the author conducted a simulation study comparing the proposed estimator, the exact Cox estimator, and Efron’s approximation. Efron's method is a popular shortcut often used because it is faster than the exact method, but it is known to be an approximation.

## Conclusion

This research provides a long-awaited theoretical completion to the Cox proportional hazards model. By deriving the efficiency bound for general failure distributions and proving the efficiency of the exact Cox estimator, the author has unified five decades of survival analysis theory. Furthermore, the introduction of a computationally efficient, asymptotically equivalent estimator provides practitioners with a powerful new tool. This new method allows for rigorous, optimal inference on large-scale datasets with tied observations, ensuring that statistical accuracy does not have to be sacrificed for computational feasibility. As survival analysis continues to move toward larger and more complex datasets, these theoretical and algorithmic advancements ensure that the Cox model remains a reliable and efficient foundation for scientific discovery.
