## The Puzzle of Relative Performance

In many competitive environments, performance is measured relative to others rather than on an absolute scale. In the labor market, employees are often ranked against their peers for promotions; in education, students may be graded on a curve; and in professional sports, success is defined by wins and losses against opponents. A central challenge in these systems is that if every participant improves simultaneously—perhaps due to a shared technological advancement—their relative rankings and measures may remain unchanged, masking a significant shift in absolute capability.

This research utilizes classical chess as a case study to investigate how a widespread technology shock affects outcomes when the primary measure of skill, the Elo rating, is purely relative. The Elo system updates a player's rating based on the difference between their actual game score and their expected score, which is calculated from the rating difference between them and their opponent. If both players in a matchup improve their absolute playing strength by an equal amount, their ratings will likely stay the same because their relative advantage has not shifted. However, the nature of the games they play—specifically the likelihood of a win, loss, or draw—might change fundamentally.

The paper focuses on a specific period starting in late 2020, following the release of advanced chess engines that utilized neural network architectures. Despite a significant increase in the frequency of draws in classical chess shortly after this period, the distribution of player ratings remained remarkably stable. The authors set out to reconcile these two observations: a drastic shift in game outcomes and the apparent stasis of the relative skill measure.

## The Technology Shock: Neural Network Engines

The catalyst for the observed changes was a fundamental shift in the technology used to analyze chess. For decades, chess engines relied on "hand-crafted" evaluation functions, where human experts programmed specific heuristics—such as the value of pieces or the safety of the king—to help the computer assess a position. In September 2020, the release of Stockfish 12 introduced Efficiently Updatable Neural Networks (NNUE) to the most widely used open-source chess engine.

This technological transition allowed engines to evaluate positions with a depth and nuance previously unavailable. Stockfish 12 was estimated to be approximately 130 Elo points stronger than its immediate predecessor, a significant jump in a field where progress is typically incremental. Because these engines are free and run on standard consumer hardware, the "technology shock" was accessible to the entire chess-playing population simultaneously. Players could use these tools for opening preparation, post-game analysis, and studying complex endgames, potentially leading to a rapid and widespread improvement in defensive and technical accuracy.

## Quantifying the Outcome Shift

To investigate the impact of this shock, the researchers analyzed a dataset of approximately 3.9 million classical games played between March 2015 and November 2023. They identified a sharp break in the data following the COVID-19 hiatus (which ended around August 2021 for the purposes of this study). 

Before the pandemic, the average draw rate in classical chess was stable at approximately 24 percent. After the interruption and the introduction of NNUE technology, the draw rate rose to approximately 28 percent. This 4-percentage-point increase represents a substantial change in the landscape of competitive chess. The increase in draws was not a continuation of any previous trend; rather, it was a discrete step upward that has persisted through the end of the sample period in late 2023.

The rise in draws was mirrored by a decline in decisive results. White-win probabilities fell by an average of 1.7 percentage points, while Black-win probabilities fell by 2.5 percentage points. Notably, the increase in draw rates was more pronounced at higher rating levels, where players are more likely to utilize engine-based preparation effectively. However, the shift was observed across a wide range of skill levels, suggesting the technology's influence was not limited to the elite grandmaster level.

## Methodology: Mapping Ratings to Probabilities

The researchers modeled the probability of different game outcomes as a function of the players' ratings. They defined two primary variables to capture the dynamics of a matchup: the average rating of the two players $A$ and the rating difference between them $D$. These are calculated as:

$$A = \frac{R_W + R_B}{2}$$
$$D = R_W - R_B$$

where $R_W$ is the rating of the player with the white pieces and $R_B$ is the rating of the player with the black pieces. To account for non-linearities in how ratings translate to outcomes, the authors used penalized tensor-product splines to estimate the outcome surfaces. They normalized these variables for the analysis:

$$A_c = \frac{A - 2000}{100}$$
$$D_c = \frac{D}{100}$$

By comparing the outcome surfaces generated from the pre-Covid period (2015–2020) with those from the post-Covid period (2021–2023), the authors could visualize exactly how the "map" from ratings to results had changed. The results showed that for any given pair of ratings, the probability of a draw had shifted upward, and the advantage traditionally held by the higher-rated player (the "slope" of the win probability relative to $D$) had flattened.

## Resilience to Compositional Changes

A potential critique of observing aggregate changes in draw rates is that the composition of the playing pool might have changed. For instance, if the post-Covid period saw more games between highly-rated players (who draw more often naturally), the average draw rate would rise even if no individual player's behavior changed.

To address this, the authors performed a series of robustness checks using increasingly restrictive samples. They moved from the full dataset to a "repeated-players" sample, then to a "repeated-pairs" sample, and finally to a "repeated same-color-pairs" sample. The latter only includes matchups where the exact same two players met with the same color assignment both before and after the technology shock. 

Even in the most restrictive "same-color-pair" sample, the increase in the draw rate remained at approximately 4 percentage points. The researchers also employed a regression model to control for the specific ratings in each game:

$$\text{Draw}_{ij} = \alpha \cdot \text{Post}_t + m(A_{ij}, D_{ij}) + \epsilon_{ij}$$

In this model, $\alpha$ represents the shift in draw probability that cannot be explained by changes in the ratings ($A$ and $D$) of the players. The results consistently pointed to an intrinsic change in the game's outcome patterns rather than a shift in who was playing or how they were matched.

## Rating Relabeling and Skill Gains

The authors then asked if the post-Covid outcome surface could be explained by a simple re-scaling of the ratings. They tested whether the players in the post-Covid era were effectively playing "as if" they had higher pre-Covid ratings. They estimated a linear transformation $f(R)$ to map post-Covid ratings to a pre-Covid equivalent:

$$f(R) = a + bR$$

By minimizing the difference between the post-Covid draw surface and the transformed pre-Covid surface, they found the following relationship:

$$f(R) = 559.9 + 0.792R$$

This transformation suggests that a player rated 2000 in the post-Covid period plays with the drawing-resilience equivalent to a 2144-rated player from the pre-Covid era. Interestingly, the gain is even larger for lower-rated players. A player rated 1700 post-Covid is equivalent to a 1906-rated player pre-Covid (a 206-point gain). 

This "rating-equivalent" gain indicates that the technology shock provided the greatest marginal benefit to players at lower rungs of the competitive ladder, potentially by providing them with the tools to defend more accurately against stronger opponents. The researchers also tested whether rating differences $D$ needed a separate scaling factor $\sigma$:

$$D^* = \frac{bD}{\sigma}$$

However, they found that $\sigma$ was close to 1, meaning the primary change was a shift in the absolute level of play rather than a change in the relative importance of rating gaps.

## Reconciling Results with Rating Stability

One of the most striking findings is that despite these massive shifts in win and draw probabilities, the actual distribution of Elo ratings did not drift significantly. In many systems, if a group of participants improves, one might expect their scores to rise. But in a relative system like Elo, ratings only change if a player performs differently than *expected* given their current rating.

The authors calculated the "expected score" for players—where a win is 1 point, a draw is 0.5 points, and a loss is 0 points—across the outcome surfaces. They found that while the probabilities of wins and losses both fell (replaced by draws), the net effect on the expected score was very small. For a higher-rated player, the loss in win probability was largely offset by the reduction in loss probability, resulting in an expected score that remained nearly identical to the pre-shock era.

Because the Elo update mechanism depends on the gap between the actual result and the expected score, and because the expected scores remained stable, the ratings themselves remained stable. This explains how a "hidden" improvement in absolute skill can occur without being reflected in the standard metrics used to rank and sort participants.

## Implications for Broader Economics

The findings from this study extend beyond the chessboard. They provide a clear empirical example of how technological progress can be "invisible" to relative performance measures. 

In a corporate setting, if a new AI tool makes every accountant 20% more efficient, a firm's internal ranking of accountants might remain exactly the same. If promotions are based on that ranking, the increased productivity of the workforce will not be reflected in the promotion rates or the internal "ratings" of the employees. Similarly, in competitive markets, if all firms adopt a cost-saving technology, their market shares (a relative measure) might stay constant even as the entire industry becomes more efficient.

The study also highlights that technology shocks do not always favor the elite. In chess, the neural-network engines appear to have acted as an equalizer, providing larger "rating-equivalent" gains to lower-rated players and making it harder for top players to convert small advantages into wins. This suggests that the impact of technology on inequality in competitive environments depends heavily on whether the technology aids "attack" (increasing the gap between participants) or "defense" (allowing the trailing party to hold their ground). In the case of classical chess and neural networks, the evidence points strongly toward the latter.
