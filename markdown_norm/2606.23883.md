In quantitative finance, the concept of implied volatility serves as a critical bridge between theoretical models and observed market prices. For European options, which grant the right to buy (call) or sell (put) an asset at a specific strike price $K$ at maturity $T$, the implied volatility is the parameter that, when plugged into a standard pricing model, equates the model price to the market price. While the Black-Scholes (lognormal) model is the most common convention, the Bachelier (normal) model is increasingly relevant, particularly for assets that can exhibit negative prices or near-zero rates.

A central challenge in financial engineering is ensuring that a "smile"—the collection of implied volatilities across different strikes for a fixed maturity—does not allow for arbitrage. Static no-arbitrage requires that option prices satisfy specific structural properties, such as monotonicity and convexity. Research in this area often relies on "normalized coordinates," which are non-linear transformations of the strike and volatility. This work explores the fundamental monotonicity of these coordinates, demonstrating that they are not merely mathematical conveniences of specific models but are deeply rooted in the basic principles of no-arbitrage.

## Foundations of Static No-Arbitrage

To analyze the behavior of implied volatility, one must first define the conditions under which a set of option prices is considered free from static arbitrage. For a fixed maturity $T$ and a forward price $F$, the market prices for calls $C(K)$ and puts $P(K)$ must adhere to several key requirements:

1.  **Monotonicity and Convexity:** Call prices $C(K)$ must be strictly decreasing and convex in strike $K$. Conversely, put prices $P(K)$ must be strictly increasing and convex in $K$.
2.  **Put-Call Parity:** The relationship $C(K) - P(K) = F - K$ must hold for all strikes.
3.  **Boundary Conditions:** In the lognormal framework, $C(0) = F$ and $\lim_{K \to \infty} C(K) = 0$. In the normal framework, the call price must satisfy $\lim_{K \to -\infty} \{C(K) - (F - K)\} = 0$ and $\lim_{K \to \infty} C(K) = 0$.

Historically, proofs regarding the monotonicity of normalized coordinates have assumed a "continuous-strike" framework. This assumes that option quotes exist for every possible strike price and that the resulting smile is a differentiable function. However, real-world market data consists of a finite number of discrete quotes. This paper addresses this gap by providing proofs that operate at the discrete-strike level. It demonstrates that if any two discrete points on an option chain satisfy the basic no-arbitrage inequalities, the normalized coordinates derived from them will exhibit strict monotonicity.

## Monotonicity in the Bachelier (Normal) Framework

The Bachelier model assumes that the underlying asset price follows a normal distribution. In this context, the paper defines the normalized coordinate $x(K)$ as follows:

$$
x(K) = \frac{F - K}{\sigma_N(K)}
$$

Here, $\sigma_N(K)$ represents the Bachelier total implied volatility for strike $K$. The pricing formula for a Bachelier call can be expressed using helper functions based on the standard normal density $\phi(x)$ and cumulative distribution function $\Phi(x)$:

$$
C(K) = \sigma_N(K) [\phi(x(K)) + x(K)\Phi(x(K))]
$$

Let $h(x) = \phi(x) + x\Phi(x)$ be the pricing function for a call where the volatility is normalized to unity. Similarly, let $\bar{h}(x) = \phi(x) - x\Phi(-x)$ represent the normalized put price. A crucial property of these functions is that $h(x)$ is strictly increasing in $x$, and $\bar{h}(x)$ is strictly decreasing in $x$.

The paper proves that $x(K)$ is strictly decreasing in $K$. This means that for any two strikes $K_1 < K_2$, the corresponding coordinates satisfy $x_1 > x_2$. The proof proceeds by checking different regions of the strike space:

*   **Case 1 ($K_1 < F < K_2$):** By definition, $x_1 > 0$ and $x_2 < 0$, so $x_1 > x_2$ holds naturally.
*   **Case 2 ($F < K_1 < K_2$):** In this region, both $x_1$ and $x_2$ are negative. If we were to assume that $x_1 \leq x_2$ and that the implied volatility $\sigma_2 \geq \sigma_1$, then because $h(x)$ is increasing, it would imply $C(K_1) \leq C(K_2)$. This would violate the no-arbitrage rule that call prices must decrease as the strike increases.
*   **Case 3 ($K_1 < K_2 < F$):** Here, $x_1$ and $x_2$ are positive. A similar contradiction arises using the put price function $\bar{h}(x)$ and the fact that put prices must be increasing in strike.

By grounding the proof in these case-based comparisons of discrete points, the monotonicity of $x(K)$ is established as a direct consequence of $C(K)$ being decreasing and $P(K)$ being increasing.

## Monotonicity in the Black-Scholes (Lognormal) Framework

In the Black-Scholes setting, it is standard to work with log-forward moneyness $k = \ln(K/F)$. The paper defines the central normalized coordinate $z(k)$ as:

$$
z(k) = \frac{k}{v(k)}
$$

In this expression, $v(k)$ is the total Black-Scholes implied volatility (often denoted as $\sigma \sqrt{T}$). This $z(k)$ coordinate is the midpoint of two other well-known transformations, $f_1$ and $f_2$, which are related to the $d_1$ and $d_2$ terms in the Black-Scholes formula:

$$
f_1 = \frac{k}{v} - \frac{v}{2}
$$

$$
f_2 = \frac{k}{v} + \frac{v}{2}
$$

The paper proves that $z(k)$ is strictly increasing in $k$, which is equivalent to saying it is strictly decreasing in $K$. The methodology mirrors the Bachelier case but utilizes properties specific to lognormal pricing. Specifically, it uses the fact that the call price $c(z,v)$ (normalized by the forward price) is strictly decreasing in $z$, while the put price $\tilde{p}(z,v)$ (normalized by the strike price) is strictly increasing in $z$.

A notable result in this section is the use of the inequality $P(K_1)/K_1 < P(K_2)/K_2$ for $K_1 < K_2$, which is a consequence of the convexity of the put price and the boundary condition $P(0) = 0$. By showing that $z(k)$ must increase with $k$ to avoid violating these price bounds, the paper provides a finite-strike proof that does not depend on the differentiability of the volatility smile.

## A Model-Free Normal-Variance Identity

Beyond monotonicity, the paper extends model-free valuation identities to the Bachelier framework. In the lognormal world, previous research established that the expected variance of an asset can be expressed as an integral of Black-Scholes implied volatilities. This paper derives the equivalent for the Bachelier model.

The objective is to calculate the expected remaining normal variance, defined as:

$$
E[(S_T - F)^2]
$$

Using the Breeden-Litzenberger result, which states that the risk-neutral density $q(K)$ is the second derivative of the call price with respect to the strike ($C_{KK}(K) = q(K)$), the expected variance can be written as an integral over all strikes. Through a series of steps involving integration by parts and a change of variables to the normalized coordinate $x = x(K)$, the paper arrives at the following identity:

$$
E[(S_T - F)^2] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \sigma_N(x)^2 \phi(x) dx
$$

This result is significant because it is "model-free"—it does not assume a specific stochastic process for the underlying volatility. Instead, it shows that the total variance is a weighted average of the squared Bachelier implied volatilities, where the weights are given by the standard normal density. The monotonicity of $x(K)$ proven earlier is the technical requirement that justifies this change of variables, ensuring that every strike $K$ maps uniquely to a coordinate $x$.

## Practical Implications for Market Analysis

The move from continuous-strike theory to discrete-strike proofs has immediate practical utility. Most arbitrage-detection algorithms rely on smoothing or interpolating market quotes to create a continuous surface before checking for violations. However, the interpolation process itself can introduce "artificial" arbitrage or mask existing ones.

By proving that the monotonicity of $x(K)$ and $z(k)$ must hold for any two discrete quotes, the paper provides a simple, model-independent diagnostic tool. Market participants can verify the health of their option chains by checking:

1.  **Direct Monotonicity Checks:** For any two quotes $(K_1, \sigma_1)$ and $(K_2, \sigma_2)$, the calculated normalized coordinates must respect the ordering established in the proofs. If $x(K_1) \leq x(K_2)$ for $K_1 < K_2$, a static arbitrage opportunity (specifically a violation of price monotonicity) exists in the data.
2.  **Smile Parameterization:** When fitting parametric models to volatility smiles (such as the SVI model), these monotonicity results provide hard constraints that the parameters must satisfy to ensure the model remains arbitrage-free.
3.  **Variance Swap Pricing:** The normal-variance identity provides a robust way to value products like variance swaps in a Bachelier environment. By integrating market-implied volatilities against the normal density in $x$-space, practitioners can obtain an estimate of expected variance that is consistent with the current option market.

In conclusion, the investigation into normalized implied-volatility coordinates clarifies why certain transformations of the volatility smile are mathematically "natural." By demonstrating that their monotonicity is a fundamental property of no-arbitrage rather than an artifact of specific diffusion models, the work reinforces the theoretical framework used for both Black-Scholes and Bachelier pricing conventions. The derivation of the normal-variance identity further completes the parallel between these two models, offering a unified perspective on model-free valuation.
