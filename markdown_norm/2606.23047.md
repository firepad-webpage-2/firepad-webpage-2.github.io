## Introduction to Green AI in Wireless Networks

As the telecommunications industry transitions toward the sixth generation (6G) of wireless technology, the deployment of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) models has become a cornerstone for managing network complexity. 6G networks are expected to handle a tenfold increase in traffic compared to current standards, requiring automated solutions for interference classification, channel estimation, and seamless handover decisions. However, this shift toward "AI-native" networks brings a significant challenge: the environmental footprint of AI itself.

The Information and Communication Technology (ICT) sector contributed approximately 1.4% of global greenhouse gas emissions in 2020. With sustainability being a primary goal for 6G, the research community is increasingly focused on "Green AI"—the development of AI solutions that are energy-efficient during both training and operation. One of the most persistent obstacles in wireless AI is "domain shift." A model trained in a controlled simulation (the source domain) often fails when deployed in a live network (the target domain) due to differences in user mobility, signal-to-noise ratios, and physical environments.

Traditionally, addressing domain shift requires retraining models from scratch using site-specific, labeled data. This approach is often impractical because collecting and labeling data in live networks is expensive and time-consuming. While Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA) offers a way to adapt models to new environments without needing new labels, its actual energy efficiency has remained largely unquantified. This research provides a systematic comparison between UDA and traditional retraining methods to determine when UDA becomes the more sustainable choice.

## The Challenge of Domain Shift and Wireless Constraints

Wireless environments are characterized by extreme dynamism. A model optimized for a high-density urban cell may perform poorly in a rural area or during peak traffic hours. These variations, known as distribution shifts, necessitate constant model updates. While large foundation models (like those used in natural language processing) offer strong generalization, they are generally unsuitable for wireless edge deployment. The strict latency requirements of 6G—often requiring responses in the sub-millisecond range—conflict with the high computational demands and slow inference speeds of massive generative models.

Consequently, wireless AI must rely on lightweight, specialized models. When these models encounter a new domain, two main strategies emerge:
1.  **Independent Classification (IC):** Retraining the model from scratch for every new deployment using fresh, labeled data.
2.  **Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA):** Using the knowledge from a pre-trained source model and adapting it to the target domain using only unlabeled data.

UDA is theoretically appealing because it eliminates the need for manual labeling. However, UDA pipelines often involve additional components, such as adversarial networks or distribution-matching objectives, which can increase the computational complexity of the training process. The central question of this study is whether the benefits of UDA outweigh these potential energy costs.

## Methodology for Carbon Accounting

To provide an objective comparison, the researchers adopted a rigorous framework for estimating the carbon footprint of AI lifecycles. The carbon footprint $CF$ (measured in grams of $CO_2$ equivalent) is calculated based on the carbon intensity of the electricity grid $CI$, the Power Usage Effectiveness of the hardware infrastructure $PUE$, and the total energy consumed $E$:

$$CF = CI \times PUE \times E$$

The lifecycle of the AI pipeline was divided into five distinct stages to pinpoint where energy is consumed:
-   **Preprocess ($c1$):** Transforming raw input data into features.
-   **Tune ($c2$):** Hyperparameter optimization to find the best model configuration.
-   **Train ($c3$):** Fitting the model to the data.
-   **Predict ($c4$):** Generating outputs during inference.
-   **Score ($c5$):** Evaluating performance.

By tracking energy consumption across these stages using tools like CodeCarbon, the researchers were able to compare three primary strategies:
-   **Independent Classification (IC):** Each target domain requires full retraining and labeling.
-   **Sequential UDA (UDA-S):** A source model is trained once and adapted to each target domain individually.
-   **Joint UDA (UDA-J):** A source model is trained once and adapted to multiple target domains simultaneously in a single run.

## Comparative Cost Models and Break-Even Analysis

The study developed mathematical models to predict the total energy cost as a function of the number of target domains $N$.

For Independent Classification, the cost scales linearly with $N$ and includes a fixed initial cost for the source domain:
$$E^{(c)}_{IC,\text{total}}(N) = E^{(c)}_{\text{source}} + N \times E^{(c)}_{\text{ind}}$$
where $E^{(c)}_{\text{ind}}$ represents the cost of labeling and training for a single target domain.

For Sequential UDA, the labels for target domains are not required, reducing the per-domain cost:
$$E^{(c)}_{UDA\text{-}S,\text{total}}(N) = E^{(c)}_{\text{source}} + N \times E^{(c)}_{\text{adapt}}$$
where $E^{(c)}_{\text{adapt}}$ is the cost of adapting the model to one target domain.

For Joint UDA, the adaptation happens once for all $N$ domains:
$$E^{(c)}_{UDA\text{-}J,\text{total}}(N) = E^{(c)}_{\text{source}} + E^{(c)}_{\text{adapt,joint}}(N)$$

A critical contribution of this paper is the identification of the "break-even point" $N^*$, which is the minimum number of target domains required for a UDA strategy to become more energy-efficient than retraining from scratch. The break-even points are calculated as follows:
-   For UDA-S: $N^{*}_{S} = E^{(c)}_{\text{source}} / (E^{(c)}_{\text{ind}} - E^{(c)}_{\text{adapt}})$
-   For UDA-J: $N^{*}_{J} = (E^{(c)}_{\text{source}} + E^{(c)}_{\text{adapt,joint}}) / E^{(c)}_{\text{ind}}$

## Energy Consumption Patterns in UDA Pipelines

Experimental results across multiple datasets (such as Human Activity Recognition and mechanical fault diagnosis) revealed that the **tuning stage ($c2$)** is the primary driver of energy consumption. Tuning typically consumes two to three orders of magnitude more energy than preprocessing or inference because it involves running the training loop hundreds or thousands of times to find optimal parameters.

When comparing specific UDA algorithms, the researchers found a wide variance in energy-accuracy trade-offs. Some models, like Raincoat, proved to be highly efficient and robust, while others, like CoTMix, showed high energy variability due to inconsistent convergence during the adaptation process. Under realistic computational budgets, Raincoat and InceptionRain emerged as the most practical choices, balancing performance with energy consumption.

## The Impact of Labeling Costs on Sustainability

One of the most significant findings involves the inclusion of data labeling costs. In many wireless applications, generating ground-truth labels requires specialized equipment or manual intervention, which has its own carbon footprint (associated with the human-in-the-loop or additional hardware operation). 

When labeling costs are ignored, UDA-S typically breaks even with IC after $N = 2$ target domains, and UDA-J breaks even after $N = 1$ to $1.4$ domains. However, once even a modest labeling cost is factored in, the equation changes drastically.

![break-even-analysis](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.23047v1/breakeven_har_final.png)
*{fig:break-even-analysis}: Total energy cost scaling as a function of the number of target domains $N$. The dashed orange line represents Independent Classification with labeling costs, showing that UDA (green and blue lines) becomes the most efficient choice immediately.*

As shown in {fig:break-even-analysis}, the energy cost of IC with labeling (the dashed orange line) rises much more steeply than the UDA strategies. In this realistic scenario, **UDA becomes the "greener" choice from the very first target domain ($N \geq 1$)**. This is because the energy required to adapt a model without labels is significantly lower than the combined energy of labeling data and training a new model from scratch.

## Practical Guidelines for 6G Deployment

Based on these findings, the research provides several key recommendations for deploying AI in future wireless networks:

1.  **Prefer UDA for Multiple Deployments:** If a model is intended for more than two distinct geographical areas or network conditions, UDA is consistently more energy-efficient than retraining.
2.  **Use Joint Adaptation When Possible:** Adapting to multiple target domains simultaneously (UDA-J) scales better than adapting to them one by one (UDA-S), leading to greater energy savings as the network expands.
3.  **Factor in Labeling Scarcity:** In environments where labels are hard to obtain, UDA is not just a performance necessity but an environmental imperative.
4.  **Optimize Tuning:** Since hyperparameter tuning is the most energy-intensive stage, developers should prioritize UDA algorithms that are robust to parameter settings or use efficient search strategies to minimize the number of training runs.

This work clarifies the role of Unsupervised Domain Adaptation in the pursuit of sustainable 6G networks. By quantifying the energy-accuracy trade-offs and identifying the break-even points for different adaptation strategies, the study provides a roadmap for building AI systems that are both high-performing and environmentally responsible. The transition to Green AI in telecommunications depends not just on model accuracy, but on understanding the full lifecycle energy costs of keeping those models relevant in a changing world.
