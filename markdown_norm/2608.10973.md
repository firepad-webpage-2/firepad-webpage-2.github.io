The relationship between the algebraic structure of a variety and its underlying topology is a central theme in complex geometry. One might expect that if a map from a smooth variety to a curve contains singular fibers, this "algebraic" singularity should be "topologically" visible. This intuition is the basis of equisingularity theory, which seeks to understand how the geometry of a variety changes when it is deformed.

In their paper "Invisible Singularities in Complex Algebraic Geometry," Maurício Corrêa, János Kollár, Stefan Schreieder, and Botong Wang present a series of results that fundamentally challenge this intuition. They construct examples of maps from smooth varieties to curves that possess singular fibers but remain "topologically smooth" in several rigorous senses. These "invisible singularities" serve as counterexamples to long-standing conjectures in the field, demonstrating that the presence of critical points does not always manifest in the expected topological way.

## The Smoothness Conjecture and Its Counterexample

For decades, algebraic geometers have investigated whether a proper morphism from a smooth variety to a curve must be a smooth fiber bundle if it appears to be one topologically. Specifically, the "Smoothness Conjecture" proposed by Fernàndez de Bobadilla and Kollár suggested that if $Y$ is a smooth variety and $g: Y \to \mathbb{P}^1$ is a proper morphism such that all higher direct image sheaves $R^i g_* \mathbb{Z}$ are constant (meaning the cohomology of the fibers does not vary), then the map $g$ should have no critical points—it should be a smooth fibration.

The authors of this paper disprove this conjecture by constructing a morphism $g: Y \to \mathbb{P}^1$ from a smooth 5-dimensional variety $Y$ that satisfies several remarkable properties:
1. All sheaves $R^i g_* \mathbb{Z}$ are constant on $\mathbb{P}^1$.
2. The map $g$ is a homotopy fiber bundle with simply connected fibers.
3. Every fiber of $g$ is a PL-manifold (piecewise linear manifold).
4. There exists a smooth $C^\infty$ fiber bundle $\tilde{g}: Y \to \mathbb{P}^1$ that is homotopic to $g$.

Despite these properties, $g$ has singular fibers. This result shows that even when a map looks like a smooth fiber bundle from the perspective of homology, homotopy, and even differential topology (in terms of being homotopic to a smooth bundle), it can still possess algebraic singularities. The singular fibers are "invisible" to these standard topological invariants.

## Constructing Invisible Singularities: The Strategy

The construction of these counterexamples is a multi-step process involving quotients and blow-ups. The goal is to build a local model of a map that is a homology fiber bundle but not a smooth submersion, and then upgrade it to a global homotopy fiber bundle.

### Step 1: Quotient Construction
The authors begin with a local model over a complex disc $\Delta$. They take a manifold $Z$ with a map $\pi: Z \to \Delta$ that has critical points over the origin. By taking an automorphism $\gamma$ of $Z$ and a translation $\tau$ on an abelian variety $A$, they form a quotient variety:

$$\pi_Y: Y := (A \times Z) / \langle (\tau, \gamma) \rangle \to \Delta$$

The key to making the singularities "invisible" at the level of $\mathbb{Z}$-homology is the choice of the Milnor fiber $M_i$ at each critical point. The authors show that if the characteristic polynomial of the action induced by $\gamma$ on the Milnor fiber cohomology $H^{n-1}(M_i, \mathbb{Z})$ satisfies $\chi_i(1) = \pm 1$, then the higher direct image sheaves of the constant sheaf are locally constant. This condition ensures that the homology of the fibers does not "jump" or vanish at the singular point.

### Step 2: Blow-up and Simply Connected Fibers
While the quotient construction provides homology fiber bundles, they are not necessarily homotopy fiber bundles. To achieve this, the authors embed their initial variety $Y$ into a larger smooth projective morphism $P \to \Delta$ that has simply connected fibers. They then consider the blow-up:

$$X := Bl_Y P$$

By blowing up along $Y$, they create a new variety $X$. A critical topological result used here is Whitehead's theorem: if a map is a $\mathbb{Z}$-homology fiber bundle and has simply connected fibers, it must be a homotopy fiber bundle. This "upgrade" is what allows the authors to create maps that are topologically indistinguishable from smooth bundles at the level of homotopy groups.

## Kotschick's Conjecture on Holomorphic One-Forms

The paper extends these findings to address a problem in differential geometry regarding 1-forms. In 2013, Dieter Kotschick asked whether the existence of a real closed 1-form without zeros on a compact Kähler manifold $X$ implies the existence of a holomorphic 1-form without zeros.

The existence of a real closed 1-form without zeros is a strong topological condition; it implies that $X$ fibers smoothly over a circle $S^1$. The authors use their construction to provide a negative answer to this question. They produce a smooth variety $X$ of dimension 5 where every nonzero class in $H^1(X, \mathbb{R})$ can be represented by a real closed 1-form without zeros, yet every holomorphic 1-form on $X$ must have a zero.

This result highlights a gap between the real-differential and complex-analytic structures of a variety. While you can deform the real representative of a cohomology class to be nowhere-vanishing, the constraints of being a holomorphic form (or a harmonic representative of the class) force the presence of zeros.

## Aomoto Complexes and Global Regularity

Another significant contribution involves the study of Aomoto complexes. Given a holomorphic 1-form $\omega$ on a variety $X$, the Aomoto complex is the sequence of cohomology groups $(H^*(X, \mathbb{C}), \wedge \omega)$, where the differential is given by taking the wedge product with $\omega$.

A conjecture by Stefan Schreieder suggested that if a variety $X$ admits a holomorphic 1-form such that these complexes are exact for all finite étale covers $X' \to X$, then $X$ should admit a holomorphic 1-form without zeros. The authors disprove this by constructing a variety $X$ where the Aomoto complex condition is perfectly satisfied, yet every real closed 1-form on $X$ still possesses a zero.

This finding suggests that the algebraic "exactness" of these complexes, which usually indicates highly regular behavior, is not sufficient to guarantee the existence of a smooth, non-vanishing section of the cotangent bundle.

## The Dimensional Boundary: Why Threefolds are Different

One of the most intriguing results in the paper is the discovery of a dimensional limit for these "invisible" phenomena. While the authors construct counterexamples in dimension 5 and above, they prove that the Smoothness Conjecture actually holds for threefolds.

Specifically, they show that if $Y$ is a smooth complex projective threefold and $g: Y \to \mathbb{P}^1$ is a morphism with simply connected fibers that is a $\mathbb{Q}$-homology fiber bundle, then $g$ must be a smooth submersion. In other words, in dimension 3, singularities cannot hide; they are always visible to the topology of the map.

This suggests that the "invisible singularity" phenomenon requires a certain amount of "room" in the variety. In lower dimensions, the constraints imposed by the relationship between the fundamental group, the cohomology, and the canonical class of the variety are too tight to allow a map to be both singular and topologically smooth.

## Conclusion and Significance

The work of Corrêa, Kollár, Schreieder, and Wang provides a new perspective on the boundaries of equisingularity. By demonstrating that singularities can be invisible to homology, homotopy, and even $C^\infty$ differential topology, they show that algebraic smoothness is a much more restrictive condition than topological smoothness.

Their results have several key implications:
*   **Refinement of Conjectures:** The paper settles long-standing questions by Kotschick, Schreieder, and Fernàndez de Bobadilla-Kollár, forcing a re-evaluation of the relationship between 1-forms and fibrations.
*   **Topological Smoothing:** The authors prove a $C^\infty$-smoothing result for maps $g: X \to \Sigma$ where $dim_{\mathbb{C}} X \geq 4$, providing a tool to bridge the gap between holomorphic maps and smooth fiber bundles.
*   **Gap in Dimensions:** The fact that the phenomena appear at dimension 5 but fail at dimension 3 leaves dimension 4 as an open frontier. It remains to be seen if "invisible singularities" can exist in the 4-dimensional case.

Ultimately, this research underscores the complexity of higher-dimensional algebraic varieties, where the interplay of algebraic and topological properties can lead to behaviors that defy the simpler intuitions derived from curves and surfaces.
