## Understanding Multidomain Clinical Assessments

In clinical research and psychology, practitioners often use assessment batteries to evaluate complex conditions like Parkinson’s disease (PD). These assessments typically consist of multiple items or questions grouped into domains, such as motor symptoms (e.g., tremors, gait) and non-motor symptoms (e.g., sleep issues, cognitive decline). The responses to these items are often ordinal, meaning they follow a ranked scale like "none," "mild," "moderate," or "severe."

While researchers often look at the total score across these domains, doing so can hide important nuances. A patient might have severe motor issues but mild cognitive symptoms, or vice versa. To better understand these variations, researchers use Cognitive Diagnostic Models (CDMs). These models help extract "latent attribute profiles"—essentially hidden patterns that show which specific symptoms or skills a person possesses.

However, standard CDMs often treat these symptoms as a single list or ignore the directed influence one set of symptoms might have on another. For example, in PD, it is hypothesized that non-motor symptoms might influence or precede motor symptoms. This research introduces the Endogeneity-Aware Cognitive Diagnostic Model (EACDM) to specifically handle these directed, cross-domain relationships while accounting for the inherent uncertainty in measuring hidden traits.

## The Limitations of Current Cognitive Diagnostic Models

The primary goal of a CDM is to determine an individual's status across a set of binary attributes—whether they "have" or "lack" certain characteristics. This is guided by a Q-matrix, which acts as a map defining which attribute each assessment item is designed to measure. While CDMs have evolved to handle ordinal data and complex Q-matrix structures, they face a specific challenge when dealing with multiple domains.

In many clinical scenarios, researchers believe that one block of attributes (the "exogenous" block) influences another (the "endogenous" block). This directed dependence is known as endogeneity. Standard models usually handle attribute relationships by looking at how they co-occur in the population. While this captures correlation, it does not allow for a parsimonious, directed structural pathway that accounts for measurement error and subject-level covariates like age or biological markers. 

If this endogeneity is ignored, the model might merge distinct attributes from different domains together to simplify the correlation, leading to a distorted view of the patient's condition. The EACDM addresses this by integrating a block-structured measurement component with a logistic structural component to explicitly model these directed paths.

## Core Principles of the EACDM

The EACDM is built on two main pillars: a measurement component that translates observed scores into latent attributes, and a structural component that defines how these attributes relate to each other.

### The Measurement Component for Ordinal Responses
The model assumes there are $J$ items in an assessment, and each item $j$ has a set of ordered response categories $Y_j \in \{0, \dots, M_j-1\}$. To handle these rankings mathematically, the model uses an underlying continuous variable $Y_j^*$ for each item. An observed response $Y_j = m$ is recorded if this hidden continuous variable falls between specific thresholds:
$$\tau_{jc,m} \leq Y_j^* < \tau_{jc,m+1}$$
The value of the continuous variable $Y_j^*$ is determined by a linear predictor:
$$Y_j^* = \mu_{jc} + \epsilon_j$$
where $\epsilon_j \sim N(0, 1)$ represents random noise. The mean structure $\mu_{jc}$ is the heart of the diagnostic component:
$$\mu_{jc} = \beta_{j0} + \sum_{k=1}^K q_{jk} \beta_{jk} \alpha_{ck} = \beta_{j0} + \delta_j^T \alpha_c$$
In this equation:
- $\beta_{j0}$ is the item's baseline level (intercept).
- $q_{jk}$ is an indicator from the Q-matrix (1 if item $j$ requires attribute $k$, 0 otherwise).
- $\beta_{jk}$ is the "loading" or impact of attribute $k$ on the score for item $j$.
- $\alpha_{ck}$ is the presence (1) or absence (0) of the attribute for a given person.

A key innovation here is the block-diagonal structure. The items and attributes are partitioned into two distinct groups. One block of items measures endogenous attributes $\alpha^{(1)}$ (e.g., motor symptoms), and another block measures exogenous attributes $\alpha^{(2)}$ (e.g., non-motor symptoms). This leads to a structured Q-matrix and loading matrix:
$$Q = \begin{pmatrix} Q_1 & 0 \\ 0 & Q_2 \end{pmatrix}, \Delta = \begin{pmatrix} \Delta_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \Delta_2 \end{pmatrix}$$

### The Structural Component
The structural part of the model describes how the endogenous attributes are influenced by the exogenous ones and other external factors $Z$ (like age or brain imaging data). For each endogenous attribute $k = 1, \dots, K_1$, the model uses a logistic regression:
$$\text{logit}(\text{Pr}(\alpha^{(1)}_k = 1 \mid \alpha^{(2)}, Z)) = \lambda_k^T Z + \gamma_k^T \alpha^{(2)}$$
This allows researchers to quantify exactly how much the presence of a non-motor attribute increases the odds of a specific motor impairment appearing.

## Identifiability and Estimation

For any statistical model to be useful, it must be "identifiable"—meaning there is only one set of parameters that could have produced the observed data. The authors established specific mathematical conditions to ensure this. These include requirements for the Q-matrix structure, such as ensuring each attribute is measured by a sufficient number of items and that the exogenous attribute profiles cover all possible combinations in the population.

To estimate the model's parameters, the authors developed a Bayesian approach using a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm. This process involves iteratively updating the estimates for:
1.  The latent attribute profiles ($\alpha$) for each person.
2.  The Q-matrix structure (which items measure which attributes).
3.  The structural coefficients ($\eta_k$), which include the $\gamma$ and $\lambda$ parameters defining the cross-domain relationships.
4.  The item parameters (intercepts and loadings).

The algorithm uses a "spike-and-slab" prior for the loadings, which helps the model automatically decide if an item truly belongs to an attribute (effectively estimating the Q-matrix rather than assuming it is perfectly known).

## Validating the Model with Data

The researchers tested the EACDM through both controlled simulations and a real-world application using the Parkinson’s Progression Markers Initiative (PPMI) dataset.

### Simulation Results
In simulations, the EACDM showed high accuracy in recovering the true Q-matrix and structural coefficients. As the sample size increased, the error rates (measured by Root Mean Squared Error) dropped significantly. Most importantly, the study compared EACDM to a conventional, unstructured CDM. When the data had strong directed dependencies between domains, the conventional CDM failed to identify the correct number of attributes, often merging distinct symptoms into a single broad category. The EACDM, by contrast, correctly separated the domains and identified the directed relationships.

### Parkinson’s Disease Application
Applying the model to data from 1,127 PD patients, the researchers identified three motor attributes and four non-motor attributes.

The motor attributes were clinically intuitive:
1.  **Right-side motor impairment:** Focusing on right-hand and right-leg symptoms.
2.  **Global motor impairment:** Focusing on speech, facial expression, and general posture.
3.  **Left-side motor impairment:** Focusing on left-side extremities.

The non-motor attributes included three "autonomic burden" dimensions (related to sleep, pain, and digestion) and one "cognitive proficiency" dimension. The structural analysis revealed that the autonomic burden attributes were strongly associated with global motor impairment. Furthermore, the model showed that higher biological markers (like putamen uptake in the brain) were associated with lower odds of motor impairment, providing a bridge between physical symptoms and biological data.

## Significance and Impact

The EACDM provides a more precise lens for viewing multidomain assessments. By explicitly modeling the directed pathways between symptom blocks, it offers three primary advantages:

1.  **Clinical Clarity:** It preserves the distinction between domains that are often blurred in standard models, allowing for side-specific or system-specific diagnoses.
2.  **Structural Insights:** It quantifies how symptoms in one area (like sleep or digestion) might predict or influence symptoms in another (like motor control), which is vital for understanding disease progression.
3.  **Statistical Robustness:** By accounting for endogeneity and incorporating covariates directly into the latent attribute estimation, the model reduces the risk of biased conclusions that occur when measurement uncertainty is ignored.

While the model was demonstrated in the context of Parkinson's disease, its framework is broadly applicable. It can be used in education to see how foundational skills influence complex reasoning, or in psychiatry to study the interplay between different symptom clusters. The EACDM represents a move toward diagnostic models that better reflect the complex, interconnected nature of human health and behavior.
