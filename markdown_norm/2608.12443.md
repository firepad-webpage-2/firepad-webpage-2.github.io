## Enhancing Neural Combinatorial Optimization with Structure-Aware Baselines

Combinatorial Optimization Problems (COPs), such as the Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) or job scheduling, are fundamental to logistics, manufacturing, and resource management. While traditional solvers provide exact solutions, they often struggle to scale with the increasing complexity of modern industrial needs. Neural Combinatorial Optimization (NCO) offers an alternative by training deep learning models, typically using reinforcement learning, to learn heuristics that can generate high-quality solutions rapidly.

A standard training paradigm in NCO involves parallel sampling, where a model generates a group of $B$ candidate solutions for a single problem instance. The relative costs of these solutions are then used to calculate a "baseline," which acts as a benchmark to determine whether a specific solution's performance is better or worse than average. This "advantage" signal guides the policy updates. However, standard methods for utilizing these co-sampled solutions often suffer from either ignoring valuable information (by only looking at the single best solution) or being overwhelmed by redundant information (by treating all solutions as equally important regardless of their similarity).

Structure-Aware Similarity-Weighted Preference Optimization (SSPO) is a training framework designed to address these limitations. It introduces a mechanism to weight peer solutions based on their structural dissimilarity, ensuring that the training signal is both diverse and representative of the solution landscape.

## The Challenges of Existing NCO Training

Modern NCO training relies heavily on the quality of the gradient signal derived from co-sampled solutions. Research indicates that two primary issues currently hinder training efficiency:

### Gradient Signal Polarization
Many preference-optimization methods use a "best-anchor" approach. In a batch of $B$ solutions, they identify the single best-performing solution and treat all other $B-1$ solutions as negatives. While this simplifies the learning task, it creates a polarized signal. In a large batch (e.g., $B=256$), 255 solutions are treated identically, even if some are nearly as good as the best or offer unique structural insights. This polarization discards fine-grained information about why some "non-best" solutions performed better than others.

### Baseline Redundancy and Variance
Other methods, like REINFORCE with Leave-One-Out (RLOO), use the average cost of all other $B-1$ solutions as a baseline for each candidate. While this avoids polarization, it introduces "baseline redundancy." In NCO, co-sampled solutions often share significant topological similarities—they might traverse many of the same edges or select the same nodes. If the baseline is calculated by weighting all solutions uniformly, these nearly identical solutions contribute redundant information. This redundancy prevents the baseline from effectively reducing gradient variance, leading to unstable or slow training.

SSPO is designed to bridge this gap by using a structure-aware approach that keeps all solutions in the loop while weighting them according to how much unique information they provide.

## Structure-Aware Solution Embeddings

To calculate how similar two solutions are without adding new learnable parameters, SSPO reuses the internal representations already generated by the model's graph encoder. If an encoder has already learned to represent the relationship between different nodes in a problem graph (like cities in a TSP), those representations can be combined to describe a full solution.

The method for constructing these "solution embeddings" $z_k$ depends on the nature of the problem:

*   **Edge-Based Problems (e.g., TSP):** For problems where the sequence of edges matters, a solution embedding is formed by looking at the pairs of nodes $(s^{(k)}_t, s^{(k)}_{t+1})$ in the route. SSPO uses the Hadamard product (element-wise multiplication) of the encoder features $h_v$ of these nodes to represent an edge, then averages these over the whole path:
    $$z_k = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} (h_{s^{(k)}_t} \odot h_{s^{(k)}_{t+1}})$$
*   **Selection-Based Problems (e.g., Facility Location):** For problems where the goal is to select a subset of nodes, the embedding is simply the average of the encoder features for all selected nodes:
    $$z_k = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} h_{s^{(k)}_t}$$

By using the Hadamard product for routes, SSPO ensures that the embedding is sensitive to the specific connections made, rather than just the set of nodes visited.

## Similarity-Weighted Leave-One-Out Baseline

Once these embeddings are created, SSPO calculates a unique baseline for each solution $s_i$. The process involves three main steps:

**1. Embedding Centering**
To highlight the relative differences between solutions, the embeddings are centered by subtracting the mean embedding of the entire batch:
$$\tilde{z}_k = z_k - \frac{1}{B} \sum_{j=1}^B z_j$$
This centering step is crucial because, in high-dimensional spaces, embeddings often share a common global direction. Centering "zooms in" on the structural variations that distinguish one solution from its peers.

**2. Calculating Dissimilarity Weights**
SSPO computes the cosine similarity $S_{ij}$ between the centered embeddings of solution $i$ and solution $j$:
$$S_{ij} = \frac{\tilde{z}_i^\top \tilde{z}_j}{\|\tilde{z}_i\| \|\tilde{z}_j\|}$$
The weight $w_{ij}$ assigned to solution $j$ when calculating the baseline for solution $i$ is proportional to its structural *dissimilarity* (i.e., $1 - S_{ij}$):
$$w_{ij} = \frac{1 - S_{ij}}{\sum_{k \neq i} (1 - S_{ik})}$$
This means that if solution $j$ is very different from solution $i$, its cost will have a greater influence on solution $i$'s baseline. If they are nearly identical, its influence is minimized.

**3. Constructing the Baseline and Advantage**
The final baseline $b^{\text{SSPO}}_i$ is the weighted average of the costs of all other solutions:
$$b^{\text{SSPO}}_i = \sum_{j \neq i} w_{ij} c_j$$
The model is then trained using the advantage $A_i = b^{\text{SSPO}}_i - c_i$, which tells the model how much better (or worse) a solution is compared to a structurally diverse set of its peers.

## Theoretical Variance Reduction

The core intuition behind SSPO is that weighting by dissimilarity reduces the variance of the policy gradient. Theoretically, the variance of a baseline is determined by the variances of individual solution costs and the covariances between them. When solutions are structurally similar, their costs are highly correlated (high covariance). 

By down-weighting highly correlated peer solutions (those with high similarity), SSPO reduces the impact of these covariance terms. While deviating from uniform weighting (like RLOO) adds a small amount of variance, the reduction in covariance often far outweighs this penalty, especially in large-scale COPs where many solutions might be topologically similar.

## Performance on Benchmark Problems

SSPO was evaluated against several established methods, including POMO (Policy Optimization with Multiple Optima), RLOO (REINFORCE Leave-One-Out), and BOPO (Best-anchor Preference Optimization).

*   **TSP-100:** On the 100-node Traveling Salesman Problem, SSPO achieved an optimality gap of 0.40%, outperforming both BOPO (0.48%) and RLOO (1.32%). This shows that even in problems where the best-anchor approach is strong, adding structure-awareness further improves results.
*   **Entity Facility Location (EFL):** In this assignment-dominated problem, SSPO showed significant gains. It reached a 0% relative gap compared to the best discovered solutions, while BOPO and POMO trailed by over 7%. This suggests that in complex assignment spaces, anchoring only on the best solution can cause the model to get stuck in local optima, whereas SSPO's diverse baseline encourages better exploration.
*   **Job-Shop Scheduling (JSP):** On difficult scheduling benchmarks (the TA and DMU families), SSPO consistently outperformed the best-anchor method.

### Real-World Deployment
The effectiveness of SSPO is not limited to synthetic benchmarks. The model was successfully applied to an Entity Facility Location problem within JD.com's production systems. These real-world instances involve irregular demand patterns and coverage constraints. SSPO was able to generate feasible and efficient facility layouts that outperformed standard NCO baselines.

## Implementation Efficiency

One of the practical advantages of SSPO is its efficiency. Because it reuses existing encoder features, it does not add any new learnable parameters to the model. The computational cost of calculating the similarity matrix and the weighted baseline is relatively low. 

For an instance of size $N$ with $B$ sampled solutions and embedding dimension $D$, the additional complexity is $O(BND)$ for embedding construction and $O(B^2D)$ for the similarity matrix. Compared to the standard $O(N^2DL)$ complexity of the encoder-decoder attention layers (where $L$ is the number of layers), this overhead is negligible. This makes SSPO a "plug-and-play" enhancement that can be integrated into most existing NCO architectures with minimal effort.

## Conclusion

SSPO represents a shift in how NCO models utilize sampled data during training. By moving away from both the restrictive "best-anchor" view and the "structure-blind" uniform baseline, it provides a more nuanced and informative training signal. Through the use of structure-aware solution embeddings and dissimilarity weighting, SSPO reduces gradient variance and avoids the pitfalls of redundant information. Its consistent performance across diverse problems like TSP, EFL, and JSP, combined with its zero-parameter overhead and real-world applicability, makes it a robust tool for scaling neural combinatorial optimization to complex industrial challenges.
