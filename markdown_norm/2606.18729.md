## Introduction to Time Series Data Valuation

In the modern machine learning pipeline, the quality of data is often as important as the architecture of the model itself. Data valuation is the process of quantifying the utility or quality of individual data samples. By identifying which data points contribute most to a model's performance—and which might be harmful outliers or noise—researchers can perform principled data curation, such as pruning low-quality samples or prioritizing informative ones for manual labeling. 

While data valuation is well-established for independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) data like images or static tabular records, applying these concepts to time series data presents unique difficulties. Sequential data is characterized by temporal dependencies, where the meaning of a data point depends on its context in time. Furthermore, time series often exhibit non-stationarity, meaning their statistical properties change over time, and multi-scale patterns, where important information exists in both long-term trends and short-term bursts.

Existing data valuation methods typically fall into two categories. **Model-dependent** approaches, such as Influence Functions or Shapley values, measure how a specific model's performance changes when a data point is removed. These are computationally expensive and tied to a single model architecture. **Learning-agnostic** approaches aim to value data based on its intrinsic distribution relative to a "clean" reference set, usually via Optimal Transport (OT). However, previous learning-agnostic methods assume data points are independent, failing to capture the "flow" of time series.

TimeLAVA (Time Series Learning-Agnostic ValuAtion) addresses these gaps. It provides a framework to value temporal segments within a time series by combining wavelet-based feature extraction with a robust form of Optimal Transport. This allows for data valuation that is independent of any specific learning algorithm while remaining sensitive to the complex dynamics of temporal data.

## Capturing Multi-scale Dynamics with Wavelets

A core requirement for time series valuation is the ability to represent temporal segments in a way that captures both "what" happened and "when" it happened. Traditional spectral methods, like the Fourier Transform, are excellent at identifying frequencies (e.g., a periodic oscillation) but lose all information about the timing of those frequencies. Conversely, looking only at raw time-domain values fails to capture the underlying patterns or scales.

![dwt-vs-fourier-transform](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.18729v3/DWT_vs_fourier.png)
*{fig:dwt-vs-fourier-transform}: A comparison of Fourier and Wavelet transforms. While the Fourier transform shows which frequencies are present, it lacks temporal localization. The Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) provides a joint time-frequency representation, identifying both the scale of a signal component and its specific location in time.*

TimeLAVA utilizes the **Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT)** to extract multi-scale features. DWT decomposes a signal into different frequency bands while preserving temporal localization. This is particularly useful for identifying transient events—like a sudden spike in a heart rate monitor—alongside slower, sustained trends. 

In the TimeLAVA framework, the time series is first divided into segments using a sliding window of length $L$ and stride $S$. For each segment $x_i$ and $x_j$, a wavelet distance $d_{\text{wav}}$ is calculated:

$$d_{\text{wav}}(x_i, x_j) = \|\Psi(x_i) - \Psi(x_j)\|_1$$

where $\Psi$ represents the wavelet coefficients. This distance serves as a metric that accounts for similarity across multiple temporal scales.

## Selective Wavelet-based Wasserstein Discrepancy

Once segments are represented in the wavelet domain, TimeLAVA uses Optimal Transport (OT) to compare the distribution of "evaluated" segments (the data we want to value) against a "reference" set (data known to be high-quality or representative of the target task).

Standard OT seeks the most efficient way to "move" the mass of one distribution to match another. However, standard OT requires every bit of mass to be matched. In the context of data valuation, this is problematic: if the evaluated data contains an extreme outlier or a sudden regime shift that doesn't exist in the reference set, standard OT will force a match, leading to an infinitely high cost and unstable values.

To solve this, TimeLAVA adopts **Unbalanced Optimal Transport (UOT)**. UOT relaxes the requirement for strict mass conservation by adding a penalty (using KL-divergence) for leaving mass unmatched. This creates a "selective matching" mechanism. If a segment is too different from anything in the reference set, the system chooses to pay a fixed penalty rather than forcing a high-cost match. This provides two major benefits:
1.  **Robustness:** Outliers or new regimes have a bounded impact on the overall discrepancy, preventing a single bad data point from skewing the entire valuation.
2.  **Discrimination:** It naturally identifies anomalies. Segments that are difficult to match are assigned high "dual potentials," which translate to low or negative data values.

The discrepancy between the two distributions, termed the Selective Wavelet-based Wasserstein ($W_{\text{SW}}$) discrepancy, is calculated using a cost matrix:

$$D_{ij} = d_{\text{wav}}(x_i, x_j') + c \cdot W_{d_{\text{wav}}}(\mu_{\text{eval}}(\cdot|y_i), \mu_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|y_j'))$$

Here, $D_{ij}$ combines the feature similarity (wavelet distance) with a term for label consistency, weighted by $c$. By solving the UOT problem, the framework finds the optimal dual potentials $f^*$ and $g^*$.

## Efficient Data Valuation

A significant advantage of TimeLAVA is its computational efficiency. Rather than retraining a model for every segment, it derives values directly from the UOT dual solution. A segment's value $v(x_i)$ is defined as its marginal contribution to reducing the overall discrepancy $W_{\text{SW}}$.

The value for a segment $x_i$ is computed as:

$$v(x_i) = - \left( \psi_\kappa(f^*_i) - \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{j \neq i} \psi_\kappa(f^*_j) \right)$$

where $\psi_\kappa(u) = \kappa(1 - e^{-u/\kappa})$ is a scaling function governed by the UOT regularization parameter $\kappa$. Intuitively, if a segment aligns well with the reference distribution (low $f^*$ potential), it helps minimize the discrepancy and receives a high positive value. Conversely, outliers or noisy segments receive negative values. 

For applications requiring point-wise resolution, these segment values are averaged across all overlapping windows to produce a final value $v(t)$ for every time point. The overall complexity of the method is $O(nmL)$, where $n$ and $m$ are the number of segments in the evaluated and reference sets, and $L$ is the segment length. This is significantly more efficient than model-based methods like TimeInf, which often require complex temporal blocking and repeated model training.

## Theoretical Foundations and Robustness

The paper provides theoretical guarantees that justify why $W_{\text{SW}}$ is an appropriate metric for data valuation. 

First, the authors prove that $W_{\text{SW}}$ has **bounded sensitivity to outliers**. In standard OT, moving an outlier to infinity results in an infinite discrepancy. In TimeLAVA, because of the UOT formulation, the impact of an outlier mode is capped. This allows the method to remain stable even when the time series undergoes a non-stationary regime shift or contains heavy-tailed noise.

Second, the authors establish a connection to **learning-agnostic generalization**. They show that the $W_{\text{SW}}$ discrepancy between two distributions provides an upper bound on the performance gap of a predictive model trained on one and tested on the other. Specifically, for a model $f$ and a loss function with specific Lipschitz properties, reducing $W_{\text{SW}}(\mu_{\text{eval}}, \mu_{\text{ref}})$ directly implies that the model's performance on the reference set will be closer to its performance on the training set. This theoretically justifies using TimeLAVA to select training data that will lead to better generalization.

## Empirical Performance and Applications

TimeLAVA was tested across three major tasks using diverse real-world datasets from finance, health, and industrial monitoring.

### 1. Anomaly Detection
In this task, the goal is to identify rare, divergent events. TimeLAVA consistently outperformed both traditional anomaly detectors (like Isolation Forest) and specialized deep learning models (like Anomaly Transformer). Its ability to capture multi-scale patterns through wavelets allowed it to detect various types of anomalies, from point-wise spikes to contextual changes in oscillation frequency, more accurately than methods that ignore temporal scale.

### 2. Data Pruning and Selection
This task evaluates how well the valuation scores identify "harmful" data. The researchers introduced synthetic corruptions (noise, drifts, spikes) into training data. TimeLAVA achieved significantly higher AUC scores in detecting these corrupted segments compared to baselines. Furthermore, in pruning experiments, removing the segments TimeLAVA identified as "low-value" allowed forecasting models to maintain high accuracy even with a fraction of the original data, whereas random pruning or model-dependent pruning led to rapid performance degradation.

### 3. Noisy Label Detection
Time series data often suffers from labeling errors, such as a sensor being misclassified for a period due to human error. TimeLAVA proved effective at identifying these mislabeled segments, particularly when the noise followed temporal patterns (like periodic or decaying noise). By leveraging the temporal context, it could distinguish between a legitimate regime change and a labeling error that contradicts the underlying signal dynamics.

## Conclusion and Future Directions

TimeLAVA represents a shift toward data-centric AI for sequential data. By moving away from model-dependent valuations, it provides a tool that is both computationally practical and theoretically grounded. Its use of Unbalanced Optimal Transport ensures robustness against the non-stationarity that plagues real-world time series, while the wavelet representation ensures that no temporal scale is overlooked.

Future work may look into more sophisticated ways to handle multivariate dependencies—how different channels in a time series interact—and scaling the method to even larger datasets through mini-batching techniques. As it stands, TimeLAVA provides a principled way to ensure that the time series models of tomorrow are built on the highest-quality data of today.
