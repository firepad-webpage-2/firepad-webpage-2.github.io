## Modeling Gradual Transitions in Fluctuating Environments

Natural microbial populations rarely exist in static environments. Instead, they navigate a landscape of shifting conditions, such as fluctuating resource availability, temperature changes, and varying pH levels. Historically, theoretical ecology has modeled these fluctuations using binary switching frameworks—often called "feast-famine" cycles—where the environment toggles between two extreme states: one of abundance and one of scarcity. While mathematically convenient, these binary models are a coarse simplification of reality. Empirical evidence suggests that many biological systems undergo more gradual transitions, moving through a series of intermediate states rather than jumping between two extremes.

This research moves beyond the binary paradigm by developing a multi-state stochastic switching model. By introducing a finite number of intermediate environmental states, the work provides a more granular description of how populations respond to environmental variability. This approach allows for the investigation of how the frequency and amplitude of these fluctuations, along with the specific trajectory of environmental change, influence the competition between different biological strains.

## The Mechanics of Population Fluctuations

The study centers on a well-mixed population consisting of two competing strains: a faster-growing "fit" strain (F) and a slightly slower-growing "less fit" strain (S). The difference in their growth rates is characterized by a selection coefficient $s$, where the fitness of the slow strain is $f_S = 1 - s$ and the fitness of the fast strain is $f_F = 1$. These strains compete for a shared resource pool, and the total population size is constrained by a time-varying carrying capacity $K(t)$.

To capture the inherent randomness of individual birth and death events (demographic fluctuations), the population is modeled as a continuous-time multivariate birth-death process. In this framework, the probability of an individual being born or dying depends on the current state of the population. The birth rate for a strain $\alpha \in \{S, F\}$ is influenced by its relative fitness and its current abundance:

$$T^+_\alpha = \frac{f_\alpha}{\bar{f}} N_\alpha$$

where $N = N_S + N_F$ is the total population size and $\bar{f} = 1 - sx$ is the average population fitness, with $x = N_S/N$ representing the fraction of the slow-growing strain. Conversely, the death rate is dictated by the pressure on resources, scaled by the environment's current carrying capacity:

$$T^-_\alpha = \frac{N}{K} N_\alpha$$

This mathematical structure ensures that as the population size $N$ approaches the carrying capacity $K$, the death rate increases, eventually balancing the birth rate. Because the environment $K(t)$ is constantly switching, the population is in a perpetual state of adjustment, trying to reach a moving target.

## Multi-state Environmental Dynamics

The core contribution of this work is the representation of the environment as a Markov process with $2n+1$ discrete states, indexed by $i \in \{-n, \dots, 0, \dots, n\}$. Each state $i$ is associated with a specific carrying capacity $K_i$. These capacities are ordered linearly from a "harsh" famine state to a "mild" feast state:

$K_{-n} \equiv K^- < \dots < K_0 < \dots < K_n \equiv K^+$

The transitions between these states occur at specific rates. For instance, $\nu^-_{2n+1}$ and $\omega^-_{2n+1}$ represent the rates of moving toward harsher or milder conditions, respectively. To handle the complexity of $2n+1$ states, the model uses a parameter $\epsilon$ to relate different transition rates and a bias parameter $\delta$ to describe whether the environment spends more time in favorable or unfavorable conditions:

$$\delta = \frac{\nu^-_{2n+1} - \nu^+_{2n+1}}{2\nu_{2n+1}}$$

Here, $\nu_{2n+1}$ represents the mean switching rate. A positive $\delta$ implies a bias toward mild environmental states (higher carrying capacity), while a negative $\delta$ favors harsh states. This setup allows the researcher to compare the multi-state model directly with simpler binary models by ensuring that the average time taken to traverse from the minimum $K^-$ to the maximum $K^+$ is consistent across models.

## Population Size Distributions across Switching Regimes

The behavior of the population size $N$ depends heavily on the relationship between the environmental switching rate $\nu_{2n+1}$ and the internal timescales of the population (like birth and death rates). The study identifies three distinct regimes:

1.  **Slow Switching ($\nu_{2n+1} \ll 1$):** In this regime, the environment remains in a single state long enough for the population to reach a local equilibrium. Consequently, the population size distribution (PSD) is multimodal, showing $2n+1$ distinct peaks. Each peak corresponds to one of the carrying capacities $K_i$.
2.  **Intermediate Switching ($\nu_{2n+1} \sim 1$):** As switching becomes faster, the peaks in the PSD begin to merge. The population can no longer "keep up" with the environment, leading to a broader, more smeared distribution. This regime is characterized by "population bottlenecks"—critical periods where the carrying capacity drops and the population size must rapidly decline to avoid total collapse.
3.  **Fast Switching ($\nu_{2n+1} \gg 1$):** When the environment fluctuates extremely rapidly, the population size cannot respond to individual switches. Instead, it "senses" an average environment. The PSD becomes unimodal (one peak), centered around an effective carrying capacity $K_{2n+1}$, which is the weighted harmonic mean of the individual capacities.

Interestingly, the study finds that multi-state models produce much broader PSDs than binary models. This increased variance in population size suggests that intermediate states act as a buffer, allowing for a more continuous range of population sizes over time.

## Fixation and the Survival of the Slower Strain

A central question in evolutionary biology is whether a new or disadvantaged strain will reach "fixation"—meaning it takes over the entire population—or go extinct. In a static environment, the slow-growing strain $S$ is almost always expected to lose. However, environmental fluctuations can significantly alter this outcome.

The study calculates the fixation probability $\phi_{2n+1}$ for the $S$ strain across different switching regimes. By using a Piecewise Deterministic Markov Process (N-PDMP) approximation, the researcher can estimate fixation even in the complex intermediate regime where standard approximations fail. The results reveal that the number of intermediate states $n$ and the distribution of carrying capacities $K_i$ have profound effects:

*   **Quenched Regime (Slow):** When switching is slow ($\nu_{2n+1} \ll s$), the fixation probability is the average of the fixation probabilities in each individual state, weighted by the time spent in that state:
    $$\phi^0_{2n+1} = \sum_{i=-n}^n \phi_M(K_i) \pi_i$$
*   **Annealed Regime (Fast):** When switching is very fast ($\nu_{2n+1} \gg s$), the fixation probability depends on the effective average environment:
    $\phi^\infty_{2n+1} = \phi_M(K_{2n+1})$

The research demonstrates that multi-state environments can either enhance or hinder the fixation of the slow strain compared to binary environments. For example, if the intermediate states are relatively harsh (low $K_0$), the slow strain $S$ often has a higher chance of fixation than it would in a binary environment. This is because smaller population sizes (typical of harsh states) increase the impact of demographic randomness, making it easier for a disadvantaged strain to drift to fixation.

## Time to Fixation and Ecological Implications

Beyond the probability of fixation, the study examines the mean fixation time $\tau_{2n+1}$—how long it takes on average for one strain to disappear. The findings show that $\tau_{2n+1}$ scales with the inverse of the selection strength ($1/s$). Generally, faster environmental switching leads to shorter fixation times, as the population spends less time in stable, high-capacity states where demographic drift is slow.

The introduction of multiple states also increases the average time to fixation as the number of states $n$ increases, particularly when the environment is biased toward favorable conditions ($\delta > 0$). This suggests that environmental complexity can act to preserve genetic diversity for longer periods by slowing down the process of competitive exclusion.

## Conclusion

This work provides a detailed look at how the granularity of environmental change dictates population outcomes. By moving from a 2-state "feast-famine" model to a $(2n+1)$-state model, the research highlights that the specific path of environmental transition is just as important as the extreme states themselves. 

The use of the N-PDMP approximation offers a robust tool for future researchers to study populations where environmental and demographic fluctuations are both significant. Ultimately, the study suggests that the "coarse-grained" reality of many natural habitats—where resources vary gradually—leads to broader population distributions and a more complex set of evolutionary possibilities than previously predicted by simpler models. This has significant implications for our understanding of microbial adaptation, the maintenance of biodiversity, and the management of biological systems in shifting climates.
