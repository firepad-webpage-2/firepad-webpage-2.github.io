## Understanding Phase Separation through Physics-Informed Neural Networks

Phase separation is a ubiquitous phenomenon in nature, governing the formation of microstructures in metal alloys, the behavior of polymer mixtures, and the internal organization of biological cells. To model these processes, researchers typically employ phase-field models, which use continuous order parameters to describe the state of a system. However, accurately simulating these systems requires precise knowledge of the underlying physics, specifically the homogeneous bulk free energy and the interfacial properties.

In many practical scenarios, such as the study of biomolecular condensates or the development of new functional materials, these constitutive properties are not known in advance. Traditional inverse methods often struggle with non-identifiability—the inability to uniquely determine multiple parameters simultaneously from the same set of data. The research presented here introduces the Extended Pseudo-Spectral Physics-Informed Neural Network (ESPINN) framework, designed to simultaneously learn the functional form of the bulk chemical potential and unknown physical coefficients from sparse, transient observations.

## The Mechanics of Phase-Field Models

Phase-field models represent a system's state using an order parameter $\phi(x,t)$. The evolution of this parameter is driven by the minimization of a free energy functional, $E$. A standard form for this energy in a domain $\Omega$ is:

$$
E = \int_\Omega \left( \frac{\epsilon^2}{2} |\nabla\phi|^2 + F(\phi) \right) dx
$$

In this equation, $\epsilon$ represents the interfacial thickness parameter, which determines the width of the transition zone between different phases. The term $F(\phi)$ is the homogeneous free energy density, which describes the energy of the bulk material. The system evolves based on the chemical potential, defined as the functional derivative of the energy:

$$
\frac{\delta E}{\delta\phi} = f(\phi) - \epsilon^2\Delta\phi
$$

where $f(\phi) = F'(\phi)$ is the bulk chemical potential. For a conserved system, such as a mixture where the total amount of material remains constant, the dynamics follow the Cahn-Hilliard equation:

$$
\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} = M\Delta \left[ f(\phi) - \epsilon^2\Delta\phi \right]
$$

Here, $M$ is the mobility coefficient. The primary challenge in the inverse problem is that while we might observe $\phi$ at various points in time, we often do not know the exact mathematical form of $f(\phi)$ or the value of $\epsilon$.

## The ESPINN Framework

The ESPINN framework extends previous work on Pseudo-Spectral Physics-Informed Neural Networks (SPINNs). While SPINNs were previously able to identify the function $f(\phi)$ if $\epsilon$ was already known, ESPINN introduces the capability to learn both simultaneously.

### Neural Network Architecture
ESPINN uses two distinct components to represent the unknown physics:
1.  **Functional Approximator ($N_f$):** A Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) is used to approximate the unknown bulk chemical potential $f(\phi)$. This network takes the local concentration $\phi$ as input and outputs the corresponding chemical potential value.
2.  **Gradient Coefficient Estimator ($N_\gamma$):** The unknown gradient coefficient $\gamma = \epsilon^2$ is parameterized as a trainable scalar. To ensure the parameter remains physically meaningful (i.e., positive and within a reasonable range), a sigmoid-based reparameterization is used:

$$
\gamma = \gamma_{lb} + (\gamma_{ub} - \gamma_{lb})\sigma(\theta)
$$

This approach allows the optimizer to adjust $\theta$ freely while $\gamma$ stays between the lower bound $\gamma_{lb}$ and upper bound $\gamma_{ub}$.

### Loss Function Construction
Unlike standard PINNs that evaluate the PDE residual at random collocation points, ESPINN leverages the efficiency of pseudo-spectral methods. The domain is discretized on a grid, and the spatial derivatives are computed using the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT).

The training data consists of $N$ pairs of snapshots $(\Phi^{(1)}_i, \Phi^{(2)}_i)$, where $\Phi^{(2)}_i$ is the state of the system a short time $\Delta t$ after $\Phi^{(1)}_i$. The loss function is defined by the difference between the observed next state and a prediction $N_R$ generated by a semi-implicit time-stepping scheme:

$$
L(\Theta) = \frac{C_0}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \| \Phi^{(2)}_i - N_R(\Phi^{(1)}_i, (\Delta t)_i; \Theta) \|_2^2
$$

This loss function forces the neural network to find a function $f(\phi)$ and a parameter $\epsilon$ that accurately "predict" how the system evolves from one snapshot to the next according to the physics of the Cahn-Hilliard equation.

## Performance and Data Efficiency

One of the most significant findings of this work is the high data efficiency of the ESPINN framework. In noiseless conditions, the model can reconstruct the bulk chemical potential and the interfacial thickness parameter with high accuracy using as little as a single pair of snapshots ($N=1$).

## Robustness to Measurement Noise

In real-world applications, observational data is rarely perfect. The research team tested the ESPINN framework against varying levels of multiplicative noise. They found that while noise naturally increases the reconstruction error, the method degrades gracefully.

By increasing the number of snapshot pairs $N$, the impact of noise can be effectively mitigated. With enough data points, the network can "average out" the stochastic noise to recover the underlying deterministic physics. Even at a noise level of $\delta_L = 10^{-3}$, the recovered functional form of the chemical potential remains qualitatively correct, and the interfacial thickness $\epsilon$ is identified with high precision.

## Architectural Considerations

The study performed a detailed sensitivity analysis on the neural network's architecture and the optimization process:

*   **Activation Functions:** The choice of activation function is critical. Smooth, continuously differentiable functions like `tanh`, `sigmoid`, `SiLU`, and `GELU` were found to be highly effective. In contrast, the widely used `ReLU` activation performed poorly, resulting in errors several orders of magnitude higher. This is likely because the underlying physical solutions and the PDE itself require smoothness that `ReLU` cannot provide.
*   **Network Capacity:** Interestingly, the depth and width of the MLP (number of layers and neurons) had a minimal impact on the results within the tested ranges. This suggests that the bottleneck in inverse modeling for these systems is the quality and quantity of data, rather than the representational power of the network.
*   **Learning Rates:** The use of an exponentially decaying learning rate was found to be the most effective strategy for the Adam optimizer, providing a balance between rapid initial convergence and long-term stability.

## Significance for Scientific Research

The ability to simultaneously learn functional forms and scalar coefficients from transient data represents a shift in how researchers can approach phase-field modeling. In materials science, this could allow for the rapid characterization of new alloys by observing their microstructure evolution. In biology, it provides a tool to quantify the energy landscapes of liquid-liquid phase separation in cells, where traditional experimental measurements of free energy are extremely difficult.

By combining the physical rigor of pseudo-spectral PDE solvers with the flexible functional approximation of neural networks, ESPINN offers a physically consistent way to bridge the gap between observation and theory. It addresses the non-identifiability problem by enforcing the governing dynamics as a hard constraint during the learning process, ensuring that the discovered "physics" is not just a fit to the data, but a viable description of the system's temporal evolution.
