## The Interface of Tropical Oscillations and Meridional Overturning

The Hadley circulation is a defining feature of Earth's atmospheric architecture. This large-scale system of tropical overturning—characterized by rising air near the equator, poleward flow in the upper troposphere, sinking in the subtropics, and a surface return flow—governs the distribution of global rainfall and the location of the world's major deserts. While the Hadley circulation is well-studied on seasonal and interannual timescales (such as its response to El Niño), its behavior on subseasonal scales has remained less understood. Specifically, researchers have long sought to understand how the Madden-Julian Oscillation (MJO), the dominant mode of tropical intraseasonal variability, interacts with this global-scale meridional circulation.

The MJO is a massive complex of clouds and rainfall that propagates eastward along the equator, typically cycling every 30 to 90 days. While previous research has linked the MJO to localized wind anomalies in the Indo-Pacific region, it was unclear whether these regional disruptions aggregated into a coherent signal that affects the *zonal-mean* (global average at each latitude) Hadley circulation. Recent research conducted at the Weizmann Institute of Science provides evidence that the MJO systematically modulates the global Hadley circulation, inducing anomalies that represent approximately 20% of its total climatological strength. This interaction creates a specific pathway through which tropical subseasonal weather patterns influence the global hydrological cycle.

## Defining the Coupled System

To investigate this connection, researchers analyzed the ERA5 reanalysis dataset, which provides a high-resolution historical record of the atmosphere. The strength and structure of the Hadley circulation are traditionally quantified using the meridional mass streamfunction, denoted as $\Psi$. The streamfunction at a given latitude $\phi$ and pressure level $p$ is calculated as:

$$\Psi(\phi, p) = \frac{2\pi a \cos \phi}{g} \int_{0}^{p} v(\phi, p) dp$$

In this expression, $a$ represents the Earth's radius, $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity, and $v$ is the zonal-mean meridional wind.

To track the MJO, the study utilized the Velocity Potential MJO (VPM) index. Unlike other indices that rely on cloud cover data, the VPM index focuses on the large-scale divergent circulation in the upper atmosphere, making it particularly useful for studying overturning motions. The MJO amplitude $A_{VPM}$ is derived from the index's two principal components:

$$A_{VPM} = \sqrt{VPM1^2 + VPM2^2}$$

By comparing the state of the atmosphere during "active" MJO periods ($A > A_{90\%}$) and "inactive" periods ($A < A_{10\%}$), the researchers identified a distinct, hemispherically asymmetric anomaly in the Hadley circulation. During active MJO phases in boreal autumn (SON) and winter (DJF), the circulation shifts in a way that resembles the typical boreal winter pattern: enhanced ascent in the Southern Hemisphere subtropics and increased subsidence in the Northern Hemisphere subtropics.

## Dynamical Drivers: The Dominance of Latent Heating

A critical question in atmospheric science is whether these circulation changes are driven by thermodynamic forces (like the heat released from condensing water vapor) or mechanical forces (like the momentum transferred by large-scale atmospheric eddies). To answer this, the researchers employed the Kuo-Eliassen (KE) equation, a diagnostic tool that decomposes the anomalous streamfunction $\Delta\psi$ into individual forcing components:

$$L(\Delta\psi) = \Delta S_{Q_{LH}} + \Delta S_{Q_R} - \Delta S_{F} - \Delta S_{ehf} - \Delta S_{emf} + \text{RES}$$

In this framework, $L$ is a mathematical operator, $\Delta S_{Q_{LH}}$ represents latent heating, $\Delta S_{Q_R}$ is radiative heating, $\Delta S_{F}$ is friction, $\Delta S_{ehf}$ is eddy heat flux, and $\Delta S_{emf}$ is eddy momentum flux. The term $\text{RES}$ accounts for any remaining residuals.

The results of this decomposition, illustrated in {fig:kuo-eliassen-decomposition}, demonstrate that the MJO-induced Hadley cell anomaly is primarily maintained by $\Delta S_{Q_{LH}}$—the forcing from latent heating. As MJO-related convection moves through the tropics, the massive release of energy from rainfall drives the vertical motion that powers the meridional overturning. While eddy momentum fluxes and mechanical damping play secondary roles, they are significantly smaller than the contribution from latent heat. This finding indicates that moisture processes are the fundamental constraint on how the MJO influences the global circulation.

## Moisture Gradients and Circulation Scaling

Building on the discovery that latent heating is the primary driver, the research examined how the circulation strength scales with the distribution of moisture. Latent heating is intrinsically linked to the amount of water vapor in the air. The study calculated the column-integrated moisture $W$ using the specific humidity $q$:

$$W = \frac{1}{g} \int q dp$$

The researchers then looked at the hemispheric moisture contrast, defined as $\Delta W = W_{SH} - W_{NH}$, which measures the difference in total moisture between the Southern and Northern Hemispheres.

The analysis revealed a near-linear relationship between the maximum strength of the anomalous Hadley circulation $\psi_{max}$ and the moisture gradient $\Delta W$. This scaling, shown in {fig:moisture-scaling}, suggests that as the MJO creates larger disparities in moisture across the equator, the global overturning circulation strengthens proportionally. This empirical observation aligns with theoretical "moist dynamical" models of the atmosphere, which predict that meridional circulations are highly sensitive to cross-equatorial moisture contrasts.

## Temporal Dynamics and Lead-Lag Relationships

To establish whether the MJO is truly the cause of these circulation changes, rather than a byproduct, the researchers performed a lead-lag correlation analysis. By shifting the time series of the MJO indices relative to the Hadley circulation index, they could determine which phenomenon occurs first.

The data shows that the Hadley circulation responds to the MJO with a distinct lag. Specifically, the circulation follows the first MJO principal component (VPM1) by about 4 days and the second component (VPM2) by approximately 15 days. These different lags correspond to the time it takes for the MJO's convective center to propagate across different regions of the tropical ocean. The fact that the strongest correlations occur when the MJO leads the circulation suggests a causal direction: the convective variability of the MJO acts as a trigger that forces the global Hadley cell to adjust.

## Hydroclimatic Impacts and Forecasting

The modulation of the Hadley circulation by the MJO has tangible effects on regional weather. Because the Hadley cell dictates where air rises (creating rain) and sinks (creating dry conditions), shifts in its structure lead to coherent precipitation anomalies.

During boreal winter (DJF), the coupled MJO–Hadley system is associated with significant drying over North Africa, the Sahel, and parts of South Asia. Conversely, it enhances rainfall over Central America and the southern United States. Interestingly, the study found that the precipitation response when both the MJO and the Hadley cell are considered together is stronger than the sum of their individual effects. This "non-linear" amplification suggests that the interaction between tropical convective blobs and the global-scale overturning creates weather patterns that are more intense and spatially organized than previously thought.

These findings have significant implications for subseasonal-to-seasonal (S2S) prediction. The S2S timescale—typically 2 weeks to 2 months—is notoriously difficult to forecast, yet it is critical for agricultural planning and water resource management. By demonstrating a robust, moisture-driven link between the MJO and the global Hadley circulation, this research provides a new framework for understanding subseasonal predictability. Improving how climate models represent this coupling could lead to more accurate forecasts of rainfall and drought across the tropics and subtropics.

## Conclusion

This research clarifies the relationship between regional tropical weather and the global climate system. By systematically quantifying the MJO's influence on the zonal-mean Hadley circulation, the authors have shown that the most prominent subseasonal oscillation in the tropics is not just a regional phenomenon, but a major driver of the Earth's primary meridional overturning cell. Driven by latent heating and scaled by moisture gradients, this MJO-driven modulation represents a significant portion of the atmosphere's subseasonal variability, offering a promising avenue for enhancing our ability to predict weather patterns weeks in advance.
