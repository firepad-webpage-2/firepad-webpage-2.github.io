## Navigating Hybrid Uncertainty in Complex Systems

In the field of engineering design and reliability analysis, quantifying how variations in input parameters affect the performance of a system is a fundamental task. This process, known as global sensitivity analysis (GSA), traditionally operates under the assumption that all uncertainties can be represented by precise probability distributions. However, real-world engineering systems often face two distinct categories of uncertainty. The first is aleatory uncertainty, which refers to inherent randomness that cannot be reduced, such as fluctuations in wind speed or material variability. The second is epistemic uncertainty, which arises from a lack of knowledge, such as imprecise model parameters or bounded intervals for uncertain variables.

When these two types of uncertainty coexist—termed hybrid uncertainty—traditional sensitivity measures like the variance-based Sobol' indices often struggle. They typically require nested Monte Carlo simulations, where one loop handles the aleatory randomness and an outer loop handles the epistemic uncertainty, leading to an enormous computational burden. Furthermore, modern engineering outputs are rarely single numbers; they are often high-dimensional vectors, such as the pressure distribution across an entire aircraft wing or the temperature field of a turbine blade. Standard methods usually aggregate these high-dimensional responses into a single scalar value, which inevitably discards the spatial or structural information inherent in the data.

This research presents a new framework for sensitivity analysis designed to handle these exact challenges. By utilizing the Hilbert–Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC) within a double-space tensor-product Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS), the authors provide a way to separate the influences of aleatory and epistemic uncertainties while maintaining the full structural detail of high-dimensional outputs. This approach avoids the need for nested simulations and allows engineers to see exactly which output components are most affected by specific types of uncertainty.

## The Challenge of Hybrid Aleatory-Epistemic Modeling

To effectively analyze a system, one must first build a mathematical representation of its uncertainties. In a hybrid setting, we might have an aleatory input variable $X$ whose distribution itself depends on an epistemic parameter $\theta$. For example, $X \sim U(a, b) \text{ where } a, b \text{ are epistemic parameters}$. This hierarchical structure creates a dependency between the types of uncertainty that complicates standard sensitivity analysis, as most decomposition methods require inputs to be statistically independent.

The authors address this by mapping the physical input space into a latent space. Using an inverse probability integral transform (PIT), the original physical variables are converted into independent latent variables. If the physical system is described by $Y = g(X)$ where $X$ depends on $\theta$, the transformation yields a latent representation $Z = (\xi, \theta) \in \mathcal{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{d+p}$, where $\xi$ represents the inherent aleatory variability and $\theta$ represents the epistemic uncertainty. The system output is then re-expressed as a function of these independent latent components:

$$
Y = h(\xi, \theta)
$$

This decoupling is a critical step. It ensures that any subsequent sensitivity indices can be mathematically attributed to either the pure randomness of the environment ($\xi$) or the lack of knowledge regarding model parameters ($\theta$) without these effects being artificially entangled by the mathematical structure of the input distributions.

## A Kernel-Based Framework for Dependence: The HSIC

Rather than relying on variance—which only captures linear or specific functional relationships—the authors utilize the Hilbert–Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC). The HSIC is a moment-independent measure of dependence between two variables. It works by mapping the variables into a high-dimensional feature space (the RKHS) and measuring the correlation between them in that space.

A key property of HSIC is that it equals zero if and only if the variables are independent, provided a "characteristic" kernel is used. For a set of $n$ samples, the empirical estimator for HSIC can be calculated using Gram matrices $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{L}$ derived from the input and output kernels:

$$
\text{HSIC}(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{y}) = \frac{1}{(n-1)^2} \text{tr}(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{L})
$$

Where $\mathbf{H}$ is a centering matrix that ensures the features have zero mean. To perform sensitivity analysis, the authors construct the global input kernel as a tensor product of univariate kernels for each independent latent dimension $z_i \in Z$:

$$
k_Z(z, z') = \prod_{i=1}^{d+p} k_{Z_i}(z_i, z_i')
$$

By enforcing that each univariate kernel is "zero-mean" relative to the input distribution, the authors enable a kernel-based ANOVA decomposition. This allows the total dependence to be broken down into a sum of partial contributions from every possible subset of input variables $A$:

$$
\text{HSIC}_{total} = \sum_{A \subseteq \{1,\dots,d+p\}, A \neq \emptyset} \text{HSIC}_A
$$

## Dimension-Resolved Sensitivity via Double-Space RKHS

The most significant contribution of this work is the extension of this decomposition to the output space. In systems with $m$ output components $Y = (y_1, y_2, \dots, y_m)$, typical methods use a single global kernel for the entire vector $Y$. While this measures the total sensitivity, it masks how each individual $y_k$ behaves.

The authors propose a "Double-Space Tensor-Product RKHS" by factorizing the output kernel similarly to the input kernel:

$$
k_Y(y, y') = \prod_{k=1}^m k_{Y_k}(y_k, y_k')
$$

Using a mathematical tool called Möbius inversion, they derive a Double ANOVA Decomposition Theorem. This theorem allows for the calculation of interaction indices $\text{HSIC}_{A,V}$ that isolate the dependence between a specific subset of inputs $Z_A$ and a specific subset of outputs $Y_V$:

$$
\text{HSIC}_{A,V} = \sum_{B \subseteq A} \sum_{U \subseteq V} (-1)^{(|A|-|B|) + (|V|-|U|)} \text{HSIC}(Z_B, Y_U)
$$

These terms can be normalized to create scale-free sensitivity indices $S_{HSIC_{A,V}} = \frac{\text{HSIC}_{A,V}}{\text{HSIC}_{total}}$. Furthermore, the authors introduce a "universal scale" $V_{uni}$ which is the sum of all independent output variances. This allows for a direct, unbiased comparison across different dimensions. For instance, the main effect of an input $z_i$ on a specific output component $y_k$ is given by:

$$
S_{Z_i \to Y_k} = \frac{\text{HSIC}_{\{i\},\{k\}}}{V_{uni}}
$$

This level of granularity allows engineers to identify if a certain uncertainty (e.g., sensor noise) only affects a specific part of a structure (e.g., the wingtip) without affecting others.

## Efficient Implementation: The Single-Loop Architecture

A major practical advantage of this framework is its computational efficiency. By using the latent space transformation and the empirical HSIC estimator, the entire sensitivity analysis can be performed using a single loop of system evaluations.

1.  **Sampling:** A Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS) design generates samples in an independent uniform latent space.
2.  **Mapping:** These latent samples are mapped to physical aleatory variables $X$ and epistemic parameters $\theta$ using inverse cumulative distribution functions.
3.  **Evaluation:** The system model $Y = g(X, \theta)$ is evaluated. Because the model is treated as a black box, this stage is fully vectorized.
4.  **Estimation:** The sensitivity indices are computed directly from the latent samples and the resulting outputs. 

To ensure the results are not due to random chance, the framework incorporates permutation tests for statistical significance and Bootstrap resampling to provide confidence intervals for each index.

## Experimental Validation: Case Studies in Engineering

The authors validated their approach using two distinct problems. The first, a modified Ishigami function, served as a mathematical benchmark. The system featured aleatory variables with boundaries controlled by epistemic parameters. The analysis revealed that interactions accounted for nearly 70% of the total system variance. Interestingly, the framework identified "hidden" interactions in additive equations. For an output like $Y_3 = X_1^2 + X_2^2 + X_3^2$, one might expect no interactions. However, because the bounds of $X_1$ were determined by an epistemic parameter $\theta_1$, a strong interaction was detected between the latent variable $\xi_1$ and $\theta_1$. This demonstrates the framework's ability to uncover couplings introduced by the uncertainty structure itself, rather than just the physical equations.

The second case study involved the aerodynamic pressure field of a NACA 2412 airfoil. The output was a high-dimensional vector representing the pressure coefficient ($C_p$) at 30 different points along the airfoil surface. 

![spatial-heterogeneity-of-aerodynamic-interaction-matrices-at-the-leading-edge-mid-chord-and-trailing-edge-of-the-airfoil](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.14053v1/Example3_3.png)

The dimension-wise sensitivity indices showed a clear physical progression. At the leading edge, the uncertainty was almost entirely dominated by the angle of attack and its associated epistemic hyperparameters. As the flow moved toward the trailing edge, the sensitivities of variables related to the Mach number, Reynolds number, and boundary layer transition increased. This result aligns with aerodynamic theory regarding the development of viscous effects and provides a spatial "map" of uncertainty that can guide where more precise measurements or better model parameters are most needed.

## Significance and Broader Implications

This work provides a rigorous bridge between kernel-based dependence measures and the practical requirements of modern engineering uncertainty quantification. By enabling the separation of aleatory and epistemic uncertainties in high-dimensional settings, it offers several benefits:

*   **Targeted Uncertainty Reduction:** Engineers can identify which epistemic parameters (reducible uncertainty) contribute most to the variance of critical system components. This allows for prioritized data collection.
*   **Structural Insight:** The dimension-resolved indices prevent the loss of information that occurs when high-dimensional data is reduced to a single number, revealing how uncertainty "flows" through a physical system.
*   **Computational Accessibility:** The transition to a single-loop, vectorized architecture makes global sensitivity analysis feasible for high-fidelity simulations that were previously too expensive for nested Monte Carlo methods.

Ultimately, this framework allows for a more nuanced understanding of system reliability, ensuring that designs are robust not just to random environmental noise, but also to the fundamental gaps in our scientific knowledge.
