## Real-Time Price Impact Detection in Algorithmic Trading

In modern financial markets, the execution of large orders is rarely a single transaction. Instead, institutional investors and algorithmic desks break down "parent" orders into many smaller "child" orders to manage liquidity and minimize costs. A central challenge in this process is market impact—the phenomenon where a trader's own buying or selling activity causes the market price to move in an unfavorable direction. If a trader buys aggressively, the price tends to rise; if they sell, it tends to fall. This adverse drift reduces the realized profitability of the trade.

Managing this impact requires constant monitoring. Traditionally, traders have relied on "slippage"—the difference between the price they expected and the price they actually received. However, slippage is a noisy and slow metric. This paper proposes a different approach: detecting market impact by analyzing the precise timing of market reactions following a trader's actions. By looking for "timing signatures" where adverse price changes occur surprisingly soon after a fill, the method aims to provide a faster, more causally relevant signal than traditional price-based metrics.

## The Limitations of Conventional Execution Monitoring

Currently, the most common way to monitor execution quality is to measure post-fill slippage or "markouts." A markout measures the price change from the moment of execution to some future timestamp (e.g., 1 second, 10 seconds, or 1 minute later). If the price consistently moves against the trader shortly after their fills, it is assumed that their trading is impacting the market.

While conceptually simple, this approach faces two significant hurdles in practice: statistical slowness and causal ambiguity.

**Statistical Slowness**
The primary difficulty with price-based monitoring is the high level of background noise in financial markets. Asset prices are volatile, and the movement caused by a single small child order is often much smaller than the random price fluctuations occurring simultaneously. To distinguish a genuine impact signal from this background noise, a trader typically needs to aggregate data over hundreds of fills. For many execution schedules, by the time enough data has been collected to confirm that the impact is too high, the parent order may already be largely completed, making real-time adjustments impossible.

**Causal Ambiguity**
Even when a trader detects significant adverse price movement, the "why" remains unclear. Does the slippage occur because the trader’s own orders are leaking information and "pushing" the market? Or is the slippage happening because the market was already trending in that direction due to external news or other participants?

These two scenarios require opposite responses. If the trader is causing the impact, the correct response is often to slow down or change venues to reduce their footprint. If the market is moving due to external factors (often called "alpha"), the correct response might be to speed up the execution to finish before the price becomes even worse. Traditional slippage metrics cannot differentiate between these "informative fills" and "exogenous trends," leading to a risk where controllers react to noise or misattribute the cause of price drift.

## Quantifying "Surprise" Through Event Timings

The methodology described in this paper shifts the focus from the magnitude of price changes to the synchronicity of their timing. The core hypothesis is that if a trader's fill is genuinely impacting the market, the market's reaction should follow the fill with a specific timing signature. Specifically, an adverse price move should arrive "surprisingly" soon after the fill.

To quantify this surprise, the paper models the arrival of market events (price changes) as a point process. Under the null hypothesis—where the trader's fills have no impact—the market events are assumed to arrive according to a local Poisson process. In this model, the time between consecutive adverse price changes (durations) follows an exponential distribution:

$$p(\delta | \lambda) = \frac{1}{\lambda} e^{-\delta/\lambda}$$

where $\delta$ is the duration and $\lambda$ is the average time between events. In a dynamic market, $\lambda$ is not constant but varies over time as liquidity and volatility shift. The proposed method handles this by estimating $\lambda$ locally, using a small window of the $n$ most recent market events observed just before a trader's fill.

When a fill occurs, the trader measures the time $\delta'$ until the very next adverse market price change. The question then becomes: given the $n$ previous durations $\delta = (\delta_1, \dots, \delta_n)$, how likely is it that we would observe a duration as short as $\delta'$ purely by chance?

## The Predictive Density and Per-Fill p-values

To answer this question without assuming a fixed, known value for the market rate $\lambda$, the paper derives a "predictive density." This allows the trader to compare the new duration $\delta'$ directly against the previous observations $\delta$. Using a Bayesian approach with a non-informative prior $\pi(\lambda) \propto 1/\lambda$, the predictive probability density for the next duration $\delta'$ is found to follow a Lomax (or Pareto Type II) distribution:

$$p(\delta' | \delta) = \frac{n (n \bar{\delta})^n}{(n \bar{\delta} + \delta')^{n+1}}$$

where $\bar{\delta}$ is the average of the $n$ previous market durations. This specific distribution accounts for the uncertainty inherent in estimating the market rate from a small number of observations. From this density, we can calculate a p-value, $u(\delta')$, which represents the probability of seeing a duration as short as or shorter than the one observed:

$$u(\delta') = 1 - \left( \frac{n \bar{\delta}}{n \bar{\delta} + \delta'} \right)^n$$

If the trader's fill has no impact, $u(\delta')$ will be a random value between 0 and 1. However, if the fill consistently triggers immediate reactions, $u(\delta')$ will be frequently and unusually small. This provides a standardized "surprise" score for every single fill the trader receives.

## Accumulating Evidence Across Multiple Fills

While a single low p-value might be a coincidence, a sequence of low p-values is strong evidence of impact. To combine the evidence from several fills, the paper employs Fisher’s method. This technique transforms a set of independent p-values $u_i$ into a single test statistic $X_k$:

$$X_k = -2 \sum_{i=1}^k \ln(u_i)$$

Under the null hypothesis of no impact, the statistic $X_k$ follows a chi-squared distribution with $2k$ degrees of freedom, denoted as $\chi^2_{2k}$. This allows the trader to calculate the overall statistical significance of the observed timing synchronicity:

$$P(\chi^2_{2k} \geq X_k)$$

This combined statistic is highly sensitive. If even a few fills show a very strong timing correlation (very small $u_i$), the total $X_k$ will grow rapidly, signaling impact. Conversely, if the timings are consistent with the background market rate, the statistic will stay within expected bounds.

## Performance and Real-Time Detection Power

The power of this method lies in its speed. Because it focuses on the arrival times of events rather than the noisy magnitudes of price changes, it can reach statistical significance much faster than slippage-based monitors. 

Simulations conducted in the paper (visualized in {fig:detection-power-of-the-proposed-method}) demonstrate that the method can achieve high detection power—the ability to correctly identify impact—within just 5 to 10 fills. This is a significant improvement over the hundreds of fills required for traditional methods. Furthermore, because the local rate $\lambda(t)$ is constantly updated, the detector naturally adapts to changing market conditions, such as sudden bursts of activity or "lulls" in liquidity.

The paper also highlights a critical distinction between "statistical claims" and "economic claims." The statistical validity—the fact that the method correctly identifies timing anomalies—is proven through rigorous derivation and simulation. The economic claim—that these timing anomalies actually correspond to costly price impact—is an empirical hypothesis. The paper proposes that this link can be validated by correlating the timing surprise metric with realized slippage in historical datasets.

## Implications for Adaptive Trading

The ability to detect impact in real-time opens the door to more sophisticated "controller" logic in execution algorithms. In a typical execution, a trader might start with a standard schedule (e.g., Volume Weighted Average Price or VWAP). If the timing-based detector signals a high "surprise" after the first few child orders, the algorithm can automatically adjust:

1.  **Slowing Down:** If the signal suggests the trader is causing the impact, the algorithm can reduce its participation rate to allow the market to "digest" the previous orders and let liquidity replenish.
2.  **Venue Switching:** If impact is detected on specific venues but not others, the algorithm can re-route orders toward less "toxic" liquidity pools.
3.  **Alpha Recognition:** If the price is moving adversely but the timing detector shows *no* surprise, the algorithm can conclude that the move is exogenous (driven by others). In this case, instead of slowing down, the algorithm might speed up to complete the order before the price moves further away.

## Conclusion

The proposed methodology offers a framework for moving beyond aggregate, retrospective slippage analysis toward a "per-action" causal detection system. By leveraging the statistical properties of event durations and the predictive power of the Lomax distribution, traders can identify when their own actions are likely triggering market reactions.

While this paper focuses primarily on the time axis $\delta t$, the author notes that this is the first step in a broader research program. Future work could integrate the magnitude of price changes $\delta p$ (the "marks") with the timing signatures to create a joint test. Such a system would offer an even more robust tool for navigating the complexities of high-frequency market dynamics, ultimately leading to more efficient and adaptive execution for large-scale institutional investors.
