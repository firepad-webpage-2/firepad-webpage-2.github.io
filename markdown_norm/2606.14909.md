## Addressing Uncertainty Under Distribution Shift

In modern machine learning, models are typically trained on a fixed historical dataset and then deployed in dynamic environments. A significant challenge arises when the data distribution in the deployment environment differs from the training environment, a phenomenon known as distribution shift. For instance, a medical diagnostic tool trained on data from one hospital may struggle when applied to patients in a different region, or a fraud detection system may become less effective as attackers evolve their tactics.

When these "legacy models" face distribution shift, their predictive accuracy often declines. Perhaps more critically, the models' internal estimates of uncertainty become unreliable. A model might express high confidence in a prediction that is, in fact, incorrect. To mitigate this, researchers use conformal prediction, a framework that generates prediction sets instead of single labels. These sets are designed to contain the true label with a user-specified probability, such as $90\%$.

However, standard conformal prediction primarily guarantees marginal coverage—meaning the error rate is controlled on average across all possible inputs. Under distribution shift, this global guarantee can hide significant local failures. A model might achieve $90\%$ coverage overall but fail $50\%$ of the time on specific, difficult types of data points. The research presented here introduces Audited Conformal Prediction (ACP), a method designed to improve this local reliability (conditional coverage) without requiring a full and expensive retraining of the original legacy model.

## The Limitations of Current Adaptation Strategies

When faced with distribution shift, practitioners often choose between two paths: using the legacy model as-is with standard calibration or retraining the model on new data. 

Standard conformal prediction, while robust, lacks granularity. If the shift makes the model perform poorly on a specific subgroup, the conformal prediction set might not expand enough to reflect that increased risk, as long as the overall average coverage remains near $1 - \alpha$. This is particularly problematic for "hard" samples where the legacy model is most likely to fail.

Retraining, on the other hand, involves updating the entire model using newly labeled data from the target distribution. While conceptually simple, retraining is often impractical. New labeled data is usually scarce and expensive to obtain. Training a high-capacity model (like a deep neural network) on a small dataset can lead to overfitting or inefficient use of the available information. Furthermore, retraining does not inherently solve the uncertainty quantification problem; the new model still requires calibration to provide reliable prediction sets.

ACP offers a middle ground. Instead of retraining the primary classifier, it trains a smaller, auxiliary "audit model." This auditor specifically learns to identify the strengths and weaknesses of the existing legacy model on the new data distribution.

## The Core Principle of Audited Conformal Prediction

The fundamental innovation of ACP is the use of an auditor, denoted as $\hat{r}$, to predict the reliability of the legacy model $f_0$. For a given input $X$, the oracle reliability is defined as:

$$
r^*(x) = P(f_0(X) = Y | X = x)
$$

This function represents the true probability that the legacy model's top prediction is correct for a specific input. Since the true distribution is unknown, ACP estimates this using a small amount of labeled data from the target population, $\mathcal{D}_{cal}$.

The process begins by splitting the available calibration data into two parts: $\mathcal{D}_1$ and $\mathcal{D}_2$. 
1.  **Auditor Training:** $\mathcal{D}_1$ is used to train the auditor $\hat{r}$. For each sample $(X_i, Y_i)$ in $\mathcal{D}_1$, the system generates a binary label $R_i = \mathbb{1}\{f_0(X_i) = Y_i\}$, which is $1$ if the legacy model was correct and $0$ otherwise. The auditor is trained to predict $R_i$ based on the input $X_i$ and other signals, such as the legacy model's internal confidence scores.
2.  **Conformal Calibration:** $\mathcal{D}_2$ is then used to calibrate the final prediction sets. By incorporating the auditor's predictions, the framework can adapt the size of the prediction sets: they become larger for inputs where the auditor predicts the legacy model is unreliable and stay compact where the model remains trustworthy.

## Two Strategies for Integration: ACP-MC and ACP-ACC

The authors propose two primary ways to integrate the auditor into the conformal prediction pipeline, depending on the desired type of guarantee.

### ACP with Marginal Coverage (ACP-MC)
This approach focuses on refining the probabilistic outputs of the classifier. In a binary setting, the auditor $\hat{r}$ directly estimates the probability that the legacy model is correct. For multiclass settings, the method uses a refined classifier $m(x; \hat{r}, \hat{\eta}, f_0)$ that combines the legacy model's predictions with the auditor's assessment and a "wrong-label" model $\hat{\eta}$ (which estimates the distribution of labels when the legacy model is wrong). 

Standard split conformal prediction is then applied to this refined classifier. The result is a system that maintains exact marginal coverage while often achieving better conditional coverage because the underlying scores are more sensitive to the distribution shift.

### ACP with Audited Conditional Coverage (ACP-ACC)
This strategy aims for a stronger guarantee called group-conditional coverage. The goal is to ensure that $P(Y \in \hat{C}(X) | X \in G) \geq 1 - \alpha$ for specific groups $G$. ACP-ACC defines these groups using the auditor's scores. For example, one group might consist of all samples where the auditor's reliability score is below a certain threshold.

The method defines group indicators $\hat{G}_j(x) = \mathbb{1}\{\hat{r}(x) \leq t_j\}$ and uses a specialized calibration technique (augmented quantile regression) to ensure that each reliability group receives appropriate coverage. This leads to a theoretical guarantee:

$$
P(Y \in \hat{C}(X) | G^*_j(X)=g) \geq 1 - \alpha - \frac{(1+\alpha)P[\hat{G}_j(X) \neq G^*_j(X)]}{P[G^*_j(X)=g]}
$$

This expression shows that as the auditor $\hat{r}$ becomes more accurate at identifying the legacy model's failure modes (i.e., as the discrepancy term in the fraction decreases), the system approaches perfect conditional coverage for those groups.

## Empirical Performance and Data Efficiency

The researchers tested ACP across various scenarios, including synthetic concept shifts and real-world image classification tasks (CIFAR-10 and Camelyon17). A key finding is that ACP is significantly more data-efficient than retraining the legacy model when the new labeled dataset is small. 

In the Camelyon17 dataset, which involves identifying tumor tissues across different hospitals, standard conformal prediction failed to provide adequate coverage for "hard" samples—those where the legacy model was most likely to be wrong. Specifically, for samples where the true reliability was low, standard methods significantly under-covered (e.g., providing only $60\%$ coverage when $90\%$ was requested). 

ACP-ACC, by contrast, successfully adjusted its prediction sets for these difficult samples. It produced larger sets for unreliable inputs, thereby meeting the $90\%$ coverage target for those specific subgroups, while maintaining small, efficient sets for the easy inputs. This adaptive behavior is what distinguishes ACP from static methods; it effectively "listens" to the auditor to determine when to be cautious.

## Significance and Future Directions

The significance of ACP lies in its ability to provide rigorous statistical guarantees in the face of unpredictable environment changes. By shifting the focus from "fixing" the model to "auditing" its reliability, the researchers have created a tool that is both practical and mathematically grounded.

Key takeaways from the work include:
*   **Efficiency:** High-quality uncertainty quantification can be achieved with relatively little data from the target distribution.
*   **Adaptivity:** Prediction sets are not uniform; they grow or shrink based on the predicted reliability of the underlying model.
*   **Robustness:** The method provides explicit guarantees even when the nature of the distribution shift is unknown.

While the current framework relies on the quality of the audit model, it opens doors for further research into more complex auditors or online learning settings where the auditor is updated continuously as new data arrives. For practitioners, ACP provides a pathway to deploy legacy models in new environments with a reliable safety net, ensuring that when the model is likely to fail, the uncertainty estimates will reflect that risk.
