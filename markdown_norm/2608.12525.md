## The Role of Heat Storage in Modern District Heating

District heating networks (DHNs) are a central pillar in the transition toward sustainable urban heating. By centralizing heat production and distributing it through a network of insulated pipes, DHNs improve energy efficiency and facilitate the integration of diverse heat sources, including industrial waste heat, geothermal energy, and combined heat and power (CHP) plants. In systems utilizing extraction CHP units, the facility generates both electricity and heat simultaneously. However, a fundamental challenge arises because the demand for heat and the market value of electricity often peak at different times. Without flexibility, a DHN operator might be forced to extract heat when electricity prices are high, losing potential revenue from electricity sales.

Integrating thermal energy storage (TES) allows operators to decouple heat production from immediate demand. Heat can be produced and stored when electricity prices are low (or when surplus renewable energy is available) and discharged when the CHP unit would otherwise face high opportunity costs. Despite these clear benefits, determining the optimal size for a storage tank remains a complex engineering problem. A tank that is too small fails to capture available economic benefits, while a tank that is too large incurs unnecessary capital expenditure (CAPEX) that can never be recovered through operational savings.

Existing research has often relied on simplified linear models or aggregated network representations to estimate storage needs. While computationally efficient, these methods frequently overlook the nonlinear physics of heat transport—such as temperature drops and pressure losses—that define real-world network performance. This research introduces an automated, physics-based optimization framework that bridges the gap between detailed simulation and large-scale system design.

## Limitations of Current Sizing Methodologies

Determining the capacity of short-term heat storage typically involves two main schools of thought: simplified linear modeling and physics-based nonlinear modeling. Linear approaches, often formulated as Mixed-Integer Linear Programs (MILPs), are widely used because they can handle long time horizons. However, they frequently aggregate the entire network into a single "demand point." This simplification ignores the spatial reality of the network: pipes lose heat to the environment, and fluid friction causes pressure drops that increase pumping costs. Neglecting these factors can lead to an underestimation of the total heat supply required, subsequently resulting in undersized storage units.

On the other hand, physics-based nonlinear models capture these effects but have historically struggled with scalability. Because the math required to describe fluid dynamics and thermal transients is computationally intensive, these models were often restricted to very small networks, such as those serving fewer than ten buildings. The lack of an automated way to optimize storage size while accounting for full network physics has left DHN planners without a robust tool for larger, real-world systems. This work addresses this gap by combining detailed physical equations with advanced mathematical optimization techniques that allow for the sizing of storage in networks with dozens of consumers.

## A Scalable Physics-Based Optimization Framework

The researchers developed an automated methodology formulated as a continuous nonlinear programming (NLP) problem. The objective is to minimize the total discounted project cost $J(\varphi, x)$ over a typical investment period of 20 years. This cost function includes the capital investment for the storage tank and the operational expenditures (OPEX) for heat production and pumping.

The optimization involves two sets of variables. The design variables $\varphi = [\alpha, \beta, \delta, \zeta, \iota]^T$ represent decisions like the storage size $\zeta$, the time-dependent (dis-)charging flow rate $\iota$, the producer mass flow rate $\delta$, and operational parameters for consumer substations $\alpha$ and $\beta$. The physical variables $x = [m, p, q, \theta]^T$ represent the resulting state of the system: storage mass $m$, nodal pressures $p$, pipe mass flow rates $q$, and nodal temperatures $\theta$.

To achieve scalability, the framework uses a quasi-steady-state approach for the network pipes. This assumes that while heat demand and electricity prices change over hours, the hydraulic state of the network reaches equilibrium quickly enough that transients in the pipes can be ignored. However, the transient behavior of the storage unit—how its energy content changes over time—is modeled in detail.

## Overcoming the "Discreteness" of Storage Operation

One of the primary mathematical hurdles in storage optimization is the discrete nature of charging and discharging. In a standard physical model, a storage tank either takes heat in or puts heat out, often represented by "if-then" logic or "min/max" functions. These functions are problematic for gradient-based optimization because they are not smooth; they have sharp corners where the derivative is undefined, which can cause the optimization algorithm to fail.

The researchers solved this by using the **softplus function**, a smooth approximation that allows the algorithm to treat charging and discharging as a continuous process. This enables the use of the **adjoint method**, a highly efficient way to calculate how a change in any design variable (like making the tank 1% larger) affects the total cost. By solving a set of "forward" equations to simulate the system and a set of "backward" adjoint equations to find the gradients, the optimizer can navigate the complex cost landscape of a large network much faster than traditional trial-and-error simulations.

![storage-model](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12525v1/FIG4_singlecolumn.png)
*{fig:storage-model}: The 2-zone storage model illustrating the hot and cold zones and the flow variables for charging and discharging.*

The storage unit itself is modeled as a 2-zone cylindrical tank. It tracks the mass of the hot zone $m_{ij}(t)$ and the cold zone $m_{ik}(t)$ based on the charging flow rate $\iota_{ij}(t)$. The total mass $m_{tot,ij}$ is determined by the optimized volume $\zeta_{ij}$ and the physical limit $V_{stor,max}$. Thermal energy balances account for heat losses to the ambient air $Q_{loss}$ and the energy transferred during operation ($Q_{char}$ and $Q_{dischar}$).

## Temporal Aggregation and the Oostende Case Study

Simulating every hour of a 20-year period is computationally prohibitive. Instead, the framework uses a clustering technique to identify "representative days" that capture the variation in heat demand, outside temperature, and electricity prices throughout the year. For each representative day, a cyclic constraint is applied, ensuring that the energy stored in the tank at the end of the day equals the energy it started with.

![temporal-aggregation](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12525v1/FIG3_singlecolumn.png)
*{fig:temporal-aggregation}: Representation of temporal aggregation into representative days with cyclic storage constraints.*

The methodology was tested on a real 3rd-generation DHN in Oostende, Belgium. This network is significant because it includes 30 diverse consumers—offices, hospitals, and industrial buildings—with a peak demand of 13.4 MW. A waste incineration CHP plant provides the heat. The goal was to find the optimal size for a central storage unit to maximize the revenue from electricity sales on the wholesale market.

## Economic and Operational Results

The optimization identified an optimal storage size of 1038 m³. By integrating this unit, the levelized cost of heat (LCOH) dropped from 1.82 €cent/kWh to 1.52 €cent/kWh, representing a 16.5% reduction. Over the 20-year horizon, this resulted in a net financial benefit of 730,000 €.

![cost-vs-size](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12525v1/FIG10_singlecolumn.png)
*{fig:cost-vs-size}: Discounted project costs as a function of storage size, identifying the minimum total cost at 1038 m³.*

The operational savings were driven by the storage's ability to shift production. As shown in the electricity price data ({fig:electricity-prices}), prices fluctuate significantly across different time segments.

With the storage in place, the CHP unit concentrated its heat supply during periods when electricity prices were at their lowest ({fig:chp-heat-supply}). During periods of high electricity prices, the CHP unit reduced heat extraction to maximize electricity revenue, while the heat storage unit discharged to meet the network's demand ({fig:storage-supply}).

## The Impact of Physical Accuracy

A critical finding of this research is the quantitative proof that simplified network models can lead to poor investment decisions. When the researchers compared their physics-based results to a simplified model that aggregated the DHN into a single demand point, the difference was stark. The simplified model suggested an optimal storage size of 771 m³—nearly 26% smaller than the 1038 m³ calculated by the detailed model.

The reason for this discrepancy lies in the network heat losses. In the Oostende network, pipes lose roughly 25.7% of the heat supplied by the producer. Simplified models that ignore the physical length and insulation properties of the pipes effectively assume these losses are zero. Consequently, they underestimate the total amount of heat that needs to be stored to cover demand, leading to undersized tanks. In the case study, following the "simplified" advice would have resulted in an undersized storage unit that cost the operator an additional 132,000 € in lost operational efficiency over 20 years.

This work demonstrates that for systems of significant scale, the interaction between network physics and storage capacity cannot be ignored. The proposed methodology provides a scalable solution for DHN operators to size storage accurately, ensuring that infrastructure investments are grounded in the physical and economic realities of the energy transition.
