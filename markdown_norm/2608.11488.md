## Introduction to Quantum Heat Exchange

The study of quantum thermodynamics focuses on understanding how energy, heat, and work behave at the smallest scales, where fluctuations and quantum effects dominate. As quantum technologies such as quantum batteries, heat engines, and refrigerators become increasingly sophisticated, researchers need exact frameworks to describe how these systems exchange energy with their surroundings. Unlike macroscopic thermodynamics, where quantities like heat are often deterministic, microscopic thermodynamic quantities are inherently stochastic. This means that to understand a process, one must examine the full probability distribution of outcomes rather than just the average values.

Central to this field are fluctuation theorems (FTs), which provide exact relations for the statistics of thermodynamic variables in systems far from equilibrium. One specific type, the exchange fluctuation theorem (XFT), characterizes the heat transfer between two reservoirs at different temperatures. A well-known example is the Jarzynski-Wójcik exchange fluctuation theorem (JW-XFT), which describes the ratio of probabilities for heat flowing along the temperature gradient versus against it.

*{fig:schematic-of-the-collisional-model}: A schematic representation of the quantum collisional model where environmental auxiliary units interact sequentially with a system to simulate open-system dynamics.*

Most existing derivations of XFTs assume that the environments (or "baths") are Markovian. In a Markovian setting, the environment is memoryless; each interaction between the system and the bath is independent of the past. However, real-world quantum environments often exhibit non-Markovian behavior. This memory can stem from strong coupling between the system and the environment, structured reservoirs, or correlations within the bath itself. The research presented here addresses this gap by establishing a generalized exchange fluctuation theorem for non-Markovian baths within a microscopic framework known as the quantum collisional model.

## Microscopic Foundations: The Collisional Model

To study these memory effects rigorously, the authors employ a quantum collisional model. In this framework, an open quantum system (the system of interest) interacts with an environment composed of a large number of discrete auxiliary units. The dynamics proceed in steps: in each step, the system "collides" or interacts with a fresh auxiliary unit from the bath. 

This model is particularly useful for two reasons. First, it allows for a fully microscopic description of the interaction, meaning every part of the energy exchange is accounted for through unitary operations. Second, it provides a natural way to introduce non-Markovianity. By allowing the auxiliary units within the bath to interact with one another before they interact with the system, the bath can retain information about previous collisions. 

In this study, two thermal baths, $A$ and $B$, are considered with inverse temperatures $\beta_A$ and $\beta_B$. The inverse temperature is defined as $\beta = 1/k_B T$, where $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant and $T$ is the temperature. The goal is to track the heat $Q$ exchanged between these baths when they are allowed to interact, either directly or through an intermediate probe.

## Defining Memory: Intra-bath Interactions

The key to modeling non-Markovianity in this work is the sequence of interactions. In a standard Markovian collisional model, the $n$-th auxiliary unit of a bath is prepared in a thermal state and interacts with the system, after which it is discarded. In the non-Markovian extension proposed here, the $n$-th unit interacts with the $(n-1) \text{-th}$ unit before it participates in the heat exchange process.

Let $U_{AA}$ and $U_{BB}$ be the unitary operators describing the intra-bath interactions for baths $A$ and $B$, respectively. These interactions create correlations and move the auxiliary units away from their initial local thermal equilibrium. Consequently, when the $n$-th unit eventually interacts to exchange heat, it carries "memory" of the $(n-1) \text{-th}$ unit’s history. The strength of this memory is controlled by interaction parameters like $\kappa$. When these intra-bath interactions are absent (i.e., the unitaries are identity operators), the model reverts to the standard Markovian case.

## The Generalized Exchange Fluctuation Theorem

The authors derive a generalized XFT that accounts for these memory effects. They use the Two-Point Measurement (TPM) protocol, where the energy of the relevant particles is measured at the beginning and the end of a single collision step. By comparing the probability of a "forward" trajectory (heat $Q$ flowing from $B$ to $A$) to a "reverse" trajectory (heat $-Q$ flowing from $A$ to $B$), they arrive at the following relation:

$$
P(Q) / P_B(-Q) = e^{\Delta \beta Q} C
$$

In this equation, $P(Q)$ is the probability of heat exchange $Q$, $P_B(-Q)$ is the probability of the reverse process, and $\Delta \beta = \beta_A - \beta_B$ is the difference in inverse temperatures. The factor $C$ is the central contribution of this work: it is a trajectory-dependent correction factor that quantifies the impact of bath memory. 

In a traditional Markovian setup, $C$ would be equal to $1$, leading to the standard JW-XFT. However, in the presence of non-Markovian baths, $C$ deviates from unity, indicating that memory effects fundamentally change the statistical balance of heat flow.

## Dissecting the Correction Factor $C$

The correction factor $C$ is not a simple constant but a complex term consisting of conditional averages of exponentials. It captures two primary physical effects:
1.  **Stochastic Free-Energy Changes ($\Delta f$):** Because of the intra-bath interactions, the auxiliary units are no longer in their original thermal states when they undergo the heat-exchange collision. The factor $C$ accounts for the "athermal" nature of these units.
2.  **Stochastic Mutual Information:** The interactions between successive auxiliary units ($n$ and $n-1$) create quantum correlations. The correction factor tracks the information shared between these units, which acts as a form of stored memory that influences the heat exchange.

By incorporating these terms, the generalized XFT provides a modified version of the second law of thermodynamics. For these non-equilibrium processes, the integral version of the theorem implies:

$$
\Delta \beta \langle Q \rangle + \langle \ln C \rangle \geq 0
$$

This expression shows that the average heat flow $\langle Q \rangle$ is constrained not just by the temperature gradient $\Delta \beta$, but also by the information-theoretic and energetic corrections bundled in $\langle \ln C \rangle$.

## Heat Exchange Scenarios: Direct vs. Probe-Mediated

The paper analyzes two distinct physical scenarios for heat exchange, each leading to a specific form of the correction factor $C$.

### Direct Bath-Bath Interaction
In the first scenario, the $n$-th auxiliary units of bath $A$ and bath $B$ interact directly with each other after their respective intra-bath collisions. The total unitary operation for this cycle is $U = U_{AB} (U_{AA} \otimes U_{BB})$. 

![direct-interaction-schematic](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11488v1/img-1.jpeg)
*{fig:direct-interaction-schematic}: Heat exchange occurring through a direct interaction between the $n$-th auxiliary units of two non-Markovian baths.*

The correction factor $C_{dir}$ depends on whether one or both baths are non-Markovian. If both baths have memory, $C_{dir}$ includes contributions from the free-energy changes and mutual information of both reservoirs.

### Probe-Mediated Interaction
In the second scenario, a probe system $S$ acts as a mediator. The probe interacts first with one bath and then the other. The total unitary is $U = (U_{SA} U_{SB}) (U_{AA} \otimes U_{BB})$.

![probe-mediated-interaction-schematic](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11488v1/img-2.jpeg)
*{fig:probe-mediated-interaction-schematic}: Heat exchange mediated by an intermediate probe system $S$, which sequentially interacts with the auxiliary units of the two baths.*

A key finding here is that even if the baths are Markovian, the factor $C_{probe}$ may not be unity. This is because the probe itself can reach a non-equilibrium steady state, retaining a "memory" of its own previous interactions. This underscores that the probe is not just a passive conduit but an active participant in the thermodynamics of the system.

## Thermodynamic Consequences and Reverse Heat Flow

To illustrate the impact of these theorems, the authors perform numerical simulations using two-level systems (qubits) for the bath units and the probe. The local Hamiltonian for each qubit is $H = \omega |1\rangle \langle 1|$, where $\omega$ is the energy splitting. The interactions are modeled using an exchange Hamiltonian $H_{+-}$, with coupling strengths $g$ for inter-bath/probe interactions and $\kappa$ for intra-bath memory interactions.

The research uses the Breuer-Laine-Piilo (BLP) measure to quantify the degree of non-Markovianity in the baths. As shown in the figures below, the non-Markovianity varies non-monotonically with the coupling strength $\kappa$.

![non-markovianity-measure](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11488v1/img-3.jpeg)
*{fig:non-markovianity-measure}: The BLP non-Markovianity measure as a function of the intra-bath coupling strength $\kappa$.*

One of the most significant results of the numerical analysis is the behavior of the probability ratio $P(Q) / P_B(-Q)$. As the non-Markovianity of the baths increases, this ratio decreases relative to the Markovian prediction.

![probability-ratio-for-direct-interaction](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11488v1/img-4.jpeg)
*{fig:probability-ratio-for-direct-interaction}: The ratio of forward to reverse heat transfer probabilities for the direct interaction scenario, showing suppression as memory effects ($\kappa$) increase.*

![probability-ratio-for-probe-interaction](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11488v1/img-5.jpeg)
*{fig:probability-ratio-for-probe-interaction}: The ratio for the probe-mediated scenario, further demonstrating the influence of bath memory.*

A lower ratio $P(Q) / P_B(-Q)$ implies that the probability of "reverse" heat transfer—heat flowing from the colder bath to the hotter bath—is relatively enhanced compared to the Markovian case. This indicates that environmental memory can make "atypical" thermodynamic events more likely. This suppression of the probability ratio serves as a thermodynamic signature of non-Markovianity, suggesting that heat flow statistics could be used to detect and characterize memory effects in quantum experiments.

By providing a microscopic link between memory and heat statistics, this work extends the reach of fluctuation theorems to more realistic, correlated quantum environments. It offers a framework for analyzing energy exchange in complex systems, with potential applications in the design of next-generation quantum thermal machines.
