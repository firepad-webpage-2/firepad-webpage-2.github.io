## Introduction to Electron-Electron Correlations

In the realm of quantum mechanics and particle physics, understanding how individual particles interact within a shared volume is a fundamental pursuit. One of the primary tools used to characterize these interactions is the second-order correlation function, $g^{(2)}$, which describes the probability of detecting two particles at specific coordinates in time or space relative to one another. While these functions were originally developed by Roy Glauber for photons in quantum optics, they have since become essential for studying fermions like electrons. Unlike photons, which can "bunch" together, electrons are governed by the Pauli exclusion principle and carry a negative charge, leading to "anti-bunching"—a phenomenon where electrons avoid occupying the same state or being in close proximity.

![electron-electron-repulsion-conceptual-overview](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12040v1/fig1.png)
*{fig:electron-electron-repulsion-conceptual-overview}: Conceptual overview of electron-electron repulsion during photoemission from a nanotip. Electrons emitted in close proximity experience Coulomb forces that lead to separation in both energy ($\Delta E$) and angle ($\Delta \alpha$).*

Recent advancements in ultrafast science have enabled the generation of electron pulses that are extremely short—lasting only a few femtoseconds—and originate from nanometer-sized sources, such as sharp metallic needle tips. When two or more electrons are liberated from such a small area in such a short window of time, the Coulomb repulsion between them becomes exceptionally strong. This interaction reshapes the pulse as it travels, creating correlations between the electrons' positions and their energies. 

Historically, researchers have studied these correlations primarily in one dimension, focusing on "longitudinal" effects where electrons push each other apart along their direction of travel, resulting in measurable energy shifts. However, a complete understanding requires a three-dimensional (3D) perspective that includes "transverse" correlations—how they push each other sideways. This research provides a comprehensive experimental and theoretical characterization of these 3D correlations, offering new insights into the intrinsic behavior of electron pulses before they are processed by the lenses and apertures of an electron microscope.

## Experimental Framework for Capturing Two-Electron Events

To capture the complex dance of interacting electrons, the researchers utilized a specialized experimental setup centered on a tungsten needle tip with an apex radius of approximately 10 nm. This tip was placed in an ultra-high vacuum environment and biased with a small negative voltage to help guide emitted electrons toward a detector. 

The emission process was triggered by ultrashort laser pulses from an amplified erbium fiber oscillator. These pulses were compressed to a duration of 12 fs and focused onto the tip apex. The laser intensity was carefully tuned to reach a near-field intensity of approximately $1.7 \times 10^{13}\text{ W cm}^{-2}$. Under these conditions, the Keldysh parameter was $\gamma \approx 0.7$, indicating that the electrons were emitted in a sub-cycle regime, where the oscillating electric field of the laser itself plays a significant role in driving the electrons immediately after they leave the metal surface. The ponderomotive energy, which represents the average kinetic energy an electron gains from the oscillating field, was estimated at $U_P = 4\text{ eV}$.

The key to measuring 3D correlations lies in the detection system. The researchers employed a time-of-flight spectrometer paired with a delay-line detector (DLD). A standard DLD can sometimes struggle to distinguish two electrons that hit the detector at nearly the same time and position due to a "dead time" or "dead radius" in the hardware. To overcome this, the team implemented a reconstruction method based on a neural network, which allowed them to resolve electron pairs with a separation of less than 2 mm on the detector surface. By recording the $(x, y)$ position and the arrival time $t$ for each electron, the system could reconstruct the full 3D momentum vectors $\vec{p}_1$ and $\vec{p}_2$ for every two-electron event.

## Mapping 3D Correlations: The Angular Energy-Difference Spectrum

The primary result of the measurement is the Angular Energy-Difference Spectrum (AEDS). This mapping technique plots the difference in energy between two electrons, $\Delta E$, against the mutual angle between their paths, $\Delta \alpha$. This visualization allows researchers to see how the two types of repulsion—longitudinal and transverse—compete and transition into one another.

![experimental-and-simulated-correlation-maps](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12040v1/fig2.png)
*{fig:experimental-and-simulated-correlation-maps}: Comparison of experimental and simulated Angular Energy-Difference Spectra (AEDS). The top row shows the correlation maps, while the bottom row illustrates the longitudinal repulsion (energy filtering) and transverse repulsion (spatial filtering) signatures.*

When the electrons are emitted with almost no angular separation ($\Delta \alpha \approx 0$), the Coulomb force acts almost entirely along the longitudinal axis. This results in a significant energy gap between the two particles. The researchers observed an "Energy Repulsion Line" (ERL) representing the most probable energy difference for a given angle. At small angles, this gap reached a maximum of $\Delta E = 3.3\text{ eV}$. As the mutual angle $\Delta \alpha$ increased, the energy gap was observed to decrease, demonstrating that the electrons were using their mutual repulsion to push each other sideways rather than just forward and backward.

To isolate these effects, the researchers applied specific filters to their data:
1.  **Spatial Filtering:** By selecting only those electron pairs with a small angular difference ($\Delta\alpha < 3^\circ$), they highlighted the longitudinal repulsion. The resulting energy correlation map showed a clear diagonal suppression, meaning it is highly unlikely to find two electrons with the same energy if they are traveling in the same direction.
2.  **Energy Filtering:** By selecting only those pairs with very similar energies ($\Delta E < 1\text{ eV}$), they highlighted the transverse repulsion. The spatial distribution of these pairs formed a "doughnut" shape, with a hole in the center. This indicates that electrons with the same energy cannot exist at the same spatial position; they must be separated by a minimum angle. The maximum probability for these energy-matched pairs occurred at $\Delta\alpha \approx 10.9^\circ$.

These experimental findings were supported by semiclassical point-particle simulations. In these simulations, electrons are treated as classical charged particles that are "born" at the tip surface and then propagate according to the laws of electrostatics and the laser's electromagnetic field. The high degree of agreement between the simulations and the experiments suggests that, in this regime, the observed correlations are dominated by classical Coulomb repulsion rather than quantum mechanical effects like Pauli blocking or the Coulomb blockade.

## Probing the Influence of Source Parameters

A significant advantage of the simulation model is the ability to test how various physical parameters of the electron source influence the resulting correlations. The researchers performed scaling studies on three key variables: the tip radius, the pulse duration, and the laser intensity.

![numerical-parameter-scaling-studies](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12040v1/fig3.png)
*{fig:numerical-parameter-scaling-studies}: Numerical parameter scaling studies showing how the Energy Repulsion Line (ERL) shifts with changes in tip radius, pulse duration, and laser intensity.*

The simulations revealed that the size of the tip radius ($r_{\text{tip}}$) mainly affects the slope of the Energy Repulsion Line. As the tip becomes blunter (larger radius), the ERL decays more rapidly as the angle increases. Interestingly, the maximum energy gap at $\Delta\alpha = 0$ remains relatively stable regardless of the radius, suggesting that the initial temporal confinement is a more dominant factor for longitudinal repulsion than the geometric size of the source.

The pulse duration ($\tau$) had a more pronounced effect on the magnitude of the energy gap. Shorter pulses (e.g., $\tau = 12.5\text{ fs}$) lead to a much larger $\Delta E$ because the electrons are forced into a smaller temporal "box" during emission, increasing the initial Coulomb potential energy. When the pulse duration was increased toward the picosecond regime, the energy gap significantly narrowed.

Finally, increasing the laser intensity $I$ was found to increase the maximum energy gap. This happens for two reasons: higher intensities lead to faster electron emission (sub-cycle emission) and provide a stronger "quiver" motion that can enhance the separation between particles during the initial stages of acceleration.

## Modifying Beam Statistics through Filtering

One of the most practical applications of understanding these correlations is the ability to manipulate the statistical nature of the electron beam. In a standard electron source, the number of electrons emitted per pulse typically follows a Poissonian distribution, which is characterized by inherent "shot noise." For many high-precision applications, such as ultrafast electron microscopy, it is desirable to have a "sub-Poissonian" beam—where the pulses are more likely to contain exactly one electron, and multi-electron pulses are suppressed.

The research demonstrated that by applying the spatial and energy filters identified in the correlation maps, one can "cull" multi-electron events that fall outside the single-electron norms. 

![filtering-impacts-on-correlation-function](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12040v1/meas2Appendix.png)
*{fig:filtering-impacts-on-correlation-function}: Analysis of electron correlations and the impact of filtering on the second-order correlation function, demonstrating the transition to sub-Poissonian statistics.*

The researchers evaluated the effectiveness of these filters using the $g^{(2)}$ value. A value of $g^{(2)} = 1$ indicates a standard random (Poissonian) beam, while $g^{(2)} < 1$ indicates a sub-Poissonian beam with reduced noise.
*   **Energy Filtering alone** (with a 1 eV passband) reduced the correlation function to $g^{(2)} = 0.29$.
*   **Spatial Filtering alone** (using a $2^\circ$ aperture) reduced it to $g^{(2)} = 0.57$.
*   **Combined Filtering**, which uses both energy and spatial constraints, achieved an unprecedented result of $g^{(2)} = 0.021$.

This extremely low $g^{(2)}$ value represents a near-total suppression of two-electron pulses. While this filtering process necessarily reduces the total number of electrons in the beam (reducing the current), it provides a highly controlled source where almost every pulse contains exactly one electron. This trade-off is often acceptable in experiments where signal-to-noise ratio and temporal resolution are more critical than raw beam brightness.

## Significance for Ultrafast Electron Microscopy

This work is significant because it provides the first complete 3D experimental picture of how electrons interact immediately upon emission from a nanotip. In current ultrafast electron microscopes (UEMs), apertures and energy filters are common components, but they are typically used to improve spatial resolution or minimize chromatic aberration. This study shows that these same components are inherently performing a "quantum" task: they are selecting for specific correlation states and reshaping the statistics of the electron pulse.

By providing a validated semiclassical model and a detailed map of the 3D correlations, this research allows engineers to design future electron columns that are specifically optimized for multi-electron interactions. For example, if a researcher wants to study electron entanglement or perform "correlated microscopy"—where two electrons are used to probe a sample simultaneously—they now have a roadmap for how to preserve or enhance these correlations.

Furthermore, the study establishes that the observed repulsion is primarily a classical Coulomb effect under these specific experimental conditions. This sets a clear baseline for future experiments. If researchers move to even smaller tips or even higher occupancies, they may begin to see deviations from this classical behavior, signaling the onset of purely quantum mechanical effects like Pauli blocking. Understanding where the classical description ends and the quantum description begins is a vital step in the development of next-generation quantum electron optics.
