## Redefining Color Sensing in Nanophotonics

Nanophotonic color routers (CRs) represent a potential alternative to the traditional color filter arrays (CFAs) found in nearly every digital camera. Standard CFAs rely on absorptive dyes—red, green, and blue pigments—to filter light. While effective, this process is inherently wasteful; each subpixel absorbs roughly two-thirds of the incoming visible light, effectively throwing away photons to achieve color selectivity. In contrast, CRs utilize nanostructured surfaces to sort light by wavelength, redirecting photons to their respective subpixels rather than absorbing them.

The primary appeal of CR technology is its ability to increase photon collection efficiency. Because these devices are non-absorptive, they can theoretically deliver nearly all incident photons to the detector plane. This advantage is most significant in "photon-starved" environments, such as low-light photography, where the sensor's signal is competing with read noise and shot noise. However, despite their theoretical promise, many CR designs have struggled to demonstrate their superiority in real-world imaging systems. This research addresses this gap by identifying two major flaws in conventional CR design: the use of idealized illumination models and the reliance on sub-optimal design objectives.

## The Realities of Camera Illumination

A central challenge in nanophotonics is that the performance of a structure often depends heavily on the angle and nature of the incident light. Most existing CR designs are optimized under the assumption of a single, normal plane wave—light arriving perfectly perpendicular to the sensor. In a real camera, this is never the case.

A camera lens delivers a converging cone of light to the sensor. This illumination is characterized by a finite numerical aperture (NA) and a chief-ray angle (CRA) that changes depending on where the pixel is located on the sensor. At the center of the sensor, the cone is symmetric, but as one moves toward the corners, the axis of the cone tilts significantly. For a typical mobile sensor, the CRA can reach $30^\circ$ or more. 

When a CR is optimized for a single normal plane wave, it effectively becomes specialized for an environment it will never encounter in a real camera. The researchers found that CRs optimized for plane waves lose a significant portion of their performance when re-evaluated under realistic "pupil ensemble" illumination. For example, a red-sorting structure might lose more than half of its routing contrast when the light transitions from a single wave to a $0.30$ NA cone. This discrepancy occurs because the interference effects that drive color sorting are highly sensitive to the angular distribution of the source.

## Information-Optimal Design and Linear Decoding

Beyond the illumination model, the second major contribution of this work is the re-evaluation of the design objective itself. Traditionally, CR designers have attempted to mimic the spectral responses of standard RGB filters. The goal was usually to make the red subpixel receive only red light, the green subpixel only green light, and so on.

However, the primary purpose of a camera is not to produce "raw" red, green, and blue measurements, but to reconstruct an accurate image of the scene (often represented in the CIE XYZ color space). The researchers argue that the four measurements produced by a $2 \times 2$ pixel supercell—labeled $q_1, q_2, q_3, q_4$—do not need to resemble traditional RGB channels. Instead, they should be treated as generalized spectral encoders.

The optimal design objective, therefore, is to maximize the "imaging information" $I_{img} \equiv I(Y; Z) \text{ bits/pixel}$, which measures the mutual information between the noisy raw measurements $Y$ and the reconstructed color image $Z$. By using this metric, the design process is no longer biased toward imitating existing filters. Instead, Maxwell's equations and a color prior work together to determine which set of four spectral responses carries the most information about the scene, especially under noisy conditions.

To implement this, the researchers integrated a closed-form linear decoder directly into the optimization loop. For every iteration of the nanostructure's geometry, the system calculates the optimal way to reconstruct color from the resulting spectral responses. This "co-design" of optics and reconstruction ensures that the physical structure is tailored for the specific digital processing that will follow.

## The Inverse Design Architecture

The inverse design process employs a differentiable electromagnetic solver based on Rigorous Coupled-Wave Analysis (RCWA). This allows the system to calculate how small changes in the nanostructure's shape affect the final imaging information.

*{fig:design-parameterization-and-optimization-flow}: The parameterization chain for the color router. The design starts as a latent field, which is filtered and projected to create a binary pattern of silicon nitride (SiN) on a substrate. The entire path from geometry to the final color error is differentiable.*

The structures consist of a single $600 \text{ nm}$ thick layer of silicon nitride (SiN) patterned with complex, free-form shapes. The geometry is parameterized using a latent field that is filtered and projected to ensure the resulting designs are binary and satisfy manufacturing constraints, such as minimum feature sizes. 

The illumination model is incorporated by calculating the response of the structure to a weighted ensemble of plane waves that represent the lens's exit pupil. The detected power in each subpixel, $P_b$, is computed as an incoherent aggregate:

$$
P_b = \sum_n w_n \left| h_n \right|^2 \otimes S_{x,y}
$$

Here, $w_n$ represents the pupil weights, which account for the $\cos^4(\theta_{CRA})$ relative illumination drop-off typical of camera lenses, and $S_{x,y}(k_x, k_y)$ is the spatial distribution of the scene. This ensemble-based approach ensures the design remains robust across the entire range of angles present in the converging light cone.

## Performance Comparisons and the Low-Light Regime

The study compared the optimized CRs against a matched CFA reference model that uses standard pigments and microlenses. The results reveal a clear performance "crossover" based on the light level.

In bright conditions, the standard CFA outperforms the CR. While the CR collects more total photons, its spectral responses are broader and have more overlap than the sharply defined bands of dye filters. This leads to a higher "color noise" when the raw data is decoded into an image. In bright light, where the sensor has plenty of photons, the cleaner spectral separation of the CFA is more valuable than the extra photons collected by the CR.

However, as the light level drops, the situation reverses. In low-light scenarios—specifically below a green-site $\text{SNR} = 10 \log_{10} (\mu^2 / \sigma^2)$ of $13.7 \text{ dB}$—the CR becomes superior. Because the CR delivers nearly $3\times$ as many photons to the detector, the reduction in shot noise and the improved signal-to-read-noise ratio outweigh the disadvantage of overlapping spectral bands. In these photon-starved regimes, the CR carries more imaging information and produces images with higher peak signal-to-noise ratios (PSNR) than a standard filter array.

## Managing Field Non-Uniformity through Symmetry

One of the practical hurdles for integrating CRs into sensors is the variation of the light cone across the sensor field. A design that works well at the center, where the light hits vertically, will perform poorly at the edges, where the light is tilted. 

To solve this, the researchers developed a deployment strategy that partitions the sensor into 48 regions based on their radial distance ($r$) and azimuthal angle ($\phi_{CRA}$). They leveraged the $D_4$ symmetry (the eight symmetries of a square) of the pixel grid to reduce the design problem. By optimizing just six unique masks, they could tile the entire sensor.

Each of the six masks is specifically refined for its location's chief-ray angle and orientation. This localized optimization significantly improves spatial uniformity, reducing the performance gap between the center and the corners of the sensor. Without this field-dependent design, the imaging information would drop drastically as one moves away from the optical axis.

## Future Outlook and Structural Constraints

The research identifies a specific physical bottleneck for current single-layer CR designs: "routing purity." This refers to how accurately the structure can place sorted light into the correct $1 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$ subpixel site. 

The study found that the converging cone of light causes a "walk-off" effect, where the interference patterns are laterally displaced. Because the required lateral shift for color separation is roughly $0.5 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$, even small displacements caused by the $0.30$ NA cone significantly reduce purity. While the CRs achieve a total throughput of $96\%$, their effective purity (the fraction of light landing in the intended subpixel) is only about $0.17$, compared to $0.50$ for a standard CFA.

To achieve parity with CFAs across all light levels, a CR would need to reach a purity of approximately $0.32$ while maintaining its high throughput. This finding suggests that future work should focus on multi-layer structures or higher-index materials that can achieve sharper spatial control of the routed light, potentially overcoming the walk-off limits imposed by the camera's illumination geometry.

By moving away from idealized physics and toward system-aware design, this work provides a rigorous framework for developing nanophotonic components that are actually optimized for the cameras of the future.
