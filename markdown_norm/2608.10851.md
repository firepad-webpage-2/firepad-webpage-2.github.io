## Introduction to Viscoelastic Drop Impact

The impact of liquid droplets on solid surfaces is a ubiquitous phenomenon observed in nature and utilized extensively in industrial processes. From the precise placement of ink in high-speed printers to the efficient distribution of pesticides on crop leaves, understanding how a drop spreads is critical for optimizing performance. For simple fluids like water or oil, known as Newtonian fluids, the dynamics are well-governed by a balance between the droplet's inertia, which drives spreading, and the resisting forces of viscosity and surface tension.

However, many practical fluids are not Newtonian. They often contain long-chain polymers that impart viscoelasticity—a combination of liquid-like flow and solid-like elastic properties. When these viscoelastic drops hit a surface, they exhibit striking behaviors that differ from their Newtonian counterparts. For instance, trace amounts of polymers can completely suppress the rebound or splashing of a drop, causing it to stick to a surface that it would otherwise bounce off.

![comparison-of-newtonian-and-viscoelastic-drop-impact](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10851v1/img-2.jpeg)
*{fig:comparison-of-newtonian-and-viscoelastic-drop-impact}: Time-series comparison of a water drop (top) and a viscoelastic PEO solution drop (bottom) impacting a surface. While the PEO solution significantly suppresses the late-stage retraction and rebound, the maximum spreading diameter $D_{max}$ achieved at approximately $3 \text{ ms}$ remains remarkably similar to that of water.*

A long-standing observation in this field, often referred to as a "paradox," is that while polymer additives dramatically change how a drop retracts after spreading, they seem to have almost no effect on the maximum spreading diameter itself. As shown in {fig:comparison-of-newtonian-and-viscoelastic-drop-impact}, the maximum extent of the liquid film is nearly identical for both water and a polymer solution, despite the polymer's significant influence on subsequent dynamics. This research investigates the physical mechanisms behind this observation, seeking to explain why elasticity appears "hidden" during the initial spreading phase of impact.

## The Spreading Paradox and Classical Rheology

In fluid mechanics, the behavior of a material is often characterized by dimensionless numbers that relate different physical timescales and forces. Two primary numbers used for viscoelastic fluids are the Weissenberg number $Wi$ and the Deborah number $De$. The Weissenberg number $Wi = \dot{\gamma} \tau$ relates the relaxation time of the polymer $\tau$ to the rate of deformation (shear rate) $\dot{\gamma}$. The Deborah number $De = \tau / \tau_{impact}$ compares the polymer's relaxation time to the characteristic duration of the impact event $\tau_{impact}$.

When $Wi \gg 1$ and $De \gg 1$, the polymers do not have enough time to relax during the deformation, and the fluid is expected to behave more like an elastic solid than a liquid. During the rapid impact of a drop, shear rates can exceed $10,000 \text{ s}^{-1}$, and impact times are measured in milliseconds. Under these extreme conditions, classical rheology predicts that the elastic resistance should be dominant, significantly restricting how far the drop can spread.

![classical-rheological-regimes](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10851v1/img-3.jpeg)
*{fig:classical-rheological-regimes}: Plot of experimental conditions in the $Wi$ vs. $De$ space. According to these parameters, nearly all tested viscoelastic drops fall deep within the "Elastic Regime," yet they continue to spread like inelastic Newtonian fluids.*

Despite these predictions, experiments consistently show that the maximum spreading ratio $\beta_{max} = \frac{D_{max}}{D_0}$ (where $D_0$ is the initial diameter) follows Newtonian scaling. This suggests that the available metrics, $Wi$ and $De$, while useful for steady flows, may not accurately capture the energetic trade-offs occurring during the transient, high-energy event of a drop impact.

## Isolating Elasticity: The Use of Boger Fluids

A major challenge in studying viscoelastic spreading is that most polymer solutions are shear-thinning; their viscosity decreases as the flow speed increases. This makes it difficult to determine if a change in spreading is caused by the polymer's elasticity or simply by the fact that the fluid has become "thinner" and easier to push.

To address this, researchers utilize Boger fluids. These are specialized viscoelastic solutions formulated to have a nearly constant viscosity even at high shear rates, thereby isolating the effects of elasticity. By using Boger fluids made of poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO) and polyacrylamide (PAAM) in water-glycerol mixtures, the study could systematically compare fluids with the same viscosity but different levels of elastic modulus $G$. The experiments covered a wide range of impact conditions, defined by the Weber number $We = \frac{\rho D_0 V_0^2}{\sigma}$ (inertia vs. surface tension), the Reynolds number $Re = \frac{\rho D_0 V_0}{\mu_0}$ (inertia vs. viscosity), and the Ohnesorge number $Oh = \frac{\mu_0}{\sqrt{\rho \sigma D_0}}$ (viscosity vs. inertio-capillary forces).

## An Energetic Criterion for Elasticity

The core contribution of this work is the development of a generalized energy balance that includes an elastic term. The total energy available at impact consists of the drop's kinetic energy and its surface energy. As the drop spreads, this energy is partitioned into new surface energy (due to the increased area of the liquid film), viscous dissipation (energy lost to internal friction), and elastic energy (energy stored in the stretched polymer chains).

The study models the maximum elastic energy storage by treating the spreading drop as a Hookean solid, which provides a conservative upper bound for the elastic resistance. By normalizing the energy balance, the researchers derived a new dimensionless criterion, $\Gamma$, which represents the ratio of elastic energy to viscous energy:

$$\Gamma \equiv \frac{\text{Elastic Energy}}{\text{Viscous Energy}} = \frac{8}{5\pi} \left( \frac{G D_0}{\mu_i V_0} \right) \frac{1}{\sqrt{E}}$$

In this expression, $G$ is the elastic modulus, $V_0$ is the impact velocity, $D_0$ is the drop diameter, $\mu_i$ is the apparent viscosity during impact, and $E = 1 + \frac{12}{We}$ represents the initial energy density. 

![the-gamma-criterion](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10851v1/img-1.jpeg)
*{fig:the-gamma-criterion}: Conceptual definition of the $\Gamma$ criterion. It quantifies the relative damping effect of elasticity compared to viscosity. The researchers hypothesize that elastic effects only become detectable when $\Gamma$ is large.*

Unlike $Wi$ and $De$, which focus on time scales, $\Gamma$ focuses on energy. It directly compares the capacity of the fluid to store energy elastically against the amount of energy it inevitably loses to viscous friction. This allows for a more direct prediction of whether the maximum spreading radius will be affected.

## Findings: Why Elasticity is Negligible

The experimental results confirmed that for all tested Boger and quasi-Boger fluids, the spreading data collapsed onto a single Newtonian master curve. By solving the energy balance, the maximum spreading ratio $\beta_{max}$ can be expressed as:

$$\beta_{max}(\Gamma) \approx \sqrt{We E} \left[ 1 + \left( \Lambda_{\nu} E^2 (1 + \Gamma) \right)^{1/5} \right]^{-1}$$

where $\Lambda_{\nu} = We^2 Oh$ is a viscous damping parameter. When $\Gamma = 0$, this formula recovers the behavior of a purely Newtonian fluid.

![data-collapse-on-the-newtonian-master-curve](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10851v1/img-4.jpeg)
*{fig:data-collapse-on-the-newtonian-master-curve}: Experimental spreading data for various fluids plotted against the Newtonian theoretical curve (black line). Boger fluids (blue squares) and dilute polymer solutions (red circles) follow the Newtonian prediction perfectly, indicating that $\Gamma$ is effectively zero for these impacts.*

The reason for this collapse is revealed when calculating the actual values of $\Gamma$ for these impacts. Even for highly elastic solutions, the values of $\Gamma$ were found to be much less than $1$ ($\Gamma \ll 1$). This means that the amount of energy the polymers can store is tiny compared to the energy being dissipated by the fluid's viscosity. The characteristic shear rate at the spreading edge, $\dot{\gamma}_s \approx \left( \frac{3 V_0}{2 D_0} \right) V \beta_{max}^2$, is so high that the resulting viscous stresses far exceed the structural elastic capacity of the polymer network.

The analysis shows that for elasticity to measurably reduce spreading (beyond a $10\%$ experimental error), $\Gamma$ would need to reach a value of approximately $1$. This would require a critical elastic modulus:

$$G_{critical} \approx 2 \mu_i \left( \frac{V_0}{D_0} \right)$$

For typical impacts, this $G_{critical}$ is significantly higher than the modulus provided by dilute or semi-dilute polymer solutions.

## The Role of Shear Thinning

If polymers don't reduce spreading through elasticity, why do some concentrated polymer solutions spread *more* than water? The study addressed this by examining shear-thinning fluids. In these cases, the high shear rates during impact significantly lower the fluid's effective viscosity $\mu_i$. 

![theoretical-detectability-limit](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10851v1/img-5.jpeg)
*{fig:theoretical-detectability-limit}: Theoretical prediction of how $\beta_{max}$ deviates from the Newtonian value as $\Gamma$ increases. The grey band represents the $10\%$ experimental noise floor. Elasticity only becomes visible in the yellow region where $\Gamma > 1$.*

As the concentration of polymers increases, the reduction in viscosity (thinning) happens faster than the increase in elastic modulus $G$. Consequently, the ratio $G/\mu_i$ does not grow large enough to make $\Gamma$ significant. Instead, the lower viscosity simply allows the drop to spread further, driven by the same inertial forces. The researchers quantified this using the impact Carreau number $Cu_i = \frac{\dot{\gamma}_s}{\dot{\gamma}_{rheo}^*}$, which indicates the degree of thinning during impact. For concentrated solutions, $Cu_i \gg 1$, confirming that the enhanced spreading is a purely viscous effect, not an elastic one.

## Conclusion and Significance

This research resolves the paradox of viscoelastic drop spreading by demonstrating that the process is energetically "inelastic." While polymers are capable of storing energy and exerting significant forces during the slow retraction phase, they are overwhelmed by viscous dissipation during the rapid, high-energy spreading phase. 

The introduction of the $\Gamma$ criterion provides a practical tool for researchers and engineers. It establishes a clear boundary for when elastic effects should be considered: unless the elastic modulus of the fluid is comparable to the viscous stress (defined by $G \sim \mu_i V_0/D_0$), the maximum spread will remain governed by Newtonian physics. This understanding simplifies the design of sprays and coatings, showing that spreading can be controlled primarily through viscosity and surface tension, while elasticity remains a tool reserved for controlling the subsequent stages of impact, such as rebound and retraction.
