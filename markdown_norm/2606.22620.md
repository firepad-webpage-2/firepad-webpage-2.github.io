## The Promise and Reality of Higher Education Expansion

The landscape of Indian higher education underwent a massive transformation following economic liberalization in the 1990s. Spurred by a 2002 Supreme Court ruling that facilitated private sector participation, the country saw a rapid proliferation of colleges. This expansion was frequently characterized as a democratizing force, aimed at providing upward social mobility for groups historically excluded from the upper tiers of the labor market. However, recent research suggests that the proliferation of degrees may have led to a phenomenon known as credential devaluation, where the economic value of a college degree declines as the supply of graduates increases.

In "Degrees of Devaluation: College Expansion and the Credential Trap in India," researcher Kaibalyapati Mishra investigates the distributional consequences of this expansion. The study moves beyond the average effects of education to ask a critical question: Who bears the cost when a degree loses its value? By focusing on the labor market outcomes for Scheduled Caste (SC) and Scheduled Tribe (ST) workers, the paper identifies a "double whammy." While all graduates face some degree of devaluation, disadvantaged workers face a significantly larger wage penalty in districts where the expansion was most intense.

## Theoretical Foundations: Signaling and Discrimination

To understand why educational expansion might harm certain groups more than others, the study relies on two central economic theories: signaling and statistical discrimination.

In the signaling model, a college degree is more than just a certificate of acquired skills; it serves as a signal to employers about a candidate's unobserved ability. If the higher education system expands rapidly—often through private institutions with varying quality standards—the informational value of that signal is diluted. When a degree is held by a larger and more heterogeneous pool of workers, employers find it harder to distinguish between high-ability and low-ability candidates based on the credential alone.

This leads to the second theoretical pillar: statistical discrimination. Employers, faced with a noisier signal, may revert to group-based stereotypes to estimate a worker's productivity. In the Indian context, social groups like SC/ST workers often lack the social capital, inherited professional networks, and "soft" signals (such as specific dialects or family backgrounds) that higher-caste workers use to supplement their resumes. For these disadvantaged workers, the formal degree is often the only credible signal they possess. When the signaling value of that degree is eroded by expansion, the "discount" applied by employers due to pre-existing biases is amplified. The paper argues that for SC/ST graduates, the college expansion creates a "credential trap" where the very tool intended to ensure parity becomes a source of increased wage disparity.

## Data and Empirical Design

The study utilizes two primary datasets to link educational expansion to individual labor market outcomes. Labor market data, including wages, education level, and social group, is sourced from eight rounds of the National Sample Survey (NSS) Employment–Unemployment Survey, spanning 1987 to 2011. This is combined with administrative data from the All India Survey on Higher Education (AISHE), which tracks the establishment dates and types of colleges across India.

The analysis focuses on six states—Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, and Karnataka—where data on college establishment years was sufficiently complete. The researcher constructed a district-level expansion intensity measure, denoted as $Z_{2d}$. This measure represents the ratio of colleges established during the peak expansion period (2004–2012) relative to the baseline stock of colleges established before 1995:

$$
Z_{2d} = \frac{\text{Colleges established 2004--2012 in district } d}{\max(\text{Colleges established before 1995 in district } d, 1)}
$$

To isolate the effect of expansion, the study employs a reduced-form multi-difference estimation strategy. The baseline model seeks to estimate how the log real wage, $w_{idt}$, of an individual $i$ in district $d$ at time $t$ is affected by being a graduate in a high-expansion district after the expansion period began. The specification is:

$$
\ln(w_{idt}) = \alpha + \beta_1 graduate_i + \beta_2 Z_{2d} + \beta_3 post2004_i + \beta_4 (graduate_i \cdot Z_{2d}) + \beta_5 (graduate_i \cdot post2004_i) + \beta_6 (Z_{2d} \cdot post2004_i) + \delta (graduate_i \cdot Z_{2d} \cdot post2004_i) + X'_{it} \gamma + \mu_s + \lambda_t + \epsilon_{idt}
$$

In this model, $graduate_i$ is an indicator for holding a degree, $post2004_i$ indicates cohorts graduating after the expansion accelerated, and $X'_{it}$ includes controls for age, gender, and rural residence. The fixed effects $\mu_s$ and $\lambda_t$ account for state-level and time-specific variations.

## The Double Whammy: Analyzing Credential Devaluation

The central finding of the research is the identification of the "double whammy" effect. This is estimated by adding a quadruple interaction term to the model, involving an indicator for $disadvantaged_i$ (SC/ST status). The extended specification is:

$$
\ln(w_{idt}) = \dots + \delta_1 (graduate_i \cdot Z_{2d} \cdot post2004_i) + \delta_2 (graduate_i \cdot Z_{2d} \cdot post2004_i \cdot disadvantaged_i) + \text{lower-order terms} + X'_{it} \gamma + \mu_s + \lambda_t + \epsilon_{idt}
$$

The coefficient $\delta_1$ represents the "base devaluation" faced by all graduates in high-expansion districts, while $\delta_2$ represents the *additional* penalty faced by SC/ST graduates. The empirical results show that $\delta_1 = -0.023$ and $\delta_2 = -0.086$, both statistically significant. 

For a non-disadvantaged graduate in a district with average expansion intensity ($Z_{2d} = 3.93$), this translates to a wage penalty of approximately 9.1% compared to graduates in low-expansion districts. However, for SC/ST graduates, the additional penalty $\delta_2$ is much steeper. At the same expansion mean, the total wage shortfall for SC/ST graduates reaches approximately 42.9% relative to non-disadvantaged graduates in low-expansion districts. Notably, the study finds no significant wage gap for SC/ST graduates in the pre-expansion period, suggesting that this inequality is a direct consequence of how the education system grew.

## Robustness and Placebo Tests

To ensure that these results are not driven by general economic trends in high-expansion districts, the researcher conducted several placebo tests. A key concern is whether the measured effect is truly about the *credential* or if high-expansion districts simply experienced lower overall wage growth. 

If the effect were driven by general labor demand, we would expect to see a similar wage penalty for non-graduates. However, the interaction term $Z_{2d} \cdot post2004_i$ was found to be statistically insignificant when tested on the sample of non-graduates. Furthermore, a pre-trend test on cohorts who graduated before 2004 showed no significant relationship between expansion intensity and the SC/ST wage gap, reinforcing the conclusion that the devaluation is a post-expansion phenomenon.

The researcher also tested the sensitivity of the results to the assumed graduation age (varying between 21 and 23 years) and found the coefficients remained stable. The most stringent placebo test—examining the interaction $Z_{2d} \cdot post2004_i \cdot disadvantaged_i$ among non-graduates—showed a small positive effect. This implies that the large negative effect found for graduates is specifically tied to the credential and is not a reflection of broader worsening conditions for SC/ST workers in those districts.

## Heterogeneity and Geographic Variation

The impact of credential devaluation is not uniform across all demographics or employment types. Heterogeneity analysis reveals that while base devaluation affects both men and women, the additional SC/ST penalty is particularly pronounced for male graduates. In the employment sector, the "double whammy" is clearly visible among salary earners, where formal credentials are the primary gatekeepers for hiring and promotion. In this sector, the additional SC/ST penalty coefficient was $-0.044$.

Geographically, the effects were strongest in states that experienced the most aggressive private-led expansion. In Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka, states known for a high density of private engineering and professional colleges, the SC/ST graduate penalty was notably high. For instance, in Andhra Pradesh, the additional penalty coefficient was $-0.127$. Conversely, in states like Bihar, where expansion was slower and more public-sector dominated, the estimates were less stable, suggesting that the mode of expansion (private vs. public) plays a role in the intensity of devaluation.

## Policy Implications and the Future of the Credential Trap

The research presents a challenge to the conventional wisdom that expanding access to higher education is a sufficient condition for social equity. The findings suggest that when expansion occurs without adequate regulatory oversight or quality control, it can inadvertently penalize the most vulnerable students. 

From a policy perspective, the study highlights the need for a shift in focus from "quantity" to "quality and legibility." If the signaling value of a degree is the primary mechanism of devaluation, then mechanisms to restore that value are essential. This could include:
*   **Standardized Assessments**: Implementing national or state-level exit exams that provide a clear, legible signal of ability independent of the specific college attended.
*   **Accreditation and Quality Assurance**: Strengthening the accreditation process for private institutions to ensure that a degree from a new college meets a recognized standard of quality.
*   **Targeted Affirmative Action**: Ensuring that SC/ST students are not just admitted to any college, but are given pathways into high-quality, high-prestige institutions where the signaling value of the degree remains robust.

As the Indian labor market continues to evolve, the "credential trap" remains a significant hurdle for social mobility. By documenting how the costs of educational expansion are distributed, this research provides a framework for understanding why simply opening more classroom doors may not be enough to close the wage gap. The persistent devaluation of degrees held by marginalized groups suggests that without structural interventions, the credential may continue to function more as a barrier than a bridge to economic opportunity.
