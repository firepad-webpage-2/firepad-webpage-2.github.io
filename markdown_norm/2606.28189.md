## Introduction to Surface-State Andreev Cavities

In the field of mesoscopic superconductivity, the proximity effect is a fundamental phenomenon where a non-superconducting material inherits superconducting properties when placed in electrical contact with a superconductor. This occurs because electron-hole pairs, known as Cooper pairs, can leak into the normal metal. At the interface, an electron from the normal metal can be reflected as a hole while a Cooper pair is created in the superconductor, a process called Andreev reflection.

When this occurs within a confined geometry, such as a small metallic island, the resulting quasiparticles undergo multiple reflections. These particles interfere constructively to form discrete energy levels known as Andreev bound states (ABS). An Andreev cavity is a system where these states are confined and their energies are determined by the geometry of the cavity, the scattering at its boundaries, and the phase difference between superconducting regions.

This work investigates extended two-dimensional (2D) Andreev cavities formed by copper (Cu) islands grown on a niobium (Nb) substrate. Unlike previous studies focusing on highly confined quantum dots or diffusive systems where scattering is frequent, this research explores the ballistic regime. In this regime, electrons and holes travel long distances without scattering, and their energy spectra are governed by the specific shapes of the islands and the phase accumulation along ballistic trajectories.

## Experimental System and Methodology

The researchers realized these cavities by depositing Cu onto an epitaxial Nb(110) film. Under specific annealing conditions, the Cu forms islands with a (111) crystal orientation. These islands host a two-dimensional surface state—a highly mobile layer of electrons confined to the top surface of the Cu. This surface state is proximitized by the underlying Nb, meaning it develops an effective superconducting gap $\Delta_{\text{eff}} \approx 0.92 \text{ meV}$.

To probe these states, the team used Scanning Tunneling Spectroscopy (STS) at a temperature of 400 mK. STS measures the differential conductance ($dI/dV$), which is directly proportional to the local density of states (LDOS). By moving the STM tip across the island, they could map how the superconducting spectrum changes with position and external magnetic fields.

The physical parameters of the Cu(111) surface state were determined to be an effective mass $m^* \approx 0.45 m_e$ and a Fermi velocity $v_F \approx 5.8 \times 10^5 \text{ m/s}$. These values, combined with the island geometry, define the available paths (trajectories) that the quasiparticles can take.

## Semiclassical Trajectory Model

To understand the complex spectra observed, the authors developed a semiclassical model. In this model, the local spectrum at any point on the island is treated as a sum of contributions from all possible straight-line trajectories (chords) that pass through that point and terminate at the island boundaries.

For a given trajectory $n$ of length $\ell_n$, the energy $E$ of an Andreev bound state is determined by the phase accumulation along that path. This accumulation has two components: a dynamical phase due to the travel time and a magnetic phase due to external fields. The resonance condition is given by:

$$\cos \left[ 2 \arccos \left( \frac{E}{\Delta_{\text{eff}}} \right) - \frac{2E\ell_n}{\hbar v_F} \right] = (1 - T_n) + T_n \cos(\delta\phi_n)$$

In this equation, $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant, $T_n$ represents the effective transparency of the trajectory (related to boundary scattering), and $\delta\phi_n$ is the gauge-invariant phase bias. This model allows the researchers to simulate the expected density of states by extracting the island shape from STM images and summing the contributions of thousands of trajectories.

## Geometry-Defined Energy Scales

One of the central findings is that the island's geometry directly dictates the characteristic energy of the superconducting excitations. At zero magnetic field, the lowest excitation energy $E_0$ is primarily determined by the longest available trajectories.

The researchers found a clear correlation: as the characteristic trajectory length $\ell$ increases, the zero-field energy $E_0$ decreases. This occurs because a longer path allows for a greater accumulation of dynamical phase, shifting the resonance to lower energies. This relationship was systematically verified across many islands of different sizes, confirming that the "size" of the superconducting gap in these islands is a geometrically tunable property.

## Magnetic-Field Control of the Spectrum

The application of an in-plane magnetic field $B$ introduces a phase gradient $\nabla \phi$ in the superconductor. This creates a phase difference $\delta\phi_n$ between the start and end points of a trajectory. The magnitude of this phase accumulation depends on the transverse width $w_n$ of the trajectory relative to the field direction:

$$|\delta\phi_n| = \frac{2\pi}{\Phi_0} |B| h_{\text{eff}} w_n$$

where $\Phi_0 = h/2e \approx 2.07 \times 10^{-15} \text{ Wb}$ is the magnetic flux quantum and $h_{\text{eff}} \approx 24 \text{ nm}$ is an effective vertical lever arm.

The experimental results show that the magnetic field required to collapse the superconducting gap—driving the lowest state to zero energy—scales inversely with the island's width ($B_0 \propto 1/w$). In elongated islands, this leads to a strong directional dependence. If the field is applied such that trajectories have a large transverse projection, the gap collapses at low fields. If the field is rotated by 90 degrees to align with the narrow dimension, the gap remains robust to much higher field strengths. This demonstrates that the magnetic response of the cavity is not an intrinsic material property but is "designed" by the cavity's shape and orientation.

## Spatial Variations and Vortex Textures

The researchers also explored how the spectra vary internally within a single large island. By performing line spectroscopy, they observed that the low-energy onset of the gap shifts locally as the tip moves from narrow to wide regions of the island. This confirms that the LDOS is truly a local probe of the ensemble of trajectories passing through a specific point.

Furthermore, the study investigated the impact of magnetic vortices. When an out-of-plane magnetic field is applied, vortices form in the Nb substrate. These vortices possess a $2\pi$ phase winding. The proximitized surface state in the Cu island "sees" this phase texture. 

As shown in {fig:figure-4-response-of-the-andreev-cavity-to-a-vortex-phase-texture-a-d-topography-and-vortex-imaging-b-c-experimental-and-simulated-spectral-line-cuts-across-a-vortex-f-g-local-spectra-at-the-vortex-center-showing-the-shift-of-spectral-weight-to-zero-energy}, the spectra measured near a vortex position show a characteristic X-shaped evolution and a surge in low-energy spectral weight. The trajectory model successfully reproduced these features by adding a vortex-induced phase term to the total phase accumulation. This indicates that the surface state does not just "image" the vortex core but instead integrates the complex phase information along the ballistic paths of the quasiparticles.

## Comparison with Diffusive Models

A significant part of the analysis involved comparing the experimental data to the diffusive Usadel equation, which is the standard tool for describing "dirty" superconductors where scattering is frequent. The researchers found that the diffusive model predicts a much smoother, featureless suppression of the gap.

In contrast, the experimental spectra exhibited sharp, discrete resonances and structured subgap features that were only captured by the ballistic trajectory-ensemble model. This provides strong evidence that the Cu(111) surface state allows for coherent propagation over hundreds of nanometers, placing these islands in the "clean" limit of the proximity effect.

## Significance and Outlook

This research establishes a new platform for engineering and studying phase-coherent quantum states. By identifying geometry-defined trajectories as a primary design principle, the work shows how to tailor the energy levels and magnetic sensitivity of superconducting hybrid systems.

The significance of these findings extends to several areas of quantum technology:
1.  **Josephson Devices:** The ability to control phase accumulation through geometry is critical for designing nonreciprocal devices and high-sensitivity SQUIDs.
2.  **Topological Quantum Computing:** Systems that rely on Majorana fermions often require precise control over the phase and energy of Andreev states. The Cu/Nb system provides a model for understanding how such states behave in extended geometries.
3.  **Quantum Billiards:** The islands function as Andreev billiards, where the transition from regular to chaotic dynamics can be explored by changing the island shape.

In summary, the authors have demonstrated that the local superconducting spectra in 2D surface-state cavities are a direct map of the underlying geometry and phase landscape. This allows for the rational design of superconducting properties through the manipulation of physical shape and magnetic fields.
