## The Concept of the Organizational Technology Ladder

Recent research by Gregor Schubert from UCLA Anderson investigates a fundamental shift in how firms evolve technologically. The study introduces the concept of an "organizational technology ladder," suggesting that the adoption of one technology is not an isolated event but a step that alters a firm’s capacity to embrace future innovations. Specifically, the paper examines how the mass transition to remote work during the COVID-19 pandemic served as a precursor to the rapid adoption of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI).

The central thesis is that technology adoption is sequential. When firms shifted to remote work in 2021 and 2022, they were forced to invest in digital infrastructure, cloud computing, and new management practices. These investments created a foundation—a "rung" on the ladder—that significantly lowered the costs and increased the benefits of adopting GenAI once tools like ChatGPT became widely available in late 2022. This framework suggests that the uneven adoption of AI across the economy is not merely a result of different task structures, but a consequence of firms' prior technological trajectories.

## Empirical Strategy and the IV Approach

To establish a causal link rather than a simple correlation, the study utilizes a massive dataset of U.S. job postings from Lightcast, covering the period from 2017 to 2024. The primary challenge in this analysis is endogeneity: "tech-savvy" firms might naturally adopt both remote work and GenAI because of their inherent organizational culture or forward-looking management, rather than one causing the other.

To isolate the causal effect of remote work, Schubert employs an instrumental variables (IV) strategy. The main instrument leverages exogenous variation in labor market competition. If a firm hires in a metropolitan statistical area (MSA) where other firms are aggressively offering remote work, that firm is pressured to offer remote options to remain competitive for talent, regardless of its own internal innovation preferences. The study interacts the average "teleworkability" of the MSAs where a firm hired pre-pandemic with the firm's own occupational teleworkability. 

The primary regression model used to estimate this effect is:

$$100 \times \text{GenAIJobShare}_i = \beta \text{RemoteWorkShare}_i + X_i \gamma + \epsilon_i$$

In this equation, $i$ represents a firm (or an occupation within a firm), $\beta$ is the coefficient of interest representing the effect of remote work, and $X_i$ is a vector of controls including industry fixed effects, pre-pandemic firm characteristics, and educational requirements. The dependent variable, $\text{GenAIJobShare}_i$, measures the percentage of job postings mentioning generative AI tools or skills.

## Quantifying the Link: From Remote Work to GenAI

The findings provide strong evidence for the technology ladder hypothesis. The causal estimates indicate that a 10 percentage point (pp) increase in a firm's remote hiring share during 2021-2022 led to a 0.4 pp increase in the share of its job postings requiring GenAI skills in 2023-2024.

*{fig:trends-in-generative-ai-job-posting-shares-categorized-by-the-intensity-of-remote-work}: This chart shows the dramatic divergence in GenAI hiring trends following the release of ChatGPT, with the most remote-intensive firms leading the adoption curve.*

This effect is observed not only across different firms but also within firms across different occupations. When comparing two occupations within the same company, the one with a higher remote work share is significantly more likely to see a surge in GenAI-related hiring. Furthermore, the study finds that remote work adoption increases the "extensive margin" of AI engagement—meaning it makes a firm substantially more likely to mention GenAI at all, even if it had no prior history of doing so.

Robustness checks confirm these results. The effect remains significant when excluding the technology sector, suggesting the ladder mechanism operates across the broader economy. Similar results were found using an alternative instrument based on pre-pandemic commuting times, which captures worker demand for remote work in high-traffic MSAs.

## Mechanisms of Enablement: Infrastructure and Skills

The paper identifies two primary channels through which remote work facilitates GenAI adoption: infrastructure complementarity and skill accumulation.

### Infrastructure Complementarity
The shift to remote work required firms to move processes to the cloud, implement digital workflow tools, and strengthen cybersecurity for distributed access. These investments are highly reusable for GenAI. For instance, a firm that already digitized its internal documentation to support remote staff has the "data readiness" required to fine-tune a Large Language Model (LLM) for internal use. 

Qualitative analysis of earnings call transcripts using LLMs corroborates this. Firms frequently discuss investments in cloud infrastructure and data management as necessary precursors that enabled their subsequent AI initiatives.

### Skill Accumulation and Organizational Adaptation
Remote work changed *who* firms hire and *how* they manage them. The research finds that remote work adoption caused firms to increase their hiring for:
*   **Technical Capabilities:** Data scientists, machine learning engineers, and developers who can implement AI tools.
*   **Managerial Roles:** Leaders capable of coordinating distributed teams, which often involves more structured, asynchronous communication—a style that fits well with AI-augmented workflows.
*   **Social and Decision-Making Skills:** As routine tasks are automated or moved remote, the value of high-level coordination and social intelligence increases.

These organizational changes represent "complementary assets." A firm with a robust technical team and managers experienced in digital coordination is better equipped to identify and integrate GenAI use cases than a firm that operates entirely in-person with traditional oversight.

## Organizational Frictions and the RTO Signal

An intriguing aspect of the study is the role of Return-to-Office (RTO) mandates. The author interprets RTO mandates as a proxy for firms experiencing high coordination frictions or perceived productivity losses in remote settings. 

Under the technology ladder framework, these firms have a higher incentive to automate. If remote work is perceived as "costly" due to communication overhead, GenAI can act as a technical fix—automating the very tasks that are difficult to manage remotely. The empirical data supports this: the causal effect of remote work on GenAI adoption is significantly larger in firms that issued RTO mandates. This suggests that for some organizations, AI adoption is a strategic adjustment to mitigate the downsides of distributed work.

## Significance and Economic Impact

The implications of the "organizational technology ladder" are profound for both firm strategy and economic policy. 

First, it suggests that technological progress is path-dependent. A firm's ability to compete in the AI era is dictated by its successful adaptation to the remote work shock years prior. This creates a risk of compounding inequality between firms; those that missed the "remote rung" of the ladder may find the "AI rung" too high to reach, leading to persistent productivity gaps.

Second, the study highlights that AI readiness is not just about the technology itself. It is about the organizational "plumbing"—the data infrastructure, the technical talent, and the managerial flexibility—that was installed during previous technological shifts.

Finally, for policymakers, the research indicates that interventions to promote innovation should not focus solely on the latest technology. Supporting foundational digital capabilities and organizational adaptation can have ripple effects, lowering the barriers to entry for multiple subsequent waves of technological change. The paper concludes that the transformation of the American workplace triggered by the pandemic was not just a temporary change in location, but a fundamental realignment of organizational capabilities that is now fueling the AI revolution.
