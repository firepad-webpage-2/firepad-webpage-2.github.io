## Understanding Nearfield Beamfocusing in Obstructed Environments

Radiative nearfield beamfocusing is a technique in wireless communications and sensing where an antenna array directs electromagnetic (EM) energy to a specific focal point in space, rather than just a general direction. As communication systems move toward higher frequencies, such as the millimeter-wave (mmWave) and sub-THz bands, the nearfield region—where the distance to the receiver is small relative to the array size—becomes increasingly relevant. However, real-world deployments are often cluttered with obstacles that block the direct line-of-sight (LoS) path between the transmitter and the target.

A significant challenge in this field is how to deliver energy into "occluded" regions—areas where an obstacle partially or fully blocks the transmitter. One widely discussed approach is the use of specialized beam shapes, such as Airy beams, which possess a characteristic "self-bending" property. These beams follow a curved trajectory, leading to suggestions that they could navigate around obstacles. This paper provides a physically consistent assessment of whether these elaborate and computationally expensive techniques are truly necessary, or if simpler strategies can perform just as well.

## The Physics of Nearfield Modeling

To evaluate beamforming strategies accurately, one must use a model that respects the fundamental laws of electromagnetics. Many theoretical studies use simplified "ray-tracing" or purely geometric models that ignore complex phenomena like diffraction—the bending of waves around corners. This paper utilizes a physically consistent EM nearfield model that captures the full interaction between the transmitter, the environment, and the obstacles.

The outgoing EM field at any point in space, defined by coordinates $(r, \theta, \varphi)$, is represented by a 6-dimensional phasor vector $a_N(r, \theta, \varphi)$, which includes the normalized electric and magnetic field components:
$$
a_N(r, \theta, \varphi) = [e_r, e_\theta, e_\varphi, \eta h_r, \eta h_\theta, \eta h_\varphi]^T \in \mathbb{C}^6
$$
The transmitter is modeled as a linear system where the relationship between the voltages applied to the $N$ antenna elements, $v_{Tx}$, and the resulting EM field is defined by a gain operator $G_{aNvTx}$:
$$
a_N = G_{aNvTx} v_{Tx}
$$
In this study, the gain matrix $G_{aNvTx}(r, \theta, \varphi) \in \mathbb{C}^{6 \times N}$ is extracted from high-fidelity full-wave simulations (using Ansys HFSS). This ensures that the scattering and diffraction caused by the obstacle are implicitly baked into the model, allowing for a realistic comparison of beamforming performance.

To compare different methods fairly, the authors use the power-normalized energy density $u_A$. This metric represents the energy density $u$ at the focal point relative to the total power available at the power amplifiers $P_A$:
$$
u_A = \frac{u}{P_A} = \frac{200}{\eta} \frac{\| G_{aNvTx} v_{Tx} \|^2_2}{\| v_{Tx} \|^2_2} \text{ in } \text{s/m}^3
$$
where $\eta$ is the free-space impedance. This normalization ensures that a strategy cannot "win" simply by using more power.

## Comparing Beamfocusing Strategies

The research compares four different strategies for calculating the antenna voltage vector $v_{Tx}$:

1.  **Naïve Strategy**: This approach assumes a free-space environment and ignores the obstacle entirely. It focuses the beam by adjusting the phase of each antenna element based on the geometric distance $d_i$ to the target. The voltage for the $i$-th element is:
    $$
    [v_{\text{naiveTx}}]_i = \exp(j 2 \pi d_i(r, \theta, \varphi) / \lambda) \text{ for } i = 1, \dots, N
    $$
2.  **Airy Strategy**: This strategy generates an Airy beam by applying a specific cubic phase profile across the array. The phase $\phi$ depends on the antenna position $x$, as well as tuning parameters for curvature $B$, focal length $F$, and steering angle $\alpha$:
    $$
    \phi(x; B, F, \alpha) = B(x - F \tan\alpha)^3 + \frac{2 \pi}{\lambda} x \sin\alpha
    $$
    Finding the best Airy beam $v_{\text{AiryTx}}$ requires a complex numerical search to maximize $u_A$ over these parameters.
3.  **Optimal Strategy**: This provides the theoretical upper limit of performance. By solving a generalized Rayleigh-quotient problem, the optimal weights $v_{\text{optTx}}$ are found directly from the gain matrix:
    $$
    v_{\text{optTx}} = \text{max-eigenvector} \{ G_{aNvTx}^H G_{aNvTx} \}
    $$
4.  **Line-of-Sight (LoS) Strategy**: A simpler modification proposed by the authors. It behaves like the naïve strategy but only activates antenna elements that have a clear, unblocked path to the target. If an element $i$ is blocked, its voltage is set to zero:
    $$
    [v_{\text{LoSTx}}]_i = 
    \begin{cases} 
    [v_{\text{naiveTx}}]_i & \text{if } i \in \mathcal{I}_{\text{LoS}} \\ 
    0 & \text{otherwise} 
    \end{cases}
    $$

## Performance in Partially Occluded Regions

In a partially occluded region, some antennas have a clear view of the target while others are blocked. The researchers found that in these zones, the simple **LoS strategy** is remarkably effective.

![partially-occluded-beam-shapes](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13350v1/target_partially_occluded.png)
*{fig:partially-occluded-beam-shapes}: Beam shapes for a target in the partially occluded region. (a) Optimal beam, (b) Naïve beam showing significant reflection off the obstacle, (c) Airy beam with a curved trajectory, and (d) the proposed LoS beam.*

As seen in {fig:partially-occluded-beam-shapes}, the LoS strategy (d) creates a beam shape that is nearly identical to the optimal solution (a). Quantitatively, the LoS strategy achieves near-optimal performance across the entire partially occluded zone. In contrast, the Airy beam (c), despite its characteristic curve, actually delivers less energy to the focal point than the simpler LoS approach. The naïve strategy (b) performs poorly because it continues to pump energy into blocked paths, leading to significant reflections and wasted power.

## The Reality of Full Occlusion

When the target is in the "shadow" of the obstacle (fully occluded), the only way EM energy can reach it is through diffraction—waves bending around the edges of the plate. The study shows that in these regions, the absolute energy density drops precipitously (by more than 20 dB), as illustrated in {fig:optimal-energy-density-map}.

![fully-occluded-beam-shapes](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13350v1/target_fully_occluded.png)
*{fig:fully-occluded-beam-shapes}: Beam shapes for a target in the fully occluded region. Note the absence of a distinct energy peak at the target (red cross) for all strategies due to the physical limits of diffraction.*

{fig:fully-occluded-beam-shapes} shows that none of the strategies, including the Airy beam, are able to create a strong focal point behind the obstacle. While the Airy beam can be tuned to approach the performance of the optimal beam in this region, the absolute amount of energy delivered remains extremely low. Furthermore, the LoS strategy fails here because it turns off all antennas, as none have a direct path.

## Complexity vs. Benefit: Is Elaborate Beamforming Worth It?

The core contribution of this work is the analysis of the trade-off between performance and computational cost. The authors argue that the complexity of tuning Airy beams is rarely justified:

*   **In Partially Occluded Regions**: The simple LoS strategy is "near-optimal" and easier to implement. It only requires knowing where the obstacle and the target are to determine which antennas to turn off.
*   **In Fully Occluded Regions**: To tune an Airy beam effectively to account for diffraction, one must already possess a physically consistent EM model of the environment. However, if such a model is available, one can calculate the **Optimal Strategy** directly using a closed-form eigenvector solution. Tuning the parameters of an Airy beam via grid search and gradient ascent is far more computationally intensive than simply calculating the optimal weights directly.

## Conclusion and Design Insights

This research provides a sobering look at the practical utility of specialized "bending" beams like Airy beams for obstacle avoidance in nearfield communications. By using a physically consistent model, the study demonstrates that:

1.  **Simple is often better**: In most partially obstructed scenarios, simply disabling blocked antennas (the LoS strategy) provides performance nearly identical to the theoretical maximum.
2.  **Physical limits dominate**: In fully occluded regions, the physics of diffraction limits how much energy can be delivered regardless of the beamforming complexity.
3.  **Model availability changes the game**: The high-fidelity models required to make Airy beams perform well are the same models that allow for the direct calculation of the optimal beam. Therefore, there is no practical reason to use a constrained beam shape like an Airy beam when the unconstrained optimal solution is easier to compute.

For engineers designing next-generation wireless systems, these results suggest that focusing on accurate environmental modeling and simple path selection may be more productive than pursuing increasingly complex beam-shaping algorithms. Future work will look into more complex obstacle geometries and wideband signals to see if these conclusions hold in even more challenging environments.
