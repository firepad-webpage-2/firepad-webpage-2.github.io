## The Challenge of Automated Fish Freshness Assessment

Ensuring the quality and safety of seafood is a critical concern for the global food industry. Traditionally, assessing the freshness of fish has relied on sensory evaluation by experts, microbiological tests, or chemical analyses. While these methods are established, they suffer from inherent drawbacks: they are often subjective, time-consuming, and destructive to the sample, making them impractical for high-speed, real-time industrial inspection. Hyperspectral Imaging (HSI) has emerged as a powerful non-destructive alternative, capable of capturing detailed biochemical information through light reflectance across hundreds of narrow spectral bands.

However, applying modern deep learning to HSI data in the food domain presents unique challenges. Most standard computer vision models, such as ResNet or Vision Transformers (ViT), are designed for natural images with three color channels (RGB) and prioritize spatial textures. In contrast, fish freshness is primarily reflected in subtle changes in spectral signatures rather than complex visual patterns. Furthermore, the labels for freshness are ordinal (e.g., Day 1 to Day 16), and the available datasets are often much smaller than the massive datasets used to train general-purpose models.

![sgnet-framework](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12227v1/framework.png)

To address these issues, the SGNet (Spectral-Grouped Network) architecture was developed. It is a lightweight, domain-aware convolutional neural network designed specifically to respect the characteristics of HSI data. By focusing on efficient spectral-spatial factorization and employing a hierarchical encoder, SGNet achieves high accuracy in predicting storage days with a significantly lower computational footprint than traditional deep learning backbones.

## Understanding Hyperspectral Data in the Food Domain

To appreciate the design of SGNet, one must understand the structure of the input data. A hyperspectral image is typically represented as a data cube $X \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$, where $C$ is the number of spectral bands, $H$ is the height, and $W$ is the width of the spatial image. In the case of the salmon dataset used in this research, $C=462$, meaning each pixel contains a detailed reflectance spectrum across 462 different wavelengths.

In remote sensing—the most common application for HSI—researchers often focus on identifying land-cover types (e.g., forest, water, urban) where spatial context is paramount. In food quality assessment, however, the "spectral dominance" principle applies. The chemical breakdown products that occur as fish spoils create specific spectral signatures. While spatial information helps identify the tissue structure, the most informative features for freshness are found in the relationships between these spectral bands.

Another critical factor is the ordinal nature of the task. Most classification models treat labels as independent categories. However, for a model predicting if a fish is on Day 5 or Day 6 of storage, an error of one day is much less severe than an error of ten days. The objective is to map the input cube to a label $y \in \{0, 1, \dots, K-1\}$, where $K=16$ for the salmon dataset, while maintaining a representation that reflects the continuous degradation process.

## The Architecture of SGNet

The SGNet architecture is built around a hierarchical encoder that progressively reduces spatial resolution while refining spectral features. The process begins with a "stem" consisting of two main parts:

1.  **Linear Spectral Projection:** The high-dimensional input $X$ (with 462 bands) is first compressed using a $1 \times 1$ pointwise convolution. This reduces the number of channels to an initial embedding dimension $C_0=64$. This step performs controlled spectral mixing, allowing the model to learn which combinations of wavelengths are most indicative of freshness before any spatial processing occurs.
2.  **Strided Patch Embedding:** Following projection, a $4 \times 4$ strided convolution with a stride of 4 is applied. This operation reduces the spatial dimensions (for example, from $H \times W$ to $H/4 \times W/4$) to create spatially efficient patches while preserving the spectral fidelity learned in the previous step.

Following the stem, the model uses three stages of SGNet blocks. Each stage increases the channel depth (from $64 \to 128 \to 256$) and decreases the spatial resolution ($56^2 \to 28^2 \to 14^2$). This hierarchical structure allows the model to capture features at multiple scales, from fine local tissue variations to broader physiological changes in the fillet.

## Spectral-Grouped Convolutional Blocks

The core innovation of SGNet lies within its internal blocks, which utilize a factorized approach to handle spectral and spatial information separately. This prevents the "entanglement" of unrelated features, which often leads to noise and overfitting in high-dimensional HSI data.

### Spectral Mixing Path
The spectral path uses a $1 \times 1$ grouped convolution with $g=8$ groups. Grouped convolutions divide the input channels into independent groups and perform convolutions within those groups. This is a deliberate design choice: by restricting cross-channel interactions to local subsets of the spectrum, the model avoids mixing unrelated wavelength responses too early, which helps suppress spectral noise. This is followed by a two-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with an expansion ratio of 2 and GELU activation to further refine the spectral features.

### Spatial Mixing Path
Parallel to the spectral processing, the model captures local spatial context. It uses a depthwise separable convolution strategy, specifically a depthwise convolution with a large $7 \times 7$ kernel. Depthwise convolution applies a single filter per channel, which captures spatial patterns (like the texture of the fish muscle) without mixing information across different spectral bands.

### Dual Attention Gating
To ensure the model focuses on the most informative parts of the data, a dual attention mechanism is integrated into each block. This consists of:
*   **Channel Attention:** A Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) block that recalibrates channel importance. It uses global average pooling followed by a bottleneck MLP with a reduction ratio $r=4$ to determine which spectral features are most relevant.
*   **Spatial Attention:** A lightweight $1 \times 1$ convolution that generates a gating map to highlight informative spatial regions in the fillet.

The output of these paths is combined and passed through a Feed-Forward Network (FFN) with a residual connection, ensuring stable gradient flow during training.

## Experimental Results and Benchmarking

The effectiveness of SGNet was evaluated on a newly curated dataset of salmon fillets stored over 16 days. The model achieved a Top-1 accuracy of 97.78% and a Macro F1 score of 98.00%. More importantly for freshness assessment, the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) was only 0.64 days, meaning the model's predictions were typically within 15 hours of the actual storage time.

When compared to established architectures, SGNet demonstrated superior efficiency. While heavy models like ResNet-50 or ViT-B/16 have 25.6M and 86.0M parameters respectively, SGNet achieves better performance with only 4.75M parameters. In industrial settings, this efficiency is vital; SGNet can process 420 images per second on standard hardware, comfortably meeting the requirements for real-time inspection.

The research also included an ablation study to verify the importance of each component. For instance, removing the grouped convolution (setting $g=1$) or the spatial path led to a significant drop in accuracy and an increase in ordinal error, confirming that the factorized, domain-aware design is essential for handling HSI data.

## The Importance of Data Curation

A significant contribution of this work is the development and release of a curated hyperspectral dataset. In many previous studies, data leakage—where samples from the same fish appear in both training and testing sets—has led to over-optimistic results. The salmon dataset used here was split at the "pack level," ensuring that the model is tested on entirely unseen fish samples.

Analysis of the errors showed that the model is extremely accurate during the commercially relevant period (Day 1 to Day 10). Misclassifications mostly occurred in the very late stages of spoilage (Days 15 and 16), long after the fish would have been considered unfit for consumption. This suggests that the spectral signatures of advanced spoilage become highly similar, a known biological phenomenon where the rate of chemical change plateaus.

## Conclusion and Industrial Implications

The development of SGNet demonstrates that deep learning models for food quality do not necessarily need to be "deep" or "heavy" in terms of parameters. Instead, they need to be "smart" about the data modality they process. By using spectral-grouped convolutions and a lightweight dual attention mechanism, SGNet provides a robust solution for non-destructive fish freshness classification.

The implications for the seafood industry are significant. This technology enables:
*   **Rapid Quality Control:** Automated systems can replace slow, manual sensory inspections.
*   **Waste Reduction:** Accurate shelf-life prediction allows for better inventory management and reduced food waste.
*   **Consistency:** Unlike human experts, an HSI-based system provides objective, repeatable measurements across different batches and locations.

While the current study focused on salmon, the architectural principles of SGNet—spectral-spatial factorization and hierarchical encoding—are broadly applicable to other food matrices. Future work may explore the integration of explicit ordinal loss functions to further refine performance in the late-stage spoilage window and the adaptation of the model to other species of fish and meat products.
