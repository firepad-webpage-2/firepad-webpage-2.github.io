## The Twin Transition and Critical Raw Materials

The global economy is currently navigating a "twin transition": a simultaneous shift toward green energy and digital integration. This structural transformation relies heavily on specific raw materials, particularly cobalt, copper, and nickel. These metals are indispensable for manufacturing electric vehicle (EV) batteries, solar photovoltaic (PV) panels, and high-performance electronic devices. As the adoption of these technologies accelerates, the demand for these "critical minerals" has surged, making their market dynamics a central concern for policymakers and economists.

However, monitoring these markets in real-time is challenging. Official data on metal production and consumption are typically published with significant delays and often only at an annual frequency. This data lag makes it difficult to understand the immediate drivers of price volatility. To address this, recent research has turned to "big data" as a proxy for economic activity. By analyzing what people search for online, researchers can gain a more immediate view of consumer intentions and industrial demand.

## Bridging the Information Gap with Big Data

A central contribution of this work is the development of a monthly monitoring tool called the Derived Demand Index (DDI). The concept of "derived demand" refers to the demand for an intermediate good—like copper—that arises from the demand for the final products that use it—like smartphones or electric cars. If more people are searching for "electric vehicle charging stations" or "best laptops for work," it likely signals an upcoming increase in the demand for the raw materials required to build those products.

The researchers leveraged Google Trends data from 2004 to 2023 to capture these signals. Unlike traditional economic reports that look backward, web search data provides a forward-looking glimpse into consumer and investor attention. By focusing on queries related to EVs, solar energy, and smart devices, the DDIs serve as high-frequency proxies for the demand specifically generated by the energy and digital transitions.

## Building Derived Demand Indexes (DDIs)

Creating a reliable index from millions of search queries requires a rigorous statistical approach. The authors began by identifying "primitive queries" like "solar panels" or "electric cars." They then expanded this list to include over 100 related terms suggested by Google. To ensure the data was manageable and focused, they used a technique called Targeted Principal Component Analysis (TPCA).

In the first step, an Elastic-Net selection process (using a penalty parameter $\alpha = 0.5$) filtered the most relevant search terms for each metal. This ensures that the terms included in the index are statistically linked to actual price movements. In the second step, the researchers extracted the "first principal component" from these selected terms—essentially a single numerical series that captures the most common variation across all the relevant search queries.

Before building the index, the raw Google Trends data $GT_{i,t}$ was transformed to handle volatility and seasonality. The transformation used was:

$$gt_{i,t} = \ln(1 + GT_{i,t})$$

This methodology allows for three distinct indexes—$DDI_{CO,t}$ for cobalt, $DDI_{CU,t}$ for copper, and $DDI_{NI,t}$ for nickel—each reflecting the specific technological demand profile of that metal.

## Modeling Global Metal Markets with SVAR

To understand how different economic forces affect metal prices, the researchers integrated their DDIs into a Structural Vector Autoregressive (SVAR) model. A VAR model is a statistical framework used to capture the linear interdependencies among multiple time series. By making the model "structural," the authors can identify specific economic "shocks"—unanticipated events like a sudden mining strike or a surprise jump in EV sales.

The model for each metal $m$ is expressed as:

$$A_{m,0} y_{m,t} = c_m + \sum_{j=1}^{p} A_{m,j} y_{m,t-j} + \epsilon_{m,t}$$

In this equation, $y_{m,t}$ represents a vector of four key variables:
1.  $\Delta prod_{m,t}$: The growth rate of global metal ore production (supply side).
2.  $\Delta WIP_t$: The growth rate of World Industrial Production (general economic activity).
3.  $p_{m,t}$: The real price of the metal (inflation-adjusted).
4.  $DDI_{m,t}$: The newly created index capturing transition-specific demand.

The term $\epsilon_{m,t}$ represents the structural shocks, which are assumed to be independent and normally distributed: $\epsilon_{m,t} \sim N(0, I_n)$.

## Identifying Economic Shocks

The researchers distinguished between four types of shocks that drive metal prices. They used a combination of "sign restrictions" (assuming a variable moves in a certain direction) and "magnitude restrictions" to isolate these effects:

1.  **Supply Shocks:** These occur when mining output unexpectedly drops (e.g., due to geopolitical conflict or strikes), leading to higher prices and lower industrial output.
2.  **Aggregate Demand (AD) Shocks:** These are broad economic booms or busts. When the global economy grows, demand for everything—including metals—rises, pushing prices up.
3.  **Transition Demand (TD) Shocks:** This is the most critical identification in the paper. These shocks represent a surge in metal demand specifically caused by the adoption of green and digital technologies, captured by a rise in the DDI.
4.  **Metal-specific Demand (MD) Shocks:** These are residual shocks, such as precautionary stockpiling or changes in financial speculation, that aren't captured by general economic growth or the transition index.

To differentiate between a general economic boom (AD) and a transition-specific surge (TD), the researchers imposed a magnitude restriction: an AD shock must have a larger immediate impact on World Industrial Production ($\Delta WIP_t$) than a TD shock does. This prevents the model from confusing a global recovery with a specific green-tech boom.

## Empirical Findings: The Rise of Transition Demand

The analysis revealed that different shocks have very different "lifespans" in the market. Aggregate demand shocks tend to hit prices hard and fast, reflecting the immediate impact of the global business cycle. Supply shocks also have a significant immediate impact but often fade as producers adjust.

The Transition Demand (TD) shocks, however, display a unique pattern. For copper and nickel, the price response to a TD shock is persistent—it builds over several months and decays very slowly. This suggests that the transition toward green energy creates a long-lasting "demand pull" that keeps prices elevated for an extended period.

For copper, the data shows a significant shift over time. By breaking the sample into two periods (before and after June 2015), the researchers found that TD shocks have become much more influential. In the latter period, transition-related demand became the dominant driver of copper price fluctuations, explaining nearly 60% of price variance over long horizons.

## Historical Context and Policy Implications

The value of this model is best seen when examining historical price surges. The researchers looked at two specific periods: the recovery following the 2008 financial crisis and the price spike after the COVID-19 pandemic.

![historical-decomposition-of-metal-prices](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27842v1/x7.png)
*{fig:historical-decomposition-of-metal-prices}: Historical decomposition showing which shocks contributed to price changes during two key periods. The orange bars represent Transition Demand (TD) shocks.*

In 2009, metal prices recovered primarily because the whole global economy was bouncing back (Aggregate Demand). However, in the post-pandemic period (2021-2022), the story changed. While general demand was still a factor, Transition Demand (the orange bars in {fig:historical-decomposition-of-metal-prices}) played a much larger role, particularly for copper. In fact, for copper, TD shocks were the single most important contributor to the price surge in early 2022, reflecting the massive acceleration in EV sales and renewable energy investment during that time.

These findings have significant implications for both businesses and governments. By showing that a large portion of price volatility is now driven by the "twin transition" rather than just general economic growth, the research highlights the need for specialized mineral policies. Standard macroeconomic tools might not be enough to manage the inflationary pressures coming from the green transition. Instead, policies focused on securing mineral supply chains and improving recycling technologies will be essential to ensure that the transition to a sustainable economy remains affordable and stable.
