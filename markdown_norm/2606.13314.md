## Understanding AI Exposure through the Lens of Social Stratification

Recent global research on artificial intelligence (AI) has focused extensively on "exposure"—the extent to which the tasks of a specific occupation can be performed or assisted by AI models. In developed economies, this exposure is often seen as a double-edged sword: a risk of displacement for routine clerical work, but a potential for productivity augmentation in high-skill professional roles. However, in the context of a developing economy like India, the distribution of this exposure is not merely a matter of job titles; it is deeply intertwined with historical social hierarchies, specifically the caste system.

The research paper "The Privilege of Exposure" by Kaibalyapati Mishra shifts the focus from national-level statistics to the internal social dynamics of India’s graduate labor market. It explores how generative AI exposure is distributed across different social groups, including Scheduled Tribes ($ST$), Scheduled Castes ($SC$), Other Backward Classes ($OBC$), and the historically advantaged "Others" group. The central finding is that AI exposure in India is a "privilege gradient": those in the most advantaged social positions are also those most positioned to benefit from the productivity gains offered by generative AI.

## Methodology and Data Sources

To analyze these patterns, the study utilizes the 2025 Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) from India. The analysis focuses on a subsample of 82,830 employed graduates (those with a degree or higher), as generative AI typically interacts with the cognitive and linguistic tasks prevalent in graduate-level employment.

The researcher maps three distinct AI exposure indices to the National Classification of Occupations ($NCO_{2015}$) at the 3-digit level, covering 130 occupational groups:

1.  **AI Occupational Exposure (AIOE):** An ability-based measure derived from linking AI capabilities to the human abilities required for specific occupations.
2.  **GPT Exposure:** A task-based measure that calculates the proportion of an occupation's tasks where a Large Language Model (LLM) can reduce completion time by half.
3.  **ILO GenAI Score:** A generative AI exposure index based on International Labour Organization (ILO) task descriptions, distinguishing between potential for automation and potential for augmentation.

All indices are standardized into $z$-scores with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1 across the occupational groups to allow for direct comparison. This standardization means a score of 0.5 indicates an occupation is half a standard deviation more exposed than the average occupation in the dataset.

## The Privilege Gradient in AI Exposure

The core empirical result of the study is the identification of a monotonic increase in AI exposure corresponding to caste advantage. Among employed graduates, the "Others" category (predominantly upper-caste) shows an exposure score approximately 0.50 to 0.57 standard deviations above the occupational mean. In contrast, $SC$ graduates are close to the mean (around 0.08), and $ST$ graduates often fall below it.

To isolate the specific effect of caste, the author employs an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression model:

$$E_{ij} = \alpha + \beta_1 SC_i + \beta_2 ST_i + \beta_3 OBC_i + \gamma X_i + \delta_d + \epsilon_{ij}$$

In this equation, $E_{ij}$ represents the occupational AI exposure for individual $i$ in occupation $j$. The coefficients $\beta_1, \beta_2, \text{ and } \beta_3$ capture the exposure gaps relative to the "Others" group. The model also controls for $X_i$ (a vector of demographic variables like age and sex) and $\delta_d$ (district fixed effects) to ensure that the gaps are not simply a result of geographical or age differences.

The results are stark: even when comparing graduates in the same district and of the same age and sex, $SC$ graduates are 0.24 to 0.27 standard deviations less exposed than upper-caste graduates. For $ST$ graduates, the gap is even wider, ranging from 0.31 to 0.37 standard deviations. These gaps signify that the "exposure" to new technology is not being distributed equitably, even among those who have achieved the same level of formal education.

## Mechanisms: Relegation and Sorting

The study identifies two primary institutional channels that drive these exposure gaps: occupational relegation and occupational sorting.

### Occupational Relegation
Occupational relegation refers to the phenomenon where graduates from disadvantaged groups are forced into manual or elementary roles that do not utilize their degrees. The research finds that 24.6% of $SC$ graduates and 32.0% of $ST$ graduates are employed in farm or elementary occupations—such as construction labor or agricultural work. These roles have near-zero AI exposure. For these individuals, their degree has undergone "credential devaluation," and they are effectively insulated from both the risks and the opportunities of generative AI because they are excluded from the white-collar workforce entirely.

### Occupational Sorting
Even when the analysis is restricted to white-collar professionals, a significant gap remains due to "sorting." Upper-caste graduates are heavily concentrated in the most highly exposed occupations, such as software development, finance, and senior management. For instance, while 5.3% of upper-caste graduates are software developers, only 2.1% of $SC$ graduates hold such positions. Conversely, disadvantaged groups are more likely to be found in teaching or public administration roles that, while professional, have lower generative AI exposure scores according to current task-based assessments.

## The Economic Implications: A Wage Premium for Exposure

Understanding who is exposed to AI is critical because exposure is strongly correlated with earnings. The researcher estimates the wage premium associated with AI-exposed occupations using the following regression:

$$\ln(W_i) = \alpha + \theta E_{ij} + \gamma X_i + \delta_d + \epsilon_i$$

Here, $W_i$ represents monthly earnings, and $\theta$ measures the association between a one-standard-deviation increase in exposure and the change in log earnings. 

The study finds that for regular salaried graduates, a one-standard-deviation increase in AIOE (the ability-based index) is associated with a 20.0 log point (approximately 22%) increase in monthly earnings. This suggests that the market currently values the specific high-level cognitive abilities that are most augmentable by generative AI. 

However, the relationship is not always linear. Using the ILO automation score, the researcher finds an inverted U-shaped relationship. Earnings rise with exposure up to a certain point, but then decline for the most "automatable" clerical roles (like keyboard operators). This suggests that while "augmentable" exposure (where AI assists high-level decision-making) is rewarded, "automatable" exposure (where AI might replace routine tasks) does not carry the same premium.

## Heterogeneity Across Gender and Age

The research also explores how these gaps vary by demographic subgroups:

*   **Gender:** The caste gradient exists for both men and women, though it is slightly larger among men. Interestingly, women as a whole tend to be less exposed than men because they are concentrated in professions like teaching and healthcare, which currently receive lower AI exposure scores.
*   **Age:** The $SC$ and $OBC$ exposure gaps are widest among young labor-market entrants (ages 15–30). This is a concerning finding, as it suggests that despite the expansion of higher education, the most recent graduates from disadvantaged backgrounds are still entering the workforce in less AI-exposed (and thus potentially less productive or lower-paying) roles compared to their upper-caste peers.
*   **Public vs. Private Sector:** The caste gap is significantly narrower in the public sector. This is likely due to the reservation policies (affirmative action) in government jobs, which ensure a more equitable distribution of occupational roles, although public sector jobs themselves tend to have lower average AI exposure than private-sector high-tech roles.

## Significance of the Findings

The paper concludes that generative AI is poised to interact with India’s graduate labor market in a way that may widen existing social inequalities. In many developed nations, "exposure" is discussed as a risk of job loss. In India, however, exposure is better understood as a marker of opportunity. Because the most exposed jobs are also the highest-paying and most prestigious, the fact that these roles are dominated by upper-caste graduates means they are the primary beneficiaries of the technological "shocks" provided by generative AI.

The "privilege of exposure" implies that the benefits of technological advancement are likely to accrue to those already at the top of the social hierarchy. Without proactive measures to ensure that graduates from Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Other Backward Classes have equal access to the specific high-tech, managerial, and financial roles that are most augmentable by AI, the digital divide may further entrench the historical caste divide in the Indian economy.

The study provides a quantitative foundation for future policy discussions, suggesting that simply increasing the number of graduates is insufficient. Instead, the focus must shift to the *quality* of occupational attainment and the removal of barriers that prevent disadvantaged groups from entering the high-value, AI-relevant sectors of the modern economy.
