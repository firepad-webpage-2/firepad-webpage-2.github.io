## Advancing Quantitative Myocardial Perfusion MRI

Quantitative myocardial perfusion cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging is an essential diagnostic tool for assessing cardiovascular diseases, such as myocardial ischemia and microvascular dysfunction. The goal of this technique is to move beyond qualitative visual assessment—where a clinician looks for areas of low contrast—to provide absolute measurements of myocardial blood flow (MBF) in units such as mL/min/g. By quantifying how much blood reaches the heart muscle, clinicians can more accurately diagnose multi-vessel disease and monitor treatment efficacy.

Despite its clinical value, quantification is technically demanding. It involves dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) MRI, where a gadolinium-based contrast agent is injected, and its passage through the heart is tracked over time. The standard approach uses a tracer-kinetic model to relate the measured MRI signal to physiological parameters. However, conventional methods like non-linear least squares (NLLS) fitting are highly sensitive to image noise, patient motion, and artifacts from undersampled data. Furthermore, NLLS treats every pixel (voxel) in the image independently, ignoring the natural spatial consistency of the heart tissue. This work introduces a Spatiotemporal Physics-Informed Neural Network (ST-PINN) that leverages Implicit Neural Representations (INRs) to model these dynamics as continuous functions, providing a more robust and spatially coherent way to quantify heart health.

## The Two-Compartment Exchange Model

The foundation of perfusion quantification lies in tracer-kinetic modeling. This study utilizes the two-compartment exchange model (2CXM), which mathematically describes how contrast agent moves between the blood plasma and the extravascular extracellular space (the interstitial fluid) of the heart muscle.

In the 2CXM, the myocardial tissue concentration at time $t$, denoted as $C_{MYO}(t)$, is the weighted sum of concentrations in the plasma compartment ($C_p$) and the interstitial compartment ($C_e$):

$$C_{MYO}(t) = v_p C_p(t) + v_e C_e(t)$$

where $v_p$ and $v_e$ represent the fractional volumes of the plasma and interstitial spaces, respectively. For clinical use, we often solve this system using an analytical convolution. This relates the measured tissue concentration to the Arterial Input Function ($C_{AIF}$), which is the concentration of the contrast agent in the blood entering the heart:

$$C_{MYO}(t) = F_p \cdot R(t) * C_{AIF}(t)$$

In this equation, $F_p$ is the plasma flow (related to blood flow), and $R(t)$ is the impulse response function, which depends on four kinetic parameters: $\eta = \{F_p, v_p, v_e, PS\}$, where $PS$ is the permeability–surface area product characterizing the exchange between compartments. Traditional quantification methods try to find the set of parameters $\eta$ that best fits the observed MRI data at each voxel.

## Moving Beyond Voxelwise Analysis with INRs

A major limitation of traditional NLLS and previous deep learning attempts in this field is "voxelwise" processing. These methods treat each point in the heart independently. If one voxel is corrupted by noise, the resulting blood flow estimate for that specific spot will be incorrect, leading to "salt-and-pepper" noise in the final flow maps.

To solve this, the researchers employ Implicit Neural Representations (INRs). Unlike standard neural networks that take an image and output a classification, an INR is a function $f$ that maps coordinates (like $x, y, t$) directly to a signal value (like concentration). Because the neural network is a continuous function, the values it predicts for adjacent coordinates are naturally related, providing "implicit spatial regularization."

Furthermore, the authors use a specific type of INR called a Sinusoidal Representation Network (SIREN). Most neural networks use activation functions like ReLU or `tanh`, which struggle to capture fine, high-frequency details—a problem known as "spectral bias." SIRENs use sine waves as activation functions, which are much better at representing the sharp changes seen when contrast agent first enters the heart (the "wash-in" phase).

## The ST-PINN Architecture

The ST-PINN framework consists of three specialized SIREN networks that are trained simultaneously to represent the different components of the perfusion process:

1.  **AIF Network ($f_{AIF}(t; \theta_{AIF})$):** This network models the contrast agent concentration in the blood pool over time. Since the blood pool signal is relatively smooth, it uses lower frequency settings.
2.  **Myocardium Signal Network ($f_{MYO}(x, t; \theta_{MYO})$):** This network represents the spatiotemporal concentration of the heart muscle. It takes both spatial coordinates $(x, y)$ and time $(t)$ as inputs. It uses high-frequency SIREN settings to capture sharp temporal peaks and spatial boundaries.
3.  **Parameter Network ($f_{\eta}(x; \theta_{\eta})$):** This is the most critical innovation. Instead of storing flow values in a grid, this network learns a continuous mapping from spatial coordinates $(x, y)$ to the four kinetic parameters $\eta = \{F_p, v_p, v_e, PS\}$. Because this network is shared across the whole heart, it ensures that the estimated flow maps are spatially smooth and physically plausible.

These three networks are coupled together during training by a physics-informed loss function. The loss function consists of two parts:
- **Data Loss:** Ensures the AIF and Myocardium networks accurately match the observed MRI data.
- **Physics Loss:** Enforces the 2CXM equation. It penalizes the model if the myocardium signal predicted by the signal network doesn't match the result of the physical convolution between the predicted AIF and the parameters from the parameter network.

The total loss $L$ is a weighted combination of these terms:

$$L = \lambda_{AIF} L_{AIF} + \lambda_{MYO} L_{MYO} + \lambda_{phys} L_{phys}$$

## Evaluating Performance in Realistic Environments

The researchers evaluated their ST-PINN framework using two sophisticated simulation setups. The first was a Digital Reference Object (DRO) containing 144 different combinations of tissue properties. The second, and more realistic, was a Bloch-equation simulation (CMRsim) that incorporated cardiac and respiratory motion, as well as magnetic resonance artifacts from undersampling.

![comparison-of-estimated-parameter-maps-f-p-and-v-e-between-ground-truth-gt-the-proposed-st-pinn-and-conventional-nlls-fitting-the-left-panel-shows-a-healthy-case-and-the-right-panel-shows-an-ischemic-case-with-a-perfusion-deficit-in-the-anterolateral-wall](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11282v1/results_figure.png)

In the DRO tests, ST-PINN outperformed NLLS by a significant margin. It achieved a Normalized Mean Square Error (NMSE) of 0.09 compared to 0.17 for NLLS. More importantly, it achieved much higher Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) scores (0.63 vs 0.47), indicating that the resulting maps looked more like real heart tissue and less like noisy artifacts.

In the more challenging CMRsim dataset, which mimics real clinical conditions, the benefits of ST-PINN were even more apparent. While NLLS produced highly noisy maps where it was difficult to distinguish between healthy and diseased tissue, ST-PINN produced smooth, clear maps. It correctly identified the "ischemic" regions (areas of low blood flow) and maintained high accuracy despite the presence of motion and image noise. For example, the median NMSE for ST-PINN in these realistic simulations was 0.212, whereas NLLS jumped to 0.884, indicating a failure to provide reliable quantitative values.

## Significance and Clinical Outlook

This work represents a shift in how medical image quantification can be approached. By moving from independent voxel fitting to continuous spatiotemporal modeling, the ST-PINN framework addresses the long-standing problem of noise and artifact sensitivity in CMR perfusion.

The key contributions of this research are:
- **Spatial Regularization:** By using a neural network to represent the parameter maps, the model learns that neighboring areas of the heart should have similar blood flow, preventing the noisy "speckle" common in current methods.
- **Physics Integration:** The model doesn't just "guess" values; it is strictly bound by the laws of tracer kinetics, ensuring the results are physiologically valid.
- **High Fidelity:** The use of SIREN architectures allows the model to capture the rapid dynamics of contrast passage that standard neural networks often "blur" out.

While currently validated in high-fidelity simulations, the ability of ST-PINN to handle motion and undersampled data suggests it could be a powerful tool for analyzing clinical data from patients who find it difficult to hold their breath or stay still during an MRI scan. Future work will involve testing this framework on large-scale patient datasets to confirm its diagnostic utility in real-world clinical workflows.
