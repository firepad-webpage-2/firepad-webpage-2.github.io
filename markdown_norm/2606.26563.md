## Addressing the Long-Horizon Challenge in Single-Cell Biology

The application of Artificial Intelligence (AI) to scientific discovery has historically focused on narrow, well-defined tasks. In the domain of single-cell biology, this often translates to benchmarks that measure performance on isolated steps, such as classifying a cell type or identifying differentially expressed genes. While these benchmarks are useful for tool development, they fail to capture the "long-horizon" nature of real-world scientific inquiry. A typical biological analysis requires a scientist to integrate raw molecular measurements, experimental metadata, and existing literature to formulate and verify complex claims.

The paper introduces **scBench-Long**, a benchmark designed to evaluate whether AI agents can navigate these multi-step, integrative processes. Unlike existing frameworks that might test broad biological knowledge or the execution of simple scripts, scBench-Long requires agents to process raw or near-raw data and derive specific, evidence-backed conclusions. The benchmark consists of 21 evaluations derived from five complex biological study systems, ranging from cancer immunology to embryonic development. By establishing a framework for verifiable scientific reasoning, the work aims to provide a diagnostic lens into the current capabilities and limitations of frontier AI systems in the biological sciences.

## The Complexity of Long-Horizon Scientific Reasoning

In single-cell research, the path from a raw sequencing file to a scientific discovery is rarely linear. An analyst must perform a sequence of operations: quality control, normalization, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and biological annotation. However, the true difficulty lies in the synthesis. For instance, determining if a specific T-cell population is "exhausted" requires more than just checking for the presence of a single marker; it involves evaluating the expression of multiple genes, assessing the chromatin state of regulatory regions, and often integrating T-cell receptor (TCR) sequencing data to understand clonal expansion.

Current AI benchmarks often simplify this process into "local" tasks. A model might be asked to predict a label for a pre-processed data point, which does not test its ability to handle the ambiguity and technical hurdles of a full analysis. scBench-Long addresses this by presenting agents with "global" tasks. These tasks are claim-conditioned, meaning the agent is asked to determine the validity of a scientific claim based on the provided data. This shift from local to global evaluation forces the agent to demonstrate reasoning across a "long horizon" of analytical steps.

## Benchmark Composition and Study Systems

The 21 evaluations in scBench-Long are distributed across five distinct study systems, chosen to reflect the diversity and complexity of modern single-cell research:

1.  **Melanoma Tumor-Reactive CD8 T Cells:** This system focuses on the relationship between gene expression and TCR clonotypes. Agents must determine if specific transcriptomic states correspond to expanded clones, a critical step in understanding anti-tumor immunity.
2.  **CD8 RNA+ATAC Regulatory Inference:** Using multiome data (simultaneous RNA and ATAC sequencing), agents are asked to verify regulatory links. This requires reasoning across two different molecular modalities to see if a transcription factor's expression correlates with the accessibility of its target genes.
3.  **Human–Monkey Chimeric Embryos:** This cross-species study requires agents to compare developmental trajectories and intercellular signaling between species, often requiring the use of ortholog maps and specialized databases.
4.  **KRAS-Driven Lung Tumor Aging:** This system challenges agents to distinguish between biological changes caused by aging and those caused by tumor progression, a task that requires careful handling of confounding variables.
5.  **Lethal COVID-19 Lung Pathology:** Agents must integrate single-nucleus RNA data with immune repertoire and spatial validation evidence to characterize the cellular drivers of severe disease.

For each system, the evaluations provide anonymized data and the minimum necessary experimental context. The agents are not given a prescribed workflow; they must decide which tools to use and how to interpret the results to reach a final conclusion.

## Construction of Verifiable Scientific Claims

A central difficulty in benchmarking scientific discovery is the definition of "ground truth." Scientific conclusions can be subjective, and reproducibility is a known challenge in the literature. To ensure scBench-Long provides a robust evaluation, the authors employed a rigorous five-stage construction process.

First, candidate claims were identified from high-impact publications or internal research. Each claim then underwent independent reanalysis to ensure it could be deterministically recovered from the data. During this stage, "prior-only" baselines were used to ensure the questions could not be answered simply by the model's internal training data without looking at the provided files. 

The final evaluations use a controlled answer surface. Instead of free-text responses, agents must provide their final conclusions as structured JSON objects using defined vocabularies. For example, an agent might need to specify a list of cell types or a direction of change (e.g., "upregulated" vs. "downregulated"). This structured approach allows for deterministic, automated grading while still permitting the agent total freedom in its analytical trajectory.

## Performance of Frontier AI Agents

The researchers evaluated 17 different model-harness pairs, including prominent Large Language Models (LLMs) like Claude 3.5, GPT-4, and Gemini 1.5. These models were paired with execution harnesses such as "Claude Code" or "PI," which provide the models with a sandbox environment to write and execute Python or R code.

The results indicate that long-horizon scientific reasoning remains a significant challenge for current AI. The highest-performing system, Claude Opus 4.8 paired with Claude Code, achieved a pass rate of only $25.4\%$ (passing $16/63$ completed runs). Gemini 3.5 Flash followed with a $22.2\%$ pass rate. 

Perhaps more telling than the aggregate scores is the "robustness" of the models. For most tasks, even the best models struggled to consistently reach the correct conclusion across multiple replicates. For instance, Claude Opus 4.8 passed at least one replicate in $8/21$ tasks, but it passed all three replicates in only $2/21$ tasks. This inconsistency suggests that successful trajectories often rely on fragile reasoning chains that are easily disrupted.

## Diagnostic Rubrics and Execution Harnesses

To go beyond a simple pass/fail metric, the researchers implemented "trajectory rubrics." These are hidden diagnostic checkpoints that measure an agent's progress through an analysis. For example, a rubric might check if an agent correctly identified the relevant cell clusters or if it performed a required statistical test.

While the deterministic endpoint score is the primary measure of success, the rubric scores provide a denser signal for debugging. The mean rubric score for successful runs was $75.9\%$, compared to $58.7\%$ for failed runs. This indicates that failed agents often complete several steps correctly but fail at critical "chokepoints" in interpretation or integration.

The choice of execution harness also proved critical. The harness is the software environment that allows the LLM to interact with the file system and run code. The study found that changing the harness could significantly shift a model's performance. For example, GPT-5.4 was more successful using the PI harness than the OpenAI Codex harness ($6/63$ vs. $2/63$). This highlights that the development of scientific AI agents requires optimizing not just the "brain" (the model) but also the "hands" (the execution environment).

## Identifying Systematic Failure Modes

By analyzing the failed trajectories, the researchers identified several recurring "failure modes" that illustrate the gap between current AI and human scientific reasoning:

*   **Reliance on Biological Priors:** Agents frequently favored conclusions that "sounded" biologically plausible based on common literature, even when the provided data explicitly showed something else. In T-cell tasks, models often defaulted to labeling cells as "exhausted" based on canonical markers, ignoring the specific clonal evidence requested by the prompt.
*   **Conflating Association with Mechanism:** Many agents struggled with causal reasoning. If two variables were correlated in the data, the agents often jumped to a mechanistic explanation (e.g., "Cell A causes Cell B to change") without considering alternative explanations like batch effects or simple co-occurrence.
*   **Single-Modality Bias:** In tasks requiring multi-modal integration (like RNA + ATAC), agents often focused exclusively on one data type. They might perform a perfect transcriptomic analysis but fail to incorporate the chromatin accessibility data, leading to an incomplete or incorrect final claim.
*   **Sensitivity to Data Abundance:** Agents showed a tendency to over-emphasize features that were quantitatively dominant (e.g., the largest cell cluster) regardless of their actual scientific relevance to the specific question being asked.

## Significance for AI-Driven Discovery

scBench-Long provides a rigorous framework for evaluating the next generation of AI scientists. By focusing on long-horizon, verifiable tasks, it moves the field away from "knowledge retrieval" and toward "empirical reasoning." 

The findings suggest that simply increasing the size of language models may not be sufficient for reliable scientific discovery. Instead, progress will likely require improvements in how agents integrate diverse data modalities, how they manage complex multi-step workflows, and how they distinguish between their internal training priors and the specific evidence presented in a dataset. 

The benchmark serves as a diagnostic tool for "test-driven development" in AI biology. As researchers develop new agent architectures and specialized harnesses, scBench-Long provides a objective way to measure whether these systems are becoming more faithful and transparent in their analysis. Ultimately, this work contributes to the creation of AI systems that can act as reliable partners in the complex, iterative process of biological research.
