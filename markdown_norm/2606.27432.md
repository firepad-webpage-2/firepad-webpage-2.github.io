## The Geometry of Light: Capturing Spectral Morphology

Astrophysical spectra are the primary tools for understanding the universe, providing a detailed record of the physical conditions and motions of distant objects. A spectral line profile—the specific shape of a flux distribution across wavelengths—is not merely a peak; it encodes a complex history of gas dynamics, temperature, and chemical composition. However, the standard practice in astronomy often involves compressing these high-dimensional profiles into a few scalar summaries: the total flux, the central wavelength (centroid), the width (velocity dispersion), and perhaps a measure of global asymmetry.

![pl-paths-png](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27432v1/PL-paths.png)
*{fig:pl-paths-png}: Comparison of different signal types (symmetric, skewed, double peak) represented as paths. Traditional summaries often fail to distinguish between shapes that share similar global moments but differ in their ordered structure.*

While effective for many applications, these summaries can ignore the "ordered morphology" of the profile. For example, two spectra might have the same total width and average asymmetry, but one might feature a subtle "shoulder" on the blue side indicating an outflow, while the other features a double peak indicating rotation. The sequence of flux variations matters. This research introduces a methodology based on path signatures, a concept from rough path theory, to represent spectral lines as directed trajectories in a velocity–flux space. This approach preserves the inherent ordering of the data, allowing researchers to measure and compare the geometric structure of spectra with high precision.

## Beyond Scalar Summaries: The Path Signature Approach

The core idea of this work is to treat a continuum-subtracted line profile as a directed path. In this framework, a spectrum is defined by a coordinate $u$ (usually wavelength or velocity) and a flux $F(u)$. Instead of viewing this as a static function, it is treated as a curve:

$$
X(u) = (u, F(u))
$$

As we move from the blue (shorter wavelength) to the red (longer wavelength) side of the line, the path $X(u)$ traces a specific trajectory in a 2D plane. A symmetric Gaussian line traces a simple path that goes up and then retraces itself, whereas an asymmetric line with a broad wing creates a loop or a "twist" in this plane.

The "path signature" is a mathematical tool that maps such a curve into a hierarchy of tensor coordinates. These coordinates capture the sequence and correlation between changes in the different axes ($u$ and $F$). Because the signature is based on iterated integrals, it naturally encodes the order of events. For practical use, the authors utilize the "log-signature," which provides a non-redundant set of descriptors that can be truncated to low orders to characterize the profile's shape efficiently.

## Interpretable Geometric Descriptors

To make these mathematical concepts accessible for astronomical analysis, the authors derive a set of scalar coefficients, denoted as $p$ coefficients, each with a specific geometric interpretation.

![ideal-halpha-path-response-hex-signed-r4-png](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27432v1/ideal_halpha_path_response_hex_signed_r4.png)
*{fig:ideal-halpha-path-response-hex-signed-r4-png}: An atlas of synthetic line profiles showing how different morphological variations (asymmetry, shoulders, double peaks) correspond to specific signatures in the path-coefficient space.*

The primary descriptors include:
- **$p_2$ (Second-Order):** This measures the "signed area" swept by the path. It is defined as:
  $$
  p_2 = \frac{1}{2}(L_{vF} - L_{Fv})
  $$
  where $L_{vF}$ represents the iterated integral of velocity and flux. This coefficient is sensitive to the net blue–red imbalance (asymmetry) and the "handedness" of the profile.
- **$p_{3v}$ and $p_{3F}$ (Third-Order):** These terms investigate where the asymmetry is localized. $p_{3v}$ emphasizes whether the imbalance occurs in the wings or the core, while $p_{3F}$ is flux-weighted, distinguishing between core-driven and wing-driven features.
- **$p_{4F}$ and $p_{4T}$ (Fourth-Order):** These capture higher-order complexity. $p_{4F}$ responds to broad bases or reversals in flux, while $p_{4T}$ is sensitive to "twist-like" structures, such as double peaks or multiple kinematic components.
- **$p_{\pm}$:** A specialized coefficient designed to distinguish between emission and absorption ordering. By tracking the cumulative positive and negative flux separately, it can differentiate between a P-Cygni profile (blueshifted absorption, redshifted emission) and an inverse P-Cygni profile, even if their absolute shapes appear identical.

## Benchmarking with Synthetic Profiles

To demonstrate the efficacy of these path descriptors, the researchers performed "stress tests" using synthetic spectral lines. They compared the $p$ coefficients against standard astronomical metrics like Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM), the non-parametric width $W_{80}$, and Gauss–Hermite coefficients ($\tilde{h}_3, \tilde{h}_4$), which are commonly used to measure skewness and kurtosis.

![cases-panel-1-display-axes-boundary-regions-png](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27432v1/cases_panel_1_display_axes_boundary_regions.png)
*{fig:cases-panel-1-display-axes-boundary-regions-png}: Separability tests on synthetic profiles. Path descriptors (right column) demonstrate a clearer separation between morphological families compared to standard widths (middle left) or Hermite moments (middle right).*

The results showed that path-signature descriptors consistently outperformed traditional methods in separating profiles with different ordered structures. For example, in a scenario where two sets of profiles had nearly identical widths and global moments but differed in whether a secondary component appeared on the blue or red side, the path coordinates achieved a near-perfect separation (quantified by an Area Under the Curve, or AUC, near $1.0$). In contrast, FWHM and $W_{80}$ were often unable to distinguish these cases, yielding AUC values close to $0.5$ (equivalent to random guessing).

A particularly compelling test involved profiles with identical absolute flux distributions but reversed emission and absorption ordering. Standard summaries that rely on absolute flux lose this distinction entirely. However, the path-based $p_{\pm}$ coefficient maintained high discriminative power, proving that the geometric representation retains critical physical information about the direction of gas flows.

## Mapping Galaxies with Integral-Field Spectroscopy

The practical utility of the method was tested using data from the MaNGA (Mapping Nearby Galaxies at APO) survey. MaNGA uses integral-field spectroscopy to obtain spectra for thousands of individual locations (spaxels) within a single galaxy.

The researchers calculated path descriptors ($p_{3v,i}, p_{3F,i}, p_{4T,i}$) for the H$\alpha$ emission line in every spaxel of several diverse galaxies. Crucially, they did not shift the spectra to a common center or provide the algorithm with any velocity or spatial information. Instead, they clustered the spaxels solely based on their three-dimensional path-feature vector:

$$
\phi_i = (p_{3v,i}, p_{3F,i}, p_{4T,i})
$$

![pt-and-section1-png](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27432v1/PT_and_section1.png)
*{fig:pt-and-section1-png}: MaNGA galaxy maps. The top row shows the optical images. The bottom row shows region-wise velocity maps recovered solely by clustering spaxels based on their path-signature morphology.*

The results revealed that spaxels grouped by morphology formed spatially coherent regions that closely matched the galaxies' actual velocity fields. This implies that the ordered structure of the line profile—captured by the path signature—implicitly encodes the large-scale kinematics of the galaxy. By segmenting a galaxy based on profile shape, researchers can identify distinct physical zones (such as rotating disks, outflowing cones, or merging components) without the need for complex, and sometimes degenerate, multi-component line fitting.

## Significance and the spectropath Software

The introduction of path signatures to spectral analysis represents a shift from quantifying scalar "amounts" (like total flux) to understanding the "geometry of change" along a sequence. This framework is uniquely suited for the current era of "Big Data" in astronomy, where surveys like MaNGA, DESI, and the upcoming Vera C. Rubin Observatory generate millions of spectra and light curves.

The method's primary advantages are:
1. **Preservation of Order:** It captures the sequence of flux variations that traditional moments ignore.
2. **Interpretability:** The descriptors correspond to intuitive geometric features like area, twist, and imbalance.
3. **Model-Agnosticism:** It does not require a pre-defined physical model (like a sum of Gaussians), making it robust for discovering unusual or complex shapes.

To support the community, the authors have released `spectropath`, a minimalist Python package designed to compute these descriptors. Beyond spectral lines, this methodology is widely applicable to any ordered astronomical data, including light curves (flux vs. time), radial profiles (property vs. distance), and spectral energy distributions. By providing a mathematical language for the "hidden geometry" of spectra, this work opens new pathways for identifying subtle physical signatures in the vast datasets of modern astrophysics.
