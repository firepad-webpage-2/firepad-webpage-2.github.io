## Introduction to Field-Widened Quantum Interferometry

Quantum communication systems rely heavily on the precise manipulation and measurement of photon states. One of the most robust methods for encoding quantum information is time-bin encoding, where a photon occupies a superposition of different arrival times. This technique is particularly effective for transmission through optical fibers and free-space links, as it is relatively immune to the polarization-mode dispersion that affects other encoding schemes. However, a major challenge arises when these photons travel through "messy" channels—such as the turbulent atmosphere or highly multimode fibers—that distort the photon's spatial mode.

When a photon's spatial mode is distorted, it becomes difficult to achieve high-visibility interference in standard unbalanced interferometers. Traditional setups often require coupling light into single-mode fibers to maintain spatial coherence, which necessitates complex and lossy adaptive optics systems to correct for atmospheric turbulence. To address this, researchers have turned to field-widened interferometers. These devices are designed to accept a wide range of input angles and spatial modes while maintaining high interference visibility.

A significant hurdle in field-widened designs is the physical size required for long time-bin delays. Interfacing photons with stationary quantum bits (qubits), such as trapped ions or solid-state defects, requires temporal separations in the nanosecond range. In a free-space interferometer, a $10 \text{ ns}$ delay corresponds to an optical path difference of roughly 3 meters. Building a field-widened interferometer of this scale using traditional optics results in a bulky, impractical device. This research presents a solution that integrates a multi-pass Herriott cell into a field-widened architecture, enabling nanosecond-scale delays in a compact, robust form factor.

## The Role of the Multi-Pass Herriott Cell

The core of this architecture is the Herriott cell, a multi-pass optical cavity consisting of two spherical mirrors with radii of curvature $R_1$ and $R_2$, separated by a distance $\ell$. Light enters the cell through a small hole in one of the mirrors, bounces between the surfaces multiple times, and exits through the same or a different aperture.

The advantage of the Herriott cell is its ability to "fold" a very long optical path into a small volume. In this work, the interferometer is constructed in a Michelson configuration. One arm contains the Herriott cell (the long path), while the other contains a flat mirror (the short path). To achieve field-widening, the optical system must be designed so that the exit position and angle of the light are insensitive to the input angle. 

Mathematically, the mirrors are defined by the surface equation:
$$
F(x,y,z) = x^2 + y^2 + 2(-1)^{n+1}(z - z_0)R_{n+1} + (z - z_0)^2 = 0
$$
where $x, y, z$ are the spatial coordinates, $n$ is the bounce number, $z_0$ is the mirror position, and $R_{n+1}$ is the radius of curvature of the reflecting surface. By optimizing the distance $\ell$ and the total number of bounces $N$, the researchers ensure that the light exiting the Herriott cell spatially and angularly overlaps with the light from the short arm, even when the input beam is tilted or contains multiple spatial modes.

## Numerical Modeling and Optimization

The researchers employed a two-step numerical approach to design and verify the interferometer. First, they used a 3D ray transfer matrix method to optimize the geometric parameters. The goal was to minimize a cost function that tracks the difference between the input and output ray coordinates. Specifically, for a given number of bounces $N$, they looked for a mirror separation $\ell$ and a flat mirror distance $d_{fm}$ such that the output ray matched the input ray's directional cosines ($s_{N,x,y} = s_{0,x,y}$ and $s_{N,z} = -s_{0,z}$) and spatial position ($x_N = x_0$).

This optimization ensures that the interferometer satisfies the "field-widening condition," which mathematically means the second-order derivative of the optical path difference with respect to the input angle is minimized.

To validate these geometric designs, the team used a more sophisticated technique called Gaussian Beamlet Decomposition (GBD). Unlike simple ray tracing, GBD accounts for:
*   Wavefront distortions and diffraction.
*   Polarization effects.
*   Aperture clipping (e.g., light hitting the edges of mirrors).
*   Interference fringe patterns.

The performance was quantified using the interference visibility $V$:
$$
V = \frac{I_{\max} - I_{\min}}{I_{\max} + I_{\min}}
$$
where $I_{\max}$ and $I_{\min}$ are the maximum and minimum intensities observed as the phase is varied. The simulations showed that the Herriott cell designs could maintain a multimode visibility above $0.995$ for delays exceeding $10 \text{ ns}$, even with highly distorted input beams.

## Experimental Validation

The researchers constructed a prototype based on a design with $R_1 = 1000 \text{ mm}$, $R_2 = 700 \text{ mm}$, and a separation $\ell = 208.63 \text{ mm}$. With $N = 18$ bounces, this setup achieved a time delay of $\Delta t = 11.98 \text{ ns}$.

### Continuous Wave (CW) Laser Tests
Initial tests were performed using CW lasers at two different wavelengths: $532 \text{ nm}$ and $785 \text{ nm}$. This demonstrated the achromatic nature of the reflective design. 
*   **Single-Mode Input:** At $532 \text{ nm}$, the visibility was $0.991(1)$. At $785 \text{ nm}$, it was $0.980(1)$.
*   **Multimode Input:** To simulate distorted light, the beams were passed through a multimode fiber. Even with these complex spatial modes, the visibility remained high: $0.970(2)$ at $532 \text{ nm}$ and $0.951(2)$ at $785 \text{ nm}$.

Most importantly, the field-widening was confirmed by varying the angle of incidence $\theta$. The visibility remained stable over a range of $0.4 \text{ degrees}$, confirming the device's robustness to beam wander and pointing errors.

### Pulsed Time-Bin Tests
To prove the system's utility for quantum communication, the team used an attenuated pulsed laser at $785 \text{ nm}$. They created time-bin encoded photons (early and late pulses) and passed them through a $5 \text{ m}$ multimode fiber to scramble the spatial mode. 

The photons then entered the Herriott cell interferometer. By adjusting the relative phase between the two paths, they measured the interference in the superposition basis. For constructive interference ($\phi = 0$) and destructive interference ($\phi = \pi$), they constructed arrival-time histograms. The resulting visibility was $0.88$. While lower than the CW results—likely due to slight path imbalances and fiber losses—this level of visibility is more than sufficient for protocols like Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) and entanglement swapping.

## Why This Approach Differs from Current Methods

Standard approaches to long-delay interferometry typically fall into two categories, both of which have drawbacks that this paper addresses:

1.  **Single-Mode Fiber Delay Lines:** These are compact but require coupling light into a single-mode fiber ($7 \text{ \mu m}$ core). High-efficiency coupling is nearly impossible without active adaptive optics when the input light is spatially distorted by the atmosphere. This adds significant loss, complexity, and power requirements.
2.  **Standard Free-Space Unbalanced Interferometers:** These can handle multimode light if they are field-widened (e.g., using thick glass blocks), but achieving a $12 \text{ ns}$ delay would require a physical path length of several meters. This makes the system extremely sensitive to mechanical vibrations and temperature fluctuations, and too large for many field applications.

The Herriott cell approach is unique because it is **passive**, **multimode-compatible**, and **compact**. By using reflective spherical mirrors, it avoids the dispersion issues of glass blocks and the coupling losses of fibers. It achieves a path difference that is many times larger than the physical length of the device itself.

## Significance and Future Impact

The ability to maintain high interference visibility with distorted, multimode light across a wide field-of-view has several high-impact applications:

*   **Satellite-to-Ground Quantum Links:** Low-Earth orbit (LEO) satellites move rapidly, leading to significant pointing challenges and atmospheric turbulence. This interferometer can passively compensate for these effects, potentially allowing for simpler receivers on nano-satellites or mobile ground stations.
*   **Interfacing with Quantum Memories:** Many quantum memory systems (like those based on atomic ensembles or rare-earth ions) require specific nanosecond delays to synchronize photon arrival with memory excitation. This device provides a stable, broadband interface for these systems.
*   **High-Dimensional Encoding:** The design can be extended to include multiple Herriott cells or different spectral channels, facilitating more complex encoding schemes that increase the data rate of quantum networks.

By providing a robust method for decoding time-bin information in "dirty" optical channels, this work removes a significant barrier to the deployment of practical, global-scale quantum communication networks. The transition from sensitive, lab-bound setups to compact, field-ready hardware is a critical step in the realization of the quantum internet.
