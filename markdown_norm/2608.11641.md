## Introduction to Wind-Resilient Aerial Logistics

Last-mile delivery is undergoing a significant shift toward integrated air-ground systems. In this model, a delivery truck acts as a mobile hub for multiple Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs), which carry out the final segment of delivery to individual customers. While this approach offers speed and flexibility, it introduces complex operational risks. Low-altitude environments are subject to dynamic and often unpredictable wind conditions that directly impact a UAV's battery consumption. If a UAV encounters strong headwinds or must compensate for lateral crosswinds, its energy reserves may deplete faster than anticipated, potentially preventing it from safely returning to the truck or a fixed depot.

Conventional routing methods often treat energy consumption as a static or deterministic value based purely on distance. However, in real-world scenarios, wind uncertainty and sensing delays make these assumptions unreliable. The research paper "Energy-Aware Wind-Resilient Routing for Truck-Assisted Multi-UAV Delivery under Wind Uncertainty" addresses these challenges by proposing an online routing framework named EWR. This framework explicitly models the impact of wind on flight feasibility and incorporates adaptive safety margins to ensure that a UAV always retains enough energy to return home, even as environmental conditions change during the mission.

## Modeling the Dynamic Windy Environment

The core of the EWR framework is a time-varying directed energy graph, denoted as $G_t = (V, E, c_t)$. In this model, the set of vertices $V$ represents waypoints such as customer locations or truck rendezvous points, while the edges $E$ represent the flight paths between them. The cost $c_t(e)$ for each edge $e$ is updated dynamically at each decision time $t$ to reflect current environmental states.

One of the primary challenges in wind-aware routing is that the actual wind vector $W(t, e)$ along a path is rarely known with perfect precision. Onboard sensors are subject to noise, and there is often a temporal lag between a wind measurement and its integration into the routing logic. To account for this, the researchers use a delayed online estimate $\hat{W}_t(e) = \hat{W}(t - \tau_e, e)$, where $\tau_e$ represents the sensing and update delay. The relationship between the true wind and the estimate is expressed as:
$$W(t, e) = \hat{W}_t(e) + \Delta W_t(e)$$
where the error term $\Delta W_t(e)$ is bounded by a confidence radius $\|\Delta W_t(e)\| \leq \epsilon_W(t, e)$. This radius expands as the information becomes more outdated or if the sensors report high noise levels.

To determine if an edge is even flyable, the system employs a conservative reachability filter. This filter checks if the UAV's lateral control systems can overcome the crosswind and ensures the UAV maintains positive forward progress. An edge is considered feasible only if the following conditions are met:
1.  The combined magnitude of the estimated perpendicular wind and the uncertainty radius is within the lateral control margin: $\|\hat{W}_\perp\| + \epsilon_W(t, e) < \rho_\perp V_A$
2.  The estimated ground speed remains positive even in a worst-case scenario: $\hat{V}_G - \epsilon_{W,v}(t, e) > 0$

## Predicting Energy Consumption

If an edge passes the reachability filter, the framework calculates the predicted energy consumption $E_t(e)$. This calculation is more sophisticated than simple distance-based metrics, as it decomposes the total power into several functional components:
$$E_t(e) = \left[ \frac{\ell(e)}{\hat{V}_G(t, e) } \right] [P_0 + P_\parallel(t, e) + P_\perp(t, e) + P_{load}(e)]$$
In this equation, $\ell(e)$ is the length of the segment and $\hat{V}_G$ is the estimated ground speed. The power terms represent the nominal cruise power ($P_0$), the power needed to overcome parallel wind components ($P_\parallel$), the power consumed by lateral control for crosswind rejection ($P_\perp$), and the additional energy required to lift the current payload ($P_{load}$). By modeling these components individually, the EWR framework can more accurately predict how different wind directions (e.g., tailwinds versus headwinds) will affect the battery life.

## Risk-Sensitive Online Routing

The EWR framework uses these energy predictions to perform risk-sensitive routing. Instead of simply seeking the path with the absolute lowest energy consumption, the system minimizes a combined cost function that penalizes uncertainty:
$$c_t(e) = E_t(e) + \lambda U_t(e)$$
The term $U_t(e)$ is an uncertainty penalty, and $\lambda$ is a coefficient that determines the UAV's level of risk aversion. The uncertainty penalty $U_t(e)$ is defined to favor paths where the wind data is recent and stable:
$$U_t(e) = \beta_W \epsilon_W(t, e) + \beta_\tau \tau_e + \beta_g \|\hat{W}_t(e) - \hat{W}_{t^-}(e)\|$$
This penalty considers the confidence radius ($\epsilon_W$), the sensing delay ($\tau_e$), and the volatility of the wind (the difference between the current estimate and the previous estimate $\hat{W}_{t^-}$). By incorporating these factors, the UAV is encouraged to avoid areas where the energy prediction is likely to be inaccurate, effectively steering it toward "safer" paths.

## Adaptive Safety Margins and Return Feasibility

A critical feature of EWR is its continuous evaluation of return-to-home feasibility. After every delivery or movement, the UAV must verify that its remaining battery is sufficient to reach the next waypoint and then return to the truck or depot. This verification includes an adaptive safety margin $E_{safe}(t, e)$, which dynamically adjusts based on several risk factors:
$$E_{safe}(t, e) = E_{res} + \alpha_w \sigma_E(t, e) + \alpha_b \Delta E_{soc, i}(t) + \alpha_p \rho_{phase}(t)$$
This margin consists of:
*   $E_{res}$: A fixed emergency energy reserve.
*   $\sigma_E$: The estimated standard deviation of the energy prediction due to wind.
*   $\Delta E_{soc, i}$: A term accounting for the uncertainty in the battery's state-of-charge measurement.
*   $\rho_{phase}$: A mission-phase risk indicator that allows the system to become more conservative as the mission progresses or as energy levels drop.

By calculating the wind-induced energy uncertainty as $\Delta E_W(t, e) = L_E(e) \epsilon_W(t, e)$ (where $L_E(e)$ is a Lipschitz bound on the energy model), the system ensures that it doesn't just plan for the average case, but for a reasonably pessimistic scenario.

## Experimental Evaluation and Results

The researchers validated EWR through extensive simulations using synthetic delivery graphs and replayed wind logs. The performance was compared against several baselines, including standard shortest-path algorithms and reactive replanning methods like D* Lite.

The results indicated that EWR consistently outperformed the baselines in terms of mission success rates and safety:
*   **Success Rates:** EWR achieved a mission success rate of approximately 92% with a full battery and maintained over 75% even with a constrained battery budget. In contrast, methods that did not account for wind uncertainty saw their success rates drop significantly under low-battery conditions.
*   **Return Failures:** EWR showed a drastically lower rate of return-to-home failures. While conventional methods often resulted in UAVs becoming stranded due to unexpected headwinds, EWR's adaptive safety margins allowed it to detect and avoid these risks before they became terminal.
*   **Energy Margins:** The framework maintained a positive minimum energy margin throughout its missions. This means that at any given point, the UAVs had a buffer of energy beyond what was strictly required for the return trip, providing a crucial layer of protection against sudden wind gusts or battery fluctuations.

## Scalability and Real-World Applicability

The scalability of EWR was tested with varying numbers of customers and UAVs. While the planning time per step naturally increased with the size of the problem, it remained within the range of 150-160 milliseconds for a scenario with 100 customers and 12 UAVs. This performance suggests that the framework is capable of supporting real-time online replanning in practical delivery scenarios.

Furthermore, the system was validated using real-world wind logs from weather stations. Even when faced with abrupt changes in wind direction and gust intensity, EWR maintained a success rate of over 86%. This robustness to real-world data underscores the value of the uncertainty-aware cost function and the continuous return-feasibility checks.

## Summary of Contributions

The EWR framework provides a comprehensive approach to managing the energy risks associated with wind in UAV logistics. Its primary contributions include:
1.  **Dynamic Modeling:** A time-varying graph model that captures the relationship between delayed, uncertain wind estimates and UAV power consumption.
2.  **Risk-Sensitive Routing:** A routing objective that balances the need for energy efficiency with a penalty for environmental uncertainty, steering UAVs toward predictable paths.
3.  **Guaranteed Return Safety:** The integration of adaptive safety margins and continuous feasibility checks to ensure that a UAV never commits to a delivery it cannot safely return from.

By shifting the focus from simple path optimization to risk-aware energy management, this research provides a path toward more reliable and safe autonomous aerial delivery in unpredictable environments.
