## Counterparty Risk and the Complexity of American Options

In the years following the 2008 financial crisis, the measurement of counterparty credit risk (CCR) became a central pillar of financial regulation and institutional risk management. A primary metric in this domain is Credit Valuation Adjustment (CVA), which represents the market value of the risk that a counterparty might default before the final maturity of a derivative contract. Estimating CVA requires a detailed projection of a portfolio's future value distributions, known as exposure profiles.

For many standard derivatives, calculating these profiles is straightforward. However, American options—which allow the holder to exercise the contract at any time before maturity—introduce significant complexity. To value an American option at any future point, one must determine the "optimal stopping rule": the specific conditions under which exercising the option is more valuable than continuing to hold it. This requires approximating the "continuation value," which is the expected value of all future payoffs discounted to the current time.

Traditionally, the industry has relied on the Least-Squares Monte Carlo (LSM) method, which uses polynomial regressions to approximate these continuation values. While effective for low-dimensional problems involving one or two assets, LSM faces the "curse of dimensionality." As the number of underlying assets grows, the number of required polynomial terms increases exponentially, leading to excessive computational costs and numerical instability. This paper investigates the use of Randomized Neural Networks (RNNs) as a scalable alternative for estimating exposure and CVA, particularly in high-dimensional settings where traditional regression methods struggle.

## Defining Exposure Profiles and CVA

To assess the risk of a counterparty defaulting, risk managers look at the potential loss at various points in time. The two most common measures are Expected Exposure (EE) and Potential Future Exposure (PFE). Expected Exposure at time $t$ is the average positive value of the contract across all simulated market paths, defined as:

$EE(t) = \mathbb{E}[\max(V(t), 0)]$

where $V(t)$ is the value of the derivative or portfolio at time $t$. While EE provides the average risk, $PFE_{\alpha}(t)$ represents a tail-risk measure (similar to Value-at-Risk), indicating the maximum exposure expected with a specific confidence level $\alpha$.

These exposure profiles are the building blocks of CVA. The unilateral CVA for a portfolio is calculated by summing the discounted expected exposures weighted by the probability of the counterparty defaulting in each time interval:

$$CVA = (1 - R) \sum_{i=1}^N D(0, t_i) EE(t_i) \Delta PD_i$$

In this expression, $R$ is the recovery rate (the fraction of the debt recovered after default), $D(0, t_i)$ is the discount factor, and $\Delta PD_i$ is the marginal probability of the counterparty defaulting between $t_{i-1}$ and $t_i$. Because CVA is a sum over the entire life of the contract, any errors in the estimation of the continuation value—and thus the exercise decision—propagate through the entire exposure curve, potentially leading to significant mispricing of risk.

## The Randomized Neural Network Approach (RLSM)

The research proposes a Randomized Least-Squares Monte Carlo (RLSM) framework. This approach replaces the fixed polynomial basis functions used in LSM with a single-hidden-layer neural network. The defining characteristic of a randomized neural network is that the weights and biases of the hidden layer are not trained; instead, they are randomly generated from a fixed distribution and remain constant.

For an input vector $x$ (containing market data like stock prices and volatilities), the $j$-th hidden neuron calculates a non-linear feature:

$h_j = \sigma(\sum_{k=1}^d w_{jk} x_k + b_j)$

where $\sigma$ is an activation function (like the ReLU or sigmoid), and $w$ and $b$ are the random weights and biases. Because the hidden layer is fixed, the only parameters that need to be optimized are the weights of the final output layer. This optimization is performed using a simple linear regression—specifically, ridge regression—to map the randomized features to the observed payoffs.

This architecture offers two major advantages. First, the random features can capture complex, non-linear relationships more effectively than a fixed set of polynomials. Second, because only the final layer is trained, the computational cost is significantly lower than that of fully trained deep learning models that require backpropagation. The paper utilizes ridge regression to solve the output weights $\beta$ by minimizing a loss function that includes a penalty term to prevent overfitting:

$$\mathcal{L}(\beta) = \sum_{n=1}^N (y_n - \sum_{j=1}^M \beta_j h_{j,n})^2 + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^M \beta_j^2$$

This regularization is particularly important for exposure estimation, as it ensures the projected value curves remain smooth and stable across different time steps.

## Computational Performance and Scaling

The core contribution of the study is the demonstration of how RLSM scales in high-dimensional settings. The researchers tested the method on "max-call" options, where the payoff depends on the maximum value among a group of $d$ different stocks:

$\max(\max(S_1, \dots, S_d) - K, 0)$

As the dimension $d$ increases, the state space expands, making the continuation value increasingly difficult to estimate. The experiments compared RLSM and LSM across dimensions ranging from $d=5$ to $d=50$ under both the Black-Scholes model (constant volatility) and the Heston model (stochastic volatility).

In low dimensions (e.g., $d=1$), LSM remains slightly more efficient because the relationship between the asset price and the option value is simple enough for polynomials to handle perfectly. However, as the dimension grows, the performance gap shifts. In the 50-dimensional case under Heston dynamics—a highly complex scenario involving 50 asset prices and 50 stochastic volatility processes—RLSM achieved a speedup factor of over 100 compared to LSM.

Specifically, for $d=50$, LSM required nearly 400 seconds to estimate the exposure profile, while RLSM completed the task in less than 3 seconds. Furthermore, the study found that LSM began to show signs of numerical instability and "under-convergence" at high dimensions, often requiring a much larger number of simulated paths to produce a stable price. RLSM, by contrast, provided stable and accurate exposure profiles even with a smaller path budget.

## CVA Case Study and Portfolio Netting

Risk management is rarely performed on a single trade; instead, it involves large portfolios where trades can offset one another. This is known as netting. If a bank has a long position in one option and a short position in another with the same counterparty, the total exposure is not the sum of the individual exposures, but rather the positive part of the net value of all trades.

The researchers conducted a case study on a portfolio consisting of five high-dimensional American max-call options, including one short position. The goal was to see if the efficiency of RLSM translated to a realistic portfolio setting with netting agreements.

The results showed that RLSM accurately captured the netting effects, producing a portfolio EE curve that converged closely to the LSM benchmark. When calculating the final CVA under different credit scenarios (low, base, and stressed default probabilities), the difference between the two methods was negligible. However, the computational effort required for RLSM was a fraction of that required for LSM. This suggests that RLSM is not just a theoretical alternative but a practical tool for large-scale risk systems that must process thousands of trades across multiple asset classes daily.

## Implications for Risk Management

The transition from traditional regression to randomized machine learning methods represents a significant shift in how complex derivative risks are handled. By fixing the hidden layer of the neural network, RLSM sidesteps the computational heavy lifting usually associated with deep learning while retaining the ability to process high-dimensional inputs.

The paper's findings indicate that for single-asset problems, the traditional LSM method remains the standard due to its simplicity. However, for portfolios involving multi-asset products or complex market dynamics like stochastic volatility, the randomized neural network approach provides a more robust and scalable solution.

For financial institutions, the impact of these findings is twofold. First, it enables the calculation of CVA and other risk metrics for complex products that were previously too computationally expensive to model accurately. Second, the speed of RLSM allows for more frequent risk updates and more extensive "what-if" scenario analysis, which is critical during periods of market stress. As the financial industry continues to move toward more granular and data-intensive risk management frameworks, the efficiency of randomized architectures like RLSM will likely play an increasingly important role.
