## Integrating Diffusion with Sequential Dynamics

The probabilistic modeling of sequential data has traditionally followed two distinct paths: State Space Models (SSMs) and Generative Diffusion Models. State Space Models, including modern deep neural variants like Deep Kalman Filters, provide a structured approach to temporal data by assuming that observations are generated from underlying latent states that evolve over time. While mathematically rigorous, these models frequently rely on simple Gaussian distributions to describe how one state transitions to the next. This assumption often fails to capture the complexity of real-world systems, where transitions may be highly nonlinear, discontinuous, or multimodal.

On the other hand, diffusion models have emerged as highly expressive generative tools capable of synthesizing complex, high-dimensional data. In the context of time series, they have demonstrated high performance in forecasting and imputation. However, most existing time-series diffusion models function primarily as samplers; they lack a principled mechanism for inferring the evolving internal state of a system in a step-by-step manner. Furthermore, when these models operate in a latent space, they typically require a two-stage training process—first training an autoencoder and then a diffusion model—which can lead to a suboptimal or "over-regularized" latent representation where critical dynamical information is lost.

The Diffusion-Driven State Space Model (DDSSM) addresses these limitations by integrating diffusion models directly into the transition mechanism of a State Space Model. This combination allows for a model that inherits the principled sequential inference of SSMs while utilizing the extreme flexibility of diffusion processes to represent complex latent dynamics.

## The Architecture of the DDSSM

The DDSSM models a time series $x_{1:T}$ with observations $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^D$ and external covariates $u_{1:T}$ by introducing a sequence of latent variables $z_{1:T}$, where each $z_t \in \mathbb{R}^M$ represents the system's hidden state at time $t$. The model defines a joint probability distribution over observations and latent states, factorized according to the dependencies of a State Space Model:

$$
p(x_{1:T}, z_{1:T} | u_{1:T}) = p_{\eta,\theta}(z_{1:j}, x_{1:j} | u_{1:j}) \prod_{t=j+1}^T p^{(t)}_\psi(z_t | z_{t-j:t-1}, u_{t-j:t}) p^{(t)}_\theta(x_t | z_{t-j+1:t}, u_{t-j+1:t})
$$

In this formulation, $j$ represents the Markov order, determining how many previous steps influence the current state and observation. The framework consists of three primary components:

1.  **Diffusion-Driven Transitions:** The core departure from traditional models is $p^{(t)}_\psi(z_t | \cdot)$. Instead of a fixed parametric distribution like a Gaussian, this transition is defined by a diffusion process. For every time step $t$, the model considers a sequence of intermediate noisy states $(z^1_t, z^2_t, \dots, z^K_t)$. A neural network learns the reverse diffusion process $p^{(t)}_\psi(z^{k-1}_t | z^k_t, z_{t-j:t-1}, u_{t-j:t})$, allowing the model to represent arbitrary, multimodal probability densities in the latent space.
2.  **Gaussian Emissions:** The mapping from the latent state to the observed data, $p^{(t)}_\theta(x_t | \cdot)$, is modeled as a Gaussian distribution:
    $$
    p^{(t)}_\theta(x_t | r_t) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(r_t), \Sigma_\theta(r_t))
    $$
    where $r_t = (z_{t-j+1:t}, u_{t-j+1:t})$ contains the relevant latent and covariate context.
3.  **Expressive Initialization:** To avoid the common pitfall of a restrictive initial state prior, the model employs a Variational Hierarchical Prior (VHP) for $p_\eta(z_{1:j} | u_{1:j})$. This involves auxiliary variables $z_{-j+1:0}$, providing the flexibility needed to start the sequence without over-regularizing the latent space.

## Inference through Kalman Smoothing

Inference in the DDSSM involves estimating the posterior distribution of the latent states given the observed data. Because the exact posterior is mathematically intractable, the DDSSM uses a variational approximation $q_\phi$. A distinctive feature of this model is how it factorizes the variational posterior to maintain the temporal structure of the data:

$$
q_\phi(z_{1:T}, \{z^k_t\} | x_{1:T}, u_{1:T}) = q_\phi(z_{1:j} | x_{1:T}, u_{1:j}) \times \prod_{t=j+1}^T q^{(t)}_\phi(z_t | z_{t-j:t-1}, x_{t:T}, u_{t-j:t}) \times \prod_{t=j+1}^T q_{chain}(z^k_t | z_t)
$$

This factorization incorporates a "Kalman smoothing" behavior. Specifically, the term $q^{(t)}_\phi(z_t | \dots, x_{t:T}, \dots)$ indicates that the inferred state at time $t$ depends not only on past states but also on future observations $x_{t:T}$. This is typically implemented using a backward-running recurrent neural network that summarizes future information, allowing the model to refine its understanding of the past as more data becomes available. The diffusion noising process $q_{chain}(z^k_t | z_t)$ is kept fixed, following standard diffusion principles.

## End-to-End Training and Preconditioning

One of the significant technical contributions of the DDSSM is the ability to train the entire system—the encoder, the decoder, and the diffusion transition model—simultaneously. This is achieved by maximizing a single Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) on the marginal log-likelihood $\log p(x_{1:T} | u_{1:T})$.

The training objective includes terms for reconstruction accuracy (how well $z_t$ explains $x_t$), the diffusion loss (how well the transition model captures latent changes), and regularization terms involving the VHP and posterior entropy. To ensure stability during this joint optimization, the authors utilize preconditioning techniques. This involves scaling the inputs and outputs of the diffusion network to normalize gradient magnitudes across different noise levels $k$. Without such scaling, the training of the diffusion component often interferes with the learning of the latent representation, a problem that frequently forced previous researchers to use disconnected two-stage training methods.

The parameters $(\theta, \psi, \eta, \phi, \Phi)$ are updated using stochastic backpropagation. The model also employs a weighting term $\lambda$ for the regularization components, which is often annealed during the early stages of training to allow the model to focus on basic data reconstruction before refining the complex latent dynamics.

## Demonstrating Success in Multimodal Environments

The DDSSM was evaluated using a simulation study designed to expose the weaknesses of standard Gaussian-based models. The researchers generated a synthetic time series where the latent state $z_t$ followed a random walk with bimodal noise:

$$
z^{(i)}_t = 0.9z^{(i)}_{t-1} + 4s^{(i)}_t, \quad s^{(i)}_t \sim \text{Unif}(\{-1, 1\})
$$

Observations $x_t$ were then created by adding noise to these states: $x^{(i)}_t = z^{(i)}_t + \epsilon^{(i)}_t$, where $\epsilon^{(i)}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.2)$. This environment creates a situation where, at each step, the system is likely to jump to one of two different regions. A Gaussian model, forced to pick a single mean, will likely predict the average of these two modes—a value where the system is actually least likely to be.

In contrast, the DKF's reconstructions were notably poor, suggesting that its restrictive Gaussian transition prior acted as a bottleneck, preventing the model from learning an effective latent representation of the data.

## Conclusion and Future Potential

The DDSSM represents a shift in how sequential generative models can be constructed. By replacing rigid parametric assumptions with diffusion processes within a state space framework, it enables the modeling of systems that were previously difficult to capture accurately. The successful implementation of end-to-end joint training resolves a major hurdle in latent diffusion research, ensuring that the latent space remains informative for the dynamics it is intended to represent.

The implications for this work extend to any field dealing with complex, nonlinear temporal data, such as finance, meteorology, or robotics. Beyond pure predictive performance, the DDSSM provides a framework for principled latent inference, offering a path toward more explainable AI in time-series analysis. By understanding not just *what* will happen next, but the range of possible internal states that lead to that outcome, researchers can build more robust and reliable predictive systems.
