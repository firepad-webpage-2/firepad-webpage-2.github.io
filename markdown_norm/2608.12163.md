## The Role of Surface Texture in Tool Condition Monitoring

In the manufacturing industry, the surface of a machined part serves as a "fingerprint" of the process that created it. In turning operations, the interaction between the cutting tool and the workpiece determines the final quality, dimensional accuracy, and functional performance of the component. As a cutting tool wears down over time, this fingerprint changes. Monitoring these changes is essential for maintaining process stability, ensuring workpiece quality, and optimizing the lifespan of expensive cutting tools.

Traditional methods for assessing surface quality rely on global roughness parameters defined in standards like ISO 4287 or the newer ISO 21920 series. Parameters such as the arithmetic mean deviation $R_a$ or the root mean square deviation $R_q$ provide a single numerical value that summarizes the height variations across an entire measured profile. While these values are easy to calculate, they often fail to capture the nuances of how a tool actually breaks down. Because they average all data points equally, localized changes in the cutting edge geometry—caused by specific wear mechanisms like notch wear or cratering—can be "washed out" in the final calculation.

This research investigates an alternative approach: feature characterization based on the ISO 21920-2 standard. Rather than treating a surface profile as a single statistical entity, feature characterization partitions the profile into individual geometric units, such as the periodic grooves left by the tool's feed rate. By analyzing these features individually, researchers can extract spatially resolved information that is more directly linked to the physical state of the cutting edge.

## Challenges with Global Surface Parameters

The motivation for moving beyond global parameters stems from their documented inconsistencies. Previous studies have shown that $R_a$ and $R_q$ do not always trend predictably with tool wear. In some cases, these parameters increase as a tool wears; in others, they may stay constant or even decrease depending on the specific workpiece material and cutting conditions. This lack of robustness makes it difficult to use standard roughness metrics as reliable indicators for automated tool life management.

The primary limitation is that global parameters lack spatial resolution. A turned surface is characterized by a dominant, periodic structure: the feed grooves. The geometry of these grooves is a direct reflection of the tool's nose radius and the feed rate $f$. When a tool wears, it doesn't just get "rougher" in a general sense; the specific shape of the feed groove is altered. For example, wear on the trailing flank of the tool might sharpen the peaks of the grooves, while crater wear on the rake face might change the depth of the valleys. Global parameters aggregate these distinct physical effects into a single number, losing the diagnostic information required to understand *how* the tool is failing.

## Feature Characterization via ISO 21920-2

To address these limitations, this work utilizes the feature characterization framework introduced in ISO 21920-2. This method follows a structured five-step process to transform a continuous profile measurement into a set of discrete, statistically significant features:

1.  **Watershed Segmentation:** The profile is partitioned into elementary segments, categorized as hills (peaks) or dales (valleys). This is analogous to how a landscape is divided into different drainage basins.
2.  **Pruning:** Small, insignificant features—often resulting from measurement noise or minor material imperfections—are merged into larger adjacent features based on a predefined threshold. In this study, a width-based threshold of $0.15 \text{ mm}$ was used to ensure the segments remained stable.
3.  **Significant Features:** A filter is applied to retain only the features of interest. For turning, the focus is on the feed grooves. The researchers retained features with widths between $0.19 \text{ mm}$ and $0.21 \text{ mm}$, matching the experimental feed rate of $f = 0.2 \text{ mm/rev}$.
4.  **Feature Attributes:** For each identified groove, specific geometric properties are calculated, such as height, width, volume, or local gradient.
5.  **Attribute Statistics:** Finally, statistical measures (mean, maximum, standard deviation) are derived from the attributes of all significant features in the profile.

By isolating the grooves, the researchers could distinguish between the "intended" geometry of the surface (the deterministic component) and the random variations (the stochastic component).

## The Mean-Feature Approach: Separating Groove Shape from Noise

A central contribution of this research is the development of a "mean-feature" approach. This method decomposes the total surface profile $z(x)$ into two distinct parts:

$$z(x) = z_{\text{det}}(x) + z_{\text{stoch}}(x)$$

Here, $z_{\text{det}}$ represents the deterministic profile—the repeated, average shape of the feed grooves—while $z_{\text{stoch}}$ represents the stochastic profile, containing the random micro-roughness. 

To create the mean feature, all valid grooves identified through segmentation are normalized to a standard width and averaged point-by-point. This process effectively filters out random noise and highlights the systematic changes caused by tool wear. The analysis revealed that tool wear effects are almost exclusively captured by the deterministic component $z_{\text{det}}$. Correlations between wear indicators and the stochastic component $z_{\text{stoch}}$ were consistently weak (Pearson correlation $r \leq 0.41$), whereas the deterministic component showed strong relationships ($r$ up to 0.93).

This finding is significant because it proves that tool wear primarily manifests as a change in the fundamental geometry of the tool's imprint, rather than an increase in random surface noise.

## Identifying Robust Indicators for Tool Wear

The study conducted an exhaustive search through nearly one million combinations of parameters and feature characterization settings to find the most reliable indicator of tool wear. The wear states were quantified using three metrics: the mean width of flank wear land $V_B$, the crater wear area $A_C$, and the total cutting time $t_c$.

The analysis identified a specific parameter, the mean maximum absolute gradient evaluated at the groove level (denoted as $R_{dt}$ groove), as the superior indicator. While the standard $R_{dt}$ (calculated globally over the whole profile) showed a poor correlation of $r = 0.33$, the groove-level $R_{dt}$ achieved a correlation of $r = 0.95$. 

Other parameters that showed high sensitivity to wear included hybrid parameters related to slope and length, such as the root mean square gradient $R_{dq}$, the developed length $R_{dl}$, and the developed length ratio $R_{dr}$. However, $R_{dt}$ groove remained the most robust. In fact, complex machine learning models like Gradient-Boosted Trees (XGBoost) or Partial Least Squares (PLS) failed to significantly outperform a simple linear regression using only $R_{dt}$ groove, which explained between 83% and 90% of the variance in tool wear.

## Physical Validation: The Notch Wear Mechanism

To ensure these statistical findings had a physical basis, the researchers compared the reconstructed mean surface features directly with confocal microscope measurements of the actual cutting tool edges. 

The comparison showed a high degree of agreement, with a root-mean-square error (RMSE) of approximately $0.50 \mu\text{m}$. This validation confirmed that the changes observed in the surface profile were direct copies of the worn tool's geometry. Specifically, the researchers observed a "sharpening" effect on the trailing flank of the feed grooves as wear progressed. This was traced back to **notch wear** on the tool's secondary cutting edge. 

![comparison-of-surface-profile-and-tool-geometry](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12163v1/figures/Compare_profiles.png)

As notch wear develops, it creates a localized indentation in the tool. Because of the kinematics of the turning process, this indentation results in a steeper, "sharper" peak on the workpiece surface at the trailing edge of the groove. The success of the $R_{dt}$ groove parameter is largely due to its ability to capture this specific gradient increase at the trailing flank.

## Implications for Industrial Quality Control

The results of this study have several practical implications for modern manufacturing:

*   **Improved Diagnostic Capabilities:** By using feature-based parameters like $R_{dt}$ groove, manufacturers can move beyond "pass/fail" quality checks and gain specific insights into how their tools are wearing (e.g., detecting notch wear vs. flank wear).
*   **Foundation for Inline Monitoring:** Gradient-based parameters are well-suited for high-speed, non-contact sensors, such as scattered light sensors. The finding that $R_{dt}$ is a robust wear indicator suggests that these sensors could be used for real-time tool condition monitoring on the shop floor.
*   **Optimization of Tool Life:** The high predictive accuracy of the proposed models ($R^2_{\text{LOTO}} = 0.82$ for flank wear) allows for more precise scheduling of tool changes, preventing the use of worn tools that might compromise part integrity while avoiding the waste of premature tool disposal.
*   **Support for Digital Twins:** The ability to decompose surfaces into deterministic and stochastic components provides a highly accurate dataset for validating finite element (FE) models and digital twins of the machining process.

In summary, this research demonstrates that the standardized toolbox of ISO 21920-2, when adapted to the periodic nature of turning, provides a physically interpretable and highly robust framework for monitoring tool wear directly from the workpiece surface.
