## Introduction to Formalized Stochastic Analysis

Stochastic calculus provides the mathematical framework for modeling systems that evolve under uncertainty. Since its inception, primarily through the work of Kiyosi Itô, it has become a cornerstone of quantitative finance, physics, and engineering. However, the rigorous foundations of stochastic analysis—which involve measure theory, probability spaces, and delicate convergence arguments—are notoriously complex. This complexity makes proofs prone to subtle errors, particularly when dealing with continuous-time martingales and stochastic integrals.

In recent years, the mathematical community has turned toward proof assistants—software tools like Lean 4—to verify these foundations. While basic probability and Brownian motion have been previously formalized, the central operations of the field, specifically the construction of the Itô integral and the proof of Itô’s formula, have remained elusive until now. This work by Raphael Coelho presents a complete, machine-checked development of the $L^2$ Itô calculus for Brownian motion. By bridging this gap, the work establishes a verified bedrock that ensures the correctness of any subsequent application, such as the Black-Scholes model in finance or the analysis of stochastic partial differential equations.

## The Mathematical Challenge of Stochastic Integration

To understand the contribution of this work, one must first appreciate why stochastic integration is non-trivial. In classical calculus, we integrate functions with respect to smooth variables. However, Brownian motion paths, while continuous, are nowhere differentiable and possess infinite total variation. This means that a standard Riemann-Stieltjes integral cannot be defined for Brownian motion in the classical sense.

The Itô integral addresses this by defining the integral as a limit in the $L^2$ sense rather than a path-by-path limit. An $L^2$ space is a space of functions where the square of the function is integrable, effectively allowing us to talk about the "average energy" of a process. A key property required for this integration is predictability: the value of the integrand at any time $t$ must only depend on information available strictly before $t$. Without this property, the integral loses its martingale nature—a property where the "best guess" for the future value of the integral is its current value.

This formalization effort addresses these hurdles by strictly defining the space of integrands and using a robust extension theorem to move from simple step-functions to general square-integrable processes.

## Defining the Predictable Integrand Space

A unique design choice in this work is how the space of allowed integrands, denoted as $H_T$, is constructed within Lean 4. In traditional mathematics, one might define a process and then prove it is "predictable." In a formal system, this often leads to cumbersome side-conditions that must be passed through every theorem.

Instead, the author defines $H_T$ directly as an $L^2$ space over a specialized "trimmed" measure. Specifically, the development uses the product measure of time and probability, denoted as $(\lambda_T \otimes \mu)$, and restricts it to the predictable $\sigma$-algebra. By doing so, any process residing in $H_T$ is predictable by definition. This structural choice simplifies the formal proofs significantly, as predictability becomes a property of the type itself rather than a predicate that needs to be constantly re-verified. Time is indexed using $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, ensuring consistency with existing libraries while handling the bounded horizon $[0, T]$ through a finite time measure.

## Constructing the Itô Integral as an Isometry

The formalization follows a standard two-step mathematical construction, but executes it with machine-level rigor. First, the integral is defined for "simple processes"—processes that are constant over discrete intervals and depend only on the past. For these simple processes, the integral is just a finite sum of increments.

The author proves that for these simple processes, the Itô isometry holds. This isometry states that the variance of the stochastic integral is equal to the expected total energy of the integrand. In Lean, this is verified using a $\pi$-system of predictable rectangles:
$$ \{ (s, t] \times A : s \leq t, A \in \mathcal{F}_s \} \cup \{ \{0\} \times A_0 : A_0 \in \mathcal{F}_0 \} $$
where $\mathcal{F}_s$ represents the information available at time $s$.

Once the isometry is established for simple processes, the author uses a density argument. By proving that simple processes are dense in $H_T$, the integral can be uniquely extended to all processes in $H_T$ using Lean’s `extendOfNorm` functionality. This ensures that the resulting integral map is a continuous linear isometry between the space of processes and the space of random variables.

## Realizing the Integral as a Martingale Process

While the initial construction defines the integral as a single random variable (the result of integrating up to time $T$), most applications require the integral to be viewed as a process that evolves over time $t$. The work achieves this by defining the process at any time $t \leq T$ through a central structural identity:
$$ (\phi \cdot B)_t = E[I_T(\phi) \mid \mathcal{F}_t] $$
This identity states that the value of the integral process at time $t$ is the conditional expectation of its terminal value given the information available at time $t$.

From this single, elegant definition, the author derives a suite of essential properties:
1.  **Adaptedness**: The integral at time $t$ depends only on information up to time $t$.
2.  **Martingale Property**: The process has no drift; its future expectation is its current value.
3.  **Contraction Bound**: The $L^2$ norm of the process at any time is bounded by the norm of the integrand: $\|(\phi \cdot B)_t\|_{L^2} \leq \|\phi\|_{H_T}$.
4.  **$L^2$-Continuity**: The process changes continuously in time in an $L^2$ sense.

This approach differs from some textbook treatments by deriving all properties from the projection onto the filtration $\mathcal{F}_t$, providing a highly unified proof architecture within the assistant.

## Formalizing Itô’s Formula

The pinnacle of the work is the machine-checked proof of Itô’s formula, which acts as the "Fundamental Theorem of Calculus" for stochastic processes. It accounts for the fact that Brownian motion has non-zero quadratic variation, leading to an extra second-derivative term that does not appear in classical calculus.

The author formalizes Itô’s formula for functions $f \in C^3(\mathbb{R})$ with bounded derivatives. The proof utilizes a discrete-to-continuous argument. It begins with a Taylor expansion of the function over discrete time steps, creating a "discrete Itô identity." As the time steps become infinitely small, the terms of the expansion converge:
- The first-order terms converge to the Itô integral.
- The second-order terms converge to the "correction" term involving the second derivative.
- The higher-order remainders are proven to vanish.

The resulting formalized theorem for the autonomous case is:
$$ f(B_T) - f(B_0) = I_T(g_{f'}) + \frac{1}{2} \int_0^T f''(B_s) ds $$
where $I_T(g_{f'})$ represents the Itô integral of the derivative of $f$ along the Brownian path.

The paper also includes the time-dependent version of the formula, which is essential for pricing financial derivatives like options:
$$ f(T, B_T) - f(0, B_0) = I_T(g_{f_x}) + \int_0^T (f_t + \frac{1}{2} f_{xx})(s, B_s) ds $$
This version includes the partial derivative with respect to time ($f_t$), covering the full scope of the calculus needed for the Black-Scholes framework.

## Verification Discipline and Significance

A major contribution of this work is its verification discipline. The formalization is "sorry-free," meaning every step is fully justified by the underlying axioms of Lean 4 and Mathlib. The author implemented a build-enforced axiom audit to ensure the results do not rely on unproven assumptions. 

The work also settles specific examples that serve as benchmarks for the library. For instance, the integral of Brownian motion with respect to itself is formalized:
$$ \int_0^T B_s dB_s = \frac{1}{2} (B_T^2 - B_0^2 - T) $$
The presence of the $-T$ term (which would be zero in classical calculus) serves as a verified confirmation of the non-classical nature of the Itô integral.

By providing the first machine-checked proof of these core theorems, this research provides a reliable infrastructure for the formalization of mathematical finance. Future researchers can now build upon this library to verify the correctness of hedging strategies, risk models, and complex stochastic differential equations with the absolute certainty provided by computer-aided proof. While currently limited to the $L^2$ theory and Brownian motion, the architecture is designed to be extensible to general semimartingales and more general function classes in the future.
