## Introduction to Offline Deep Q Estimation

In reinforcement learning (RL), an agent learns to make optimal decisions by interacting with an environment to maximize cumulative rewards. However, in many real-world scenarios—such as medical treatment planning, autonomous driving, or industrial process control—direct interaction is often too risky, expensive, or ethically problematic. This necessitates **Offline Reinforcement Learning**, where an agent must learn an optimal policy using only a pre-collected, static dataset of historical interactions.

![pendulum-environment](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.14401v1/figs/pendulum_env.png)
*{fig:pendulum-environment}: The Pendulum environment, a classic continuous control task used to evaluate the performance of offline RL algorithms.*

The core challenge in offline RL is estimating the **optimal action-value function**, denoted as $Q^*$. For any state-action pair $(x, a) \in \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{A}$, $Q^*(x, a)$ represents the expected long-term return starting from state $x$, taking action $a$, and following the optimal policy thereafter. Once $Q^*$ is accurately estimated, the optimal policy can be derived by selecting the action that maximizes $Q^*$ in any given state.

Standard methods often struggle because the fundamental laws governing the environment—the reward function and the transition dynamics—are unknown. This research introduces a two-stage framework that decouples the estimation of these dynamics from the learning of the value function. By utilizing **conditional diffusion models**, the authors provide a way to accurately model high-dimensional environment behaviors, leading to a more robust estimation of $Q^*$ with strong theoretical guarantees.

## The Challenge of the Unobservable Bellman Operator

The optimal action-value function $Q^*$ is defined as the fixed point of the **optimal Bellman operator** $\mathcal{T}^*$. Specifically, $Q^*$ satisfies the Bellman optimality equation:

$$
Q^*(x, a) = \mathbb{E}[R + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^*(X', a') | x, a]
$$

where $R$ is the immediate reward, $X'$ is the next state, and $\gamma \in (0, 1)$ is the discount factor. The operator $\mathcal{T}^*$ is defined as:

$$
\mathcal{T}^* Q(x, a) = \mathbb{E} [R + \gamma \max_{a' \in \mathcal{A}} Q(X', a') | X = x, A = a]
$$

In a typical offline setting, we do not know the conditional distributions of rewards or transitions. Consequently, $\mathcal{T}^*$ is unobservable. Most existing methods, such as Fitted Q-Iteration (FQI), attempt to approximate this operator iteratively using the available data. However, these recursive updates can lead to "error propagation," where small errors in early iterations compound and cause the final estimate to diverge or become biased. 

Furthermore, traditional theoretical analyses of these algorithms often rely on a restrictive assumption called **Bellman completeness**. This assumes that if a function belongs to a certain class (like a specific neural network architecture), its Bellman update must also be perfectly representable within that same class. In complex, high-dimensional spaces, this assumption rarely holds true, creating a gap between theory and practice.

## Stage 1: Modeling Dynamics with Conditional Diffusion

To overcome the unobservability of $\mathcal{T}^*$, the authors propose explicitly learning the environment's reward law $R(\cdot|x, a)$ and transition kernel $P(\cdot|x, a)$. Because these are often complex, high-dimensional conditional distributions, the researchers employ **conditional diffusion models**.

Diffusion models work by defining a forward process that slowly transforms data into noise and then learning a reverse process to reconstruct the data from that noise. In this framework, the forward process is a Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE):

$$
dX'_t = -2X'_t dt + 2dB_t
$$

The key to the reverse process is the **score function**, $\nabla \log p_t(X'_t | w)$, where $w$ represents the conditioning variables (state $x$ and action $a$). A deep neural network, $b_s(t, x', w)$, is trained to estimate this score function through a technique called denoising score matching. 

Once the score estimator is trained, the researchers can generate samples from the estimated reward law and transition kernel. These samples are then used to construct an **empirical optimal Bellman operator**, $\hat{\mathcal{T}}^*$. For a given $Q$, the operator is calculated by averaging over $m$ generated samples:

$$
\hat{\mathcal{T}}^* Q(x, a) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^m \left[ \hat{R}_j + \gamma \max_{a' \in \mathcal{A}} Q(\hat{X}'_j, a') \right]
$$

This approach effectively turns the black-box environment into a generative model that we can query at will.

## Stage 2: Value Function Learning via Residual Minimization

With the empirical operator $\hat{\mathcal{T}}^*$ in hand, the second stage involves learning the action-value function $\hat{Q}$. Instead of recursive iteration, the researchers frame this as an **Empirical Bellman Residual Minimization** problem. 

Given a third independent dataset of $n$ samples, the estimator $\hat{Q}$ is found by minimizing the squared difference between the value at a state-action pair and its update under the empirical Bellman operator:

$$
\hat{Q} = \text{arg}\min_{Q \in \mathcal{Q}} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n |\hat{\mathcal{T}}^* Q(X_i, A_i) - Q(X_i, A_i)|^2
$$

where $\mathcal{Q}$ is a class of deep neural networks (specifically ReLU networks). By minimizing this residual, the algorithm seeks a function that is "self-consistent" with the learned environment dynamics. This global optimization over the dataset helps mitigate the step-by-step error accumulation seen in iterative methods.

## Theoretical Contributions and Convergence

A significant portion of this work is dedicated to providing rigorous mathematical guarantees for the proposed estimator. The researchers decompose the total error—the excess Bellman residual risk—into components stemming from the diffusion estimation and the value function approximation.

The paper establishes that the proposed method achieves an optimal convergence rate. If the underlying optimal value function has a smoothness index $\beta$, and the state and action spaces have dimensions $d_x$ and $d_a$, the excess risk $L_{\mathcal{T}^*}(\hat{Q}) - L_{\mathcal{T}^*}(Q^*)$ converges at a rate of:

$$
O(n^{-\frac{2\beta}{d_x + d_a + 2\beta}} \log^{19} n)
$$

Under a standard "concentrability condition" (which ensures the offline data sufficiently covers the state-action space), this translates to an $L_2$ convergence rate for the value function itself:

$$
O(n^{-\frac{\beta}{d_x + d_a + 2\beta}} \log^{\frac{19}{4}} n)
$$

Crucially, this analysis **does not require the Bellman completeness assumption**. By directly bounding the approximation error of the neural network class relative to $Q^*$, the authors provide a more flexible and realistic theoretical foundation. They also prove an information-theoretic lower bound:

$$
\Omega(n^{-\max \{ \frac{\alpha}{d_x + d_a + 2\alpha}, \frac{\beta}{d_x + d_a + 2\beta} \}})
$$

which shows that their method's rate is near-optimal for this class of problems.

## Empirical Performance and Real-World Application

The framework was tested on several benchmarks, including the Swimmer and Pendulum environments, as well as a real-world medical dataset.

### Continuous Control Benchmarks
In the Swimmer environment, which requires coordinated movement of multiple joints, the diffusion-based approach significantly outperformed existing baselines like MOPO (Model-based Offline Policy Optimization) and MABO (Minimax Average Bellman Optimality).

![swimmer-training-and-evaluation](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.14401v1/figs/swimmer.png)
*{fig:swimmer-training-and-evaluation}: Performance comparison on the Swimmer environment. The proposed method (red line) shows higher and more stable evaluation rewards compared to other offline RL methods.*

### Sepsis Treatment (MIMIC-III)
The researchers applied their method to the MIMIC-III dataset, focusing on the treatment of sepsis in intensive care units. The goal was to learn a treatment policy that minimizes organ dysfunction (measured by the SOFA score).

The proposed method achieved a higher average reward than both the historical clinician decisions and the best-performing baselines. This suggests that the framework can successfully extract complex treatment patterns from noisy, real-world clinical data.

## Conclusion

This work provides a principled way to estimate the optimal action-value function in offline settings by leveraging the generative power of diffusion models. By decoupling the learning of environment dynamics from the value function estimation, the authors achieve state-of-the-art results on both simulated and real-world tasks. The primary significance of the paper lies in its ability to provide strong theoretical guarantees without relying on restrictive assumptions like Bellman completeness, offering a robust path forward for applying deep reinforcement learning to critical real-world domains.
