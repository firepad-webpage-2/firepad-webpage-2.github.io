## Achievement Sets and the Inverse Problem of Measure Theory

The study of purely atomic finite measures centers on understanding how a measure is distributed across a set of points. For a sequence of positive, non-increasing terms $x_1, x_2, \dots$ that are summable, one can define a measure $\mu$ such that $\mu(n) = x_n$. A fundamental object of interest in this context is the range of the measure, also known as the achievement set of the sequence $(x_n)$. This set, denoted $E(x_n)$, consists of all possible sums of subsets of the terms in the sequence:

$$
E(x_n) = \{ \sum_{n=1}^\infty \varepsilon_n x_n : \varepsilon_n \in \{0, 1\} \}
$$

The achievement set represents the values that the measure $\mu$ can take on different subsets of the natural numbers. While the construction of $E(x_n)$ from a sequence $(x_n)$ is straightforward, the "inverse problem" is significantly more complex: given a set $S \subset \mathbb{R}$, is there a unique sequence $(x_n)$ such that $E(x_n) = S$? If so, $S$ is said to be uniquely achievable. This paper by Nowakowski and Prus-Wiśniowski provides a definitive characterization of this uniqueness for specific topological types of achievement sets.

The topological structure of $E(x_n)$ is classified by the Guthrie-Nymann Classification Theorem (1988), which states that the range of such a measure is always one of three types: a finite union of closed intervals, a Cantor set (a set that is compact, perfect, and nowhere dense), or a Cantorval (a set that contains intervals but whose boundary is a Cantor set). The uniqueness of the underlying measure depends heavily on which of these topological categories the range falls into.

## Characterizing Unique Achievability for Central Cantor Sets

Central Cantor sets are a well-behaved family of Cantor sets often used as prototypes in fractal geometry. They are constructed by iteratively removing open middle intervals from a starting interval. In the context of achievement sets, a central Cantor set corresponds to the range of a "fast convergent" sequence. A sequence $(x_n)$ is called fast convergent if every term is strictly greater than the sum of all subsequent terms:

$$
x_n > \sum_{k=n+1}^\infty x_k
$$

Let $r_n = \sum_{k=n+1}^\infty x_k$ denote the remainder of the series. The condition $x_n > r_n$ ensures that there are "gaps" in the achievement set, preventing it from forming a continuous interval. Prior research had shown that very fast convergence, such as $x_n > 2x_{n+1}$, is a sufficient condition for uniqueness. However, this paper establishes a necessary and sufficient condition.

Theorem 4 states that a central Cantor set $E(x_n)$ is uniquely achievable if and only if the defining sequence satisfies $x_n \neq 2x_{n+1}$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$. This result is significant because it identifies exactly when the inverse problem fails: uniqueness is lost only when a term is exactly twice the value of the next term. If $x_n = 2x_{n+1}$, one can construct a different, non-fast-convergent sequence that yields the same set $E(x_n)$, meaning the measure cannot be uniquely recovered from its range.

## Symmetric Cantor Sets and Multiplicities

The authors extend their characterization to symmetric Cantor sets, which are a broader class than central Cantor sets. These sets are the achievement sets of "semi-fast convergent" sequences. Unlike fast convergent sequences where every term must be distinct and satisfy a strict inequality, semi-fast convergent sequences allow terms to repeat. Such a sequence can be represented as $( \alpha_n; M_n )$, where $\alpha_n$ are distinct values and $M_n$ are their respective multiplicities (how many times each value appears).

For these sets, the authors introduce the concept of "irreducibility." A sequence is irreducible if no term $\alpha_n$ can be perfectly represented by the sum of all subsequent terms. Specifically, the condition for irreducibility is $\alpha_n \neq (M_{n+1} + 1)\alpha_{n+1}$ for all $n$.

Theorem 5 provides the characterization for this class: a symmetric Cantor set $E(\alpha_n; M_n)$ is uniquely achievable if and only if the sequence is irreducible and the multiplicities satisfy $M_p \leq 2$ for all $p$. This means that if any term appears three or more times, or if a term is exactly the sum of the "tail" of the sequence in a specific way, the range $E$ could have been generated by a different sequence, destroying uniqueness.

![geometric-relationships-between-sequence-terms-and-their-contributions-to-the-range](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.14395v1/img-1.jpeg)

The proof for symmetric Cantor sets is considerably more involved than for central Cantor sets. It relies on a detailed geometric analysis of "E-gaps"—the open intervals missing from the achievement set. The authors demonstrate that for unique sets, the lengths and positions of these gaps provide enough information to "force" the sequence terms to match the original $(\alpha_n; M_n)$.

## Metric Invariants: The Center of Distances

To prove these characterizations, the authors utilize a tool from metric geometry called the "center of distances." For a set $S$, the center of distances $C(S)$ is defined as:

$$
C(S) = \{ |x - y| : x, y \in S \}
$$

The center of distances is an invariant that captures the set of all possible distances between points in the achievement set. A key property used in the paper is that for any achievement set $E(x_n)$, the original sequence terms (and 0) must be contained within its center of distances:

$$
\{0\} \cup \{x_n : n \in \mathbb{N}\} \subseteq C(E(x_n))
$$

The authors employ the Banakiewicz Theorem, which describes the structure of $C(S)$ for Cantor sets. When the center of distances is "minimal," it contains only the sums of the tails of the sequence. If the center of distances is not minimal, it implies specific algebraic relations between the terms, such as $x_{n-2} = 4x_n$ and $x_{n-1} = 2x_n$. By analyzing these relations, the authors can determine whether a different sequence $(y_n)$ could possibly result in the same $C(E)$, and thus the same $E$.

## The Challenge of Cantorvals

Unlike Cantor sets, which are nowhere dense, Cantorvals contain intervals but still have a "fractal-like" boundary. They occur when a sequence is neither fast convergent nor slowly convergent enough to form a single solid interval. Recovering a measure from a Cantorval range is notoriously difficult because the presence of intervals masks the individual contributions of many sequence terms.

The authors provide a new sufficient condition for the uniqueness of Cantorvals in Theorem 8. They show that if a sequence $(x_n)$ satisfies a specific oscillating relationship between its terms and remainders, the Cantorval it generates is unique. Specifically, the conditions are:

1.  $x_{2n} > r_{2n}$
2.  $x_{2n-1} < r_{2n-1}$
3.  $x_{2n} - r_{2n} > x_{2n+2} - r_{2n+2}$

This condition ensures that the "principal gaps" of the set are preserved in a way that forces any other sequence $(y_n)$ to match $(x_n)$ term by term. This theorem allows for the identification of new uniquely achievable Cantorvals beyond the previously known Guthrie-Nymann example. For instance, the sequence with terms $a_{2n-1} = \frac{1}{2^{2n-1}}$ and $a_{2n} = \frac{1}{2^{2n+1}}$ is shown to potentially fall into this category.

## Implications and Future Directions

This work completes the characterization for two major classes of measure ranges—central and symmetric Cantor sets—and provides a framework for tackling the more complex Cantorval case. The research highlights that unique achievability is sensitive to exact integer ratios between sequence terms, such as $x_n = 2x_{n+1}$ or multiplicities $M_n \geq 3$.

The paper leaves open several intriguing problems. One is the characterization of semi-fast convergent sequences that have minimal centers of distances. Another is the search for a general necessary and sufficient condition for Cantorvals. The authors hypothesize that the absence of term duplications (beyond a certain limit) and specific ratios is the key to uniqueness across all topological types.

The findings have potential relevance for any field involving the recovery of discrete distributions from aggregate data, as the achievement set is essentially the set of all possible aggregate values a discrete measure can produce. By delineating the boundaries of uniqueness, Nowakowski and Prus-Wiśniowski have clarified when the "fingerprint" of a measure (its range) is sufficient to identify it completely.
