The deployment of 5G and 6G wireless communication systems relies on high-order modulation, wideband signaling, and high-frequency millimeter-wave (mmWave) bands to meet the growing demand for throughput. These systems often employ dense antenna arrays for massive multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) and beamforming to overcome the high propagation losses at mmWave frequencies. However, to maximize energy efficiency and achieve high output power, radio-frequency (RF) power amplifiers (PAs) must operate near their saturation region. This operational necessity introduces significant hardware nonlinearities that distort signals, degrade beamforming precision, and complicate signal processing tasks.

Characterizing these nonlinearities is critical for developing "distortion-aware" algorithms. Traditional analysis often relies on Bussgang’s theorem, which provides a linear-plus-noise approximation for nonlinear systems. However, Bussgang’s theorem is primarily restricted to Gaussian input processes and fails to capture the complex, non-Gaussian nature of distortions encountered in modern high-power RF front-ends. While classical methods like Rice’s characteristic function (ch.f.) method offer a more general framework, they historically involve multiple improper integrals that are computationally expensive to evaluate numerically. This paper addresses these limitations by reformulating the characteristic function method into a simpler, summation-based numerical approach.

## The Challenge of Hardware Nonlinearity in mmWave Systems

In modern communication environments, particularly in vehicular (V2X) and integrated sensing and communications (ISAC) applications, the RF front-end must handle a complex mixture of signals and noise. When a signal passes through a memoryless nonlinearity like a power amplifier, the output is no longer a simple scaled version of the input. Instead, the nonlinearity generates harmonic components and intermodulation products.

For beamforming systems, the impact is particularly severe. Nonlinear distortions in individual antenna elements can lead to:
1.  **Beam Pattern Deviation:** The intended direction of the beam can shift or broaden.
2.  **Array-Gain Loss:** The constructive interference at the receiver is weakened.
3.  **Non-Gaussian Distortion:** Standard receivers designed for additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) lose optimality because the nonlinear noise does not follow a Gaussian distribution.

To design robust systems, engineers need a mathematical description of how specific input statistics—such as the power of a sinusoid, the variance of background noise, and the DC bias voltage—interact with the specific physical characteristics of a device like a Gallium Nitride (GaN) High Electron Mobility Transistor (HEMT).

## Reformulating the Characteristic Function Method

The core contribution of this work is the adaptation of Rice's ch.f. method. The traditional approach represents a nonlinearity $f(x)$ using its Fourier transform. This leads to the cross-correlation of the output signals being expressed as a double or triple improper integral over an infinite range, which is notoriously difficult to solve for arbitrary nonlinearities.

Instead, the authors propose representing the nonlinearity using a Fourier series expansion of its periodic extension. In practical RF systems, the input signal amplitude is always bounded. Therefore, one can define a period $2c$ that is large enough to encompass all possible input values. Outside this range, the behavior of the periodic extension is irrelevant to the system's performance.

![periodic-extension-of-nonlinearity](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.26020v1/pike_fig3.png)
*{fig:periodic-extension-of-nonlinearity}: The actual transconductance curve (solid blue) is extended into a periodic function (dashed red). This allows the nonlinearity to be represented by a discrete Fourier series rather than a continuous Fourier transform.*

For a nonlinearity $f(x)$, the Fourier series representation is:

$$
f(x) = \sum_{q=-\infty}^{\infty} F_q e^{j \frac{q\pi x}{c}}
$$

where the coefficients $F_q$ are calculated as:

$$
F_q = \frac{1}{2c} \int_{-c}^{c} f(x)e^{-j \frac{q\pi x}{c}} dx
$$

This shift from a continuous transform to a discrete series is the key to simplifying the subsequent stochastic analysis.

## Stochastic Modeling of Signals and Noise

The paper considers an input model consisting of a sinusoidal signal, a Gaussian noise process, and a DC bias. For two input branches (relevant for cross-correlation analysis), the signals are defined as:

$$
x_1(t) = P \cos(\omega t + \theta) + n_1(t) + \beta
$$
$$
x_2(t) = P \cos(\omega t + \theta) + n_2(t) + \beta
$$

In these equations, $P$ is the sinusoid amplitude, $\omega$ is the angular frequency, and $\theta$ is a random phase uniformly distributed in $(-\pi, \pi)$. The terms $n_1(t)$ and $n_2(t)$ are jointly Gaussian noise processes with correlation $\rho(\tau)$, and $\beta$ is the DC bias voltage. The goal is to find the cross-correlation of the outputs $y_1(t) = f(x_1(t))$ and $y_2(t) = g(x_2(t))$:

$$
\Psi(\tau) = E\{y_1(t+\tau)y_2(t)\}
$$

By substituting the Fourier series representations of $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ into the expectation, the problem decomposes into the product of the characteristic functions of the sinusoid, the noise, and the bias.

1.  **Noise Characteristic Function:** For Gaussian noise, the joint ch.f. is derived as an exponential function of the variances and the correlation $\rho(\tau)$:
$$
E_{n_1,n_2} \left\{ e^{j \frac{q\pi}{c} n_1(t+\tau)} e^{j \frac{r\pi}{d} n_2(t)} \right\} = e^{-\frac{\pi^2}{2c^2} [\sigma_1^2 q^2 + 2\sigma_1\sigma_2 qr\rho(\tau) + \sigma_2^2 r^2]}
$$
2.  **Sinusoid Characteristic Function:** Using Bessel function identities and Neumann’s Addition Formula, the authors express the signal contribution as a series of harmonics:
$$
\Phi_C(q,r) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \epsilon_n J_n\left(\frac{q\pi P}{c}\right) J_n\left(\frac{r\pi P}{c}\right) (-1)^n \cos(n\omega\tau)
$$
where $\epsilon_n = 1$ for $n=0$ and $\epsilon_n = 2$ for $n \neq 0$.

## The Final Analytical Solution

By combining these components, the output cross-correlation $\Psi(\tau)$ is expressed as a double summation over harmonic numbers $n$ and noise orders $k$:

$$
\Psi(\tau) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \epsilon_n h_{n,k}^2 \frac{(\sigma^2\rho(\tau))^k}{k!} \cos(n\omega\tau)
$$

The coefficient $h_{n,k}$ serves as a fundamental building block that encapsulates the interaction between the device nonlinearity and the input statistics:

$$
h_{n,k} = (j)^{n+k} \sum_{\lambda=-\infty}^{\infty} F_{\lambda} e^{j \frac{\lambda\pi\beta}{c}} J_n\left(\frac{\lambda\pi P}{c}\right) e^{-\frac{\pi^2}{2c^2}\sigma^2 \lambda^2} \left(\frac{\lambda\pi}{c}\right)^k
$$

This formulation is numerically powerful because $h_{n,k}$ can be computed efficiently using standard summation techniques. It allows researchers to isolate the power in specific harmonics (determined by $n$) and specific orders of noise distortion (determined by $k$).

## Numerical Results and Device Analysis

The authors applied this method to analyze a GaN HEMT power amplifier. By calculating the expansion coefficients $h_{n,k}^2$ for the fundamental harmonic ($n=1$), they observed how different components of the output power behave as the input signal power $P$ increases.

## Spectral Spreading and Convolution

In the frequency domain, the term $\rho^k(\tau)$ in the correlation function corresponds to the $k$-th order autoconvolution of the input noise power spectral density (PSD). As $k$ increases, the spectrum of the distortion becomes broader and smoother.

![normalized-spectral-shapes](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.26020v1/pike_fig1.png)
*{fig:normalized-spectral-shapes}: Spectral shapes ($S$) for different noise orders $k$. As $k$ increases, the spectrum spreads and becomes increasingly Gaussian-like in shape, illustrating the process of spectral regrowth in nonlinear systems.*

The $k=1$ term represents the original noise spectrum passed through the amplifier, while $k=2, 3, \dots$ represent the spectral regrowth caused by nonlinear mixing. Because the $h_{n,k}$ coefficients typically decrease rapidly for larger $k$, a small number of terms (e.g., $k \leq 3$) is often sufficient to accurately characterize the total output distortion.

## Significance and Future Impact

This numerical method provides a bridge between complex physical device modeling and high-level communication system analysis. By replacing improper integrals with summations, it makes Rice's general characteristic function method practical for real-time engineering and optimization.

The ability to accurately characterize non-Gaussian signal ensembles is essential for the next generation of wireless standards. Specifically, it enables:
*   **Optimal Receiver Design:** Designing detectors that are matched to the true, non-Gaussian statistics of the hardware distortion.
*   **Improved Beam Tracking:** Predicting how PA saturation will shift beam patterns in highly mobile V2X environments.
*   **Hardware-Algorithm Co-design:** Adjusting DC bias and power levels dynamically to balance energy efficiency with signal integrity.

While the current work focuses on memoryless nonlinearities with sinusoidal inputs, the framework is general enough to be extended to modulated signals (like OFDM) and systems with memory effects. This provides a robust foundation for future research into the fundamental limits of communication in the presence of realistic hardware impairments.
