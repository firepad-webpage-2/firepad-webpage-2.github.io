## Characterizing Cardiac Fibrosis via Spatial Graphs

Pathological myocardial fibrosis involves the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix proteins within the heart muscle. This condition is a known precursor to cardiac arrhythmias, which are disruptions in the heart's electrical rhythm that can lead to severe clinical outcomes, including sudden cardiac death. While clinicians recognize that the presence of fibrosis is dangerous, the specific geometry and topology of these fibrotic patches—the "texture" of the disease—play a fundamental role in how arrhythmogenic waves propagate through the heart.

![cardiac-fibrosis-histology-slices-and-example-patches](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13406v1/example_cardiac_fibrosis_slices_patches.png)
*{fig:cardiac-fibrosis-histology-slices-and-example-patches}: Histological sections of human hearts showing fibrotic tissue (red) embedded within the myocardium (yellow), alongside magnified examples of isolated fibrotic patches.*

Current clinical imaging techniques, such as Late Gadolinium Enhancement Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance (LGE-CMR), do not provide the resolution necessary to capture the fine, complex structures of fibrosis. Consequently, researchers often rely on qualitative visual classifications or overly simplistic models. This study addresses this limitation by developing a quantitative framework to characterize the geometry and topology of millions of isolated fibrotic patches extracted from high-resolution histological data. The core of this approach is the representation of these patches as spatial graphs—mathematical structures that capture both the physical coordinates of the tissue and its internal connectivity.

## Representing Biological Textures as Spatial Graphs

Biological structures like cardiac fibrosis are often porous, ramified, and highly irregular. Traditional shape analysis methods, which might treat an object as a continuous boundary or a fixed set of landmarks, struggle with the topological complexity of fibrotic patches. To overcome this, the researchers employ a multi-step process to transform binary images of fibrotic patches into a graph-based representation.

First, each isolated fibrotic patch is subjected to skeletonization. This image-processing technique thins the patch until it is reduced to a one-pixel-wide skeletal structure that preserves the original topology. Next, the skeleton is simplified through node coarsening, where nodes with a degree of two (those connecting exactly two edges in a straight line) are removed to reduce redundancy. The final result is a spatial graph, which is formally defined as a triplet $G = (X \times A, d_X, \mu)$.

![process-of-skeletonization-and-graph-simplification](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13406v1/example_skeletonization.png)
*{fig:process-of-skeletonization-and-graph-simplification}: The transformation of an original fibrotic patch into a simplified spatial graph through skeletonization and node reduction.*

In this definition, $(X, d_X)$ represents the metric graph where $X$ is the set of nodes and $d_X$ is the shortest path distance between nodes. The term $A$ denotes the spatial coordinates of the nodes in Euclidean space (with $p = 2$ for 2D histological slices). Finally, $\mu$ is a probability measure that assigns weights to the nodes, typically represented as a histogram:

$$\mu = \sum_{i=1}^N h_i \delta_{x_i,a_i}$$

where $h_i = 1/N$ represents a uniform weight for each node. This representation allows the researchers to treat each fibrotic patch as an object in a mathematical space that accounts for both its physical layout and its internal branching structure.

## The Fused Gromov-Wasserstein Metric

A significant challenge in analyzing spatial graphs is comparing two structures that may have different numbers of nodes and edges. Standard graph distances often ignore spatial positions, while standard Euclidean distances ignore connectivity. To bridge this gap, the study utilizes the Fused Gromov-Wasserstein (FGW) distance. This metric optimizes a coupling plan $\pi$ between the nodes of two graphs to minimize a combined cost function.

The cost function for the coupling includes a spatial component (how far apart matched nodes are in physical space) and a topological component (how much the internal distances between pairs of nodes differ between the two graphs). The total cost $E(\pi)$ is defined as:

$$E(\pi) = (1-\alpha) \sum_{i,j} \|a_i - b_j\|^2 \pi_{ij} + \alpha \sum_{i,j,k,l} |d_X(i,k) - d_Y(j,l)|^2 \pi_{ij} \pi_{kl}$$

Here, $\alpha$ is a weighting parameter that balances the importance of spatial coordinates versus graph topology. The term $\|a_i - b_j\|^2$ measures the squared Euclidean distance between the coordinates of node $i$ in the first graph and node $j$ in the second. The term $|d_X(i,k) - d_Y(j,l)|^2$ measures the discrepancy between the shortest path distances of node pairs $(i, k)$ and $(j, l)$.

To ensure the comparison is biologically meaningful, the researchers introduced a pre-alignment step to achieve rotation invariance. Before calculating the distance, each spatial graph is rotated using Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to align its principal axes of variation. This ensures that two patches with the same shape but different orientations are recognized as similar.

## Statistical Framework for Spatial Data

With a defined metric space, the researchers can apply statistical tools to understand the distribution of fibrotic textures across different patients and heart regions. They employ Anderson's ANOVA (also known as PERMANOVA), a non-parametric method that tests whether the centers of different groups of objects are significantly different based solely on their pairwise distances.

To visualize these distributions, the study introduces "DepthPlot," a tool designed for object data in metric spaces. It utilizes the Metric Half Space (MHS) depth, which assigns a value to each object indicating how "central" or "representative" it is within a sample. A high depth value indicates a graph that is near the "median" of the distribution, while a low depth value indicates a peripheral or outlier structure.

![depthplot-visualization-for-patient-2](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13406v1/dephplot_p2.png)
*{fig:depthplot-visualization-for-patient-2}: A DepthPlot for Patient 2, showing the distribution of spatial graph depths and representative patches at different depth quantiles.*

The DepthPlot arranges these depth values on a cone-like structure, providing a visual summary of the empirical distribution. By looking at the representative patches at different quantiles (e.g., the 0.75 quantile or the maximum depth), researchers can qualitatively interpret what a "typical" fibrotic patch looks like for a specific patient or region.

## Findings: Patient Specificity and Genetic Links

The application of this framework to a dataset of 15,684 patches from 10 patients revealed that fibrotic textures are highly patient-specific. PERMANOVA tests rejected the null hypothesis of identical distributions in nearly all pairwise patient comparisons.

![pairwise-permanova-results-for-all-patients](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13406v1/test_patients_perm_paper.png)
*{fig:pairwise-permanova-results-for-all-patients}: Matrix of p-values from pairwise comparisons between patients, where low values (white) indicate significant differences in fibrotic texture.*

One striking finding was the similarity between Patient 3 and Patient 8. The statistical test failed to reject the null hypothesis ($p = 0.81$), suggesting their fibrotic textures were indistinguishable. Clinically, both patients were found to share the same genetic mutation (LMNA). In contrast, Patient 2, who had a different mutation (RBM20), showed significantly different fibrotic structures. The representative "median" graphs for Patient 3 and 8 were more complex and fragmented, while Patient 2's median structures were simpler and more linear.

The study also benchmarked the spatial graph approach against a simpler method that extracted basic features like area, perimeter, and Euler characteristic. The simpler method failed to detect the differences between patients that were clearly identified by the spatial graph framework, demonstrating the increased sensitivity of the metric space approach.

## Spatial Heterogeneity within the Heart

Beyond patient-to-patient variation, the researchers explored how fibrosis changes based on its location within the heart. This was done through vertical exploration (comparing horizontal slices from the top to the bottom of the ventricles) and horizontal exploration (comparing regions based on their distance from the outer heart wall).

Vertical exploration showed that the patient-specific characteristics are remarkably consistent throughout the height of the heart. If two patients were different at one level, they remained different across all slices. Horizontal exploration divided the heart wall into three zones: the Sub-Endocardium $[0, 0.3)$, the Mid-Myocardium $[0.3, 0.6)$, and the Sub-Epicardium $[0.6, 1)$.

![horizontal-spatial-exploration-results](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13406v1/horizontal_exploration.png)
*{fig:horizontal-spatial-exploration-results}: Analysis of fibrotic texture variation across different depths of the ventricular wall.*

For Patient 2, the fibrotic texture differed significantly across all three horizontal regions. For Patients 3 and 8, the differences were primarily localized in the Sub-Epicardium, while the inner regions remained statistically similar. This suggests that while genetic factors provide a global blueprint for fibrosis, local environmental factors within the heart wall also influence the final geometry of the tissue.

## Significance and Future Impact

This work establishes a rigorous mathematical and statistical foundation for analyzing complex, porous biological structures. By treating fibrotic patches as spatial graphs and defining a robust metric space, the researchers have moved beyond qualitative descriptions to a quantitative understanding of disease texture.

The methodology has immediate implications for cardiac modeling. By providing realistic distributions of fibrotic geometry, these results can be used to improve computational simulations of heart rhythms, potentially leading to better predictions of arrhythmia risk. Furthermore, the link between specific genetic mutations and fibrotic texture suggests that this framework could eventually serve as a diagnostic tool, helping clinicians identify underlying cardiac conditions through the quantitative analysis of tissue structure. The flexibility of the spatial graph representation means this approach could also be adapted to other fields, such as analyzing vascular networks, lung tissue, or any other ramified biological system.
