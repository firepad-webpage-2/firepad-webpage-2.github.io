## The Challenge of New Product Demand Estimation

Estimating the demand function—the relationship between the price of a good and the quantity consumers are willing to purchase—is a cornerstone of economic analysis. For established products, researchers typically rely on historical market data, observing how quantities fluctuate in response to price changes. However, this approach faces two major hurdles. First, for "new products" that have not yet entered the market, no historical transaction data exists. Second, even when data is available, observed prices and quantities are outcomes of market equilibrium, meaning they are influenced by both supply and demand simultaneously. This creates an endogeneity problem where the price is correlated with the error term in statistical models, potentially leading to biased results.

Traditional methods for addressing these issues involve complex econometric techniques like instrumental variables, which require finding external factors that affect supply but not demand. In the context of new products, researchers often turn to surveys that ask potential customers how much they would buy at specific prices. This research proposes an alternative framework: using a consumer’s Willingness to Pay (WTP) as the primary primitive for deriving the demand function. By starting with the maximum price an individual is willing to pay rather than observed market transactions, the method circumvents the endogeneity trap and provides a theoretically grounded path to forecasting demand for goods that do not yet exist.

## Conceptual Framework and Behavioral Assumptions

The core principle of this approach is that a market's demand curve is an aggregation of individual WTP values. The study builds this framework on three fundamental behavioral assumptions. First, it assumes a "Unit Purchase" scenario where each consumer intends to buy one unit of the good. While this is the starting point, the framework can be extended to accommodate multiple unit purchases. Second, it posits that WTP is a random variable, denoted as $W$, which varies across the population. This WTP is influenced by a vector of other factors $Z$, such as individual income, preferences, or the prices of substitute goods. This relationship is characterized by a conditional cumulative distribution function (CDF), $F_{W|Z}(w)$.

The third assumption is the "Purchase Condition": a consumer will buy the product if the market price $p$ is less than or equal to their WTP $W$. If the market size is $L$ and the influencing factors $Z$ are discrete, the total quantity demanded $q$ at price $p$ given specific characteristics $z$ is expressed as:

$$q = [1 - F_{W|Z=z}(p)] \Pr(Z = z) L$$

In cases where the influencing factors are continuous and described by a probability density function $f_Z(z)$, the demand function becomes:

$$q = [1 - F_{W|Z}(p)] f_Z(z) L$$

Because a CDF is by definition non-decreasing, the term $[1 - F_{W|Z}(p)]$ must be non-increasing with respect to price. This ensures that the resulting demand function inherently satisfies the Law of Demand—that quantity demanded falls as price rises—without requiring additional constraints.

## Mathematical Mapping of WTP to Demand Forms

A significant contribution of this work is the demonstration that standard functional forms of demand used in economics are actually direct consequences of specific WTP distributions. This provides a theoretical justification for choosing one model over another based on how consumer preferences are likely distributed.

If the WTP in a population is distributed uniformly, the resulting demand function is linear, taking the form $q = \beta_0 + \beta_1 p$. A uniform distribution implies that consumers are spread evenly across a range of maximum price points. In contrast, if WTP follows an exponential distribution, the demand function becomes semi-logarithmic linear:

$$\ln(q) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 p + \beta_2 \log(z)$$

Furthermore, if the WTP follows a Pareto distribution—often used to model wealth and income distributions—the demand function simplifies to a logarithmic linear form:

$$\ln(q) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log(p) + \beta_2 \log(z)$$

For more complex or unknown WTP distributions, the paper suggests using Taylor expansions to approximate the demand function. A first-order expansion yields a linear approximation, while a second-order expansion can capture quadratic relationships. This flexibility allows the model to remain applicable even when the underlying distribution of consumer preferences is not perfectly understood, provided the analysis focuses on a localized range of prices.

## The Empirical Estimation Procedure

To move from theory to practice, the research outlines a five-step estimation procedure designed for ease of implementation by researchers and practitioners.

1.  **Data Acquisition**: The process begins by collecting paired data of WTP ($w_i$) and associated covariates ($z_i$) for a sample of $n$ individuals. This data is typically gathered through experimental or survey methods such as conjoint analysis or the Contingent Valuation Method (CVM).
2.  **CDF Estimation**: The researcher non-parametrically estimates the conditional CDF of WTP, $\hat{F}_{W|Z}(w)$, using the empirical distribution of the collected data.
3.  **Density Estimation**: The probability density function $\hat{f}_Z(z)$ (or mass function for discrete variables) for the influencing factors is estimated.
4.  **Quantity Construction**: For a set of hypothetical prices $p$—usually the distinct WTP values observed in the sample—the researcher constructs the estimated quantity of demand:
    $$\hat{q} = [1 - \hat{F}_{W|Z}(p)] \hat{f}_Z(z) L$$
    Here, $L$ represents the total potential market size.
5.  **Parameter Estimation**: Finally, Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression is applied to fit the chosen functional form (linear, log-linear, etc.) to the constructed $\hat{q}$ values.

The study proves that the resulting estimator $\hat{\beta}$ is consistent. This consistency is anchored in the Glivenko-Cantelli Theorem, which guarantees that as the sample size $n$ increases, the empirical CDF will converge to the true CDF. Because the quantity demanded is constructed from these converging distributions, the OLS estimates will asymptotically reach the true parameters of the demand function.

## Consistency and Resilience to Mis-reporting

One of the primary concerns with survey-based data is the accuracy of reported WTP. Consumers may strategically understate their WTP or simply struggle to provide an exact number in a hypothetical scenario. The paper addresses this by analyzing how reporting errors affect the estimation of the price coefficient $\beta$.

The theoretical analysis reveals that the procedure is surprisingly robust under certain conditions. For instance, if the true underlying WTP is uniformly distributed, the price coefficient remains accurate even if there is arbitrary mis-reporting, because the error term becomes a constant that does not vary with price. Additionally, if the probability density of the true WTP is relatively "flat"—meaning there isn't a sharp peak of consumers at one specific price point—small, independent reporting errors will not significantly bias the estimated price coefficient. This finding suggests that local approximations of the demand curve are more resilient to measurement error than global ones, especially in diverse markets where preferences are spread out.

## Validation through Simulation and Case Study

The paper validates the proposed method through both Monte Carlo simulations and a real-world application. The simulations tested the procedure against various distributions (uniform, exponential, Pareto, and log-normal) under two conditions: perfect reporting and small reporting errors. The results confirmed the theory: for uniform and exponential distributions, the estimated price coefficients were highly accurate (within 5-6% of the true values). For the Pareto distribution, the error was slightly higher but still within a reasonable range, though it increased more significantly when reporting errors were introduced.

In the empirical application, the author estimated the demand for coffee among staff at James Cook University. Using a sample of 101 respondents, the WTP data was collected via a survey with randomized starting prices to avoid "anchoring bias." The analysis showed that most respondents were willing to pay between $\$4$ and $\$6$ for a coffee, with demand becoming very sparse above $\$10$. 

The semi-logarithmic linear model provided the best fit for this data, yielding a price elasticity of -0.487 in the log-linear version. This indicates that coffee demand in this setting is relatively inelastic; a 1% increase in price would lead to a less than 0.5% decrease in the quantity demanded, which aligns with the perception of coffee as a daily necessity for many.

## Broader Impact and Applications

This WTP-based demand estimation framework offers significant utility across three main domains. In **business**, it provides a structured way to forecast the success of new products before they are launched. By using surveys or auctions to gauge WTP, companies can set prices and plan production levels with greater confidence, reducing the risk of costly market failures.

In **public policy**, the method is valuable for non-market valuation. When governments need to decide whether to invest in public goods—like a new park, a health program, or carbon emission reductions—they cannot look at market prices because these goods aren't sold in traditional stores. This framework allows policymakers to estimate the "public demand" for such services by aggregating individual WTP, facilitating more accurate cost-benefit analyses.

Finally, in **education**, the simplicity of the five-step procedure makes it an ideal pedagogical tool. Students can conduct their own WTP surveys for simple goods and use standard spreadsheet software to derive a demand curve that is guaranteed to follow the Law of Demand. This hands-on approach helps bridge the gap between abstract economic theory and practical data analysis. While the current model focuses on static demand, it sets the stage for future research into dynamic demand estimation, particularly for durable goods where consumer decisions are spread across time.
