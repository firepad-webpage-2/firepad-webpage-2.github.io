## Strategic Competition in Service Order

In many competitive markets, the price and quality of service are not the only variables firms use to attract customers. When prices are fixed or capacity is constrained, the way a firm organizes its waitlist becomes a critical strategic instrument. Industries ranging from healthcare, where patients choose providers based on appointment lead times, to high-end manufacturing, where firms like Boeing or Airbus compete on delivery slots, demonstrate that the order of service can dictate market share.

Traditional queueing theory often focuses on maximizing social welfare—minimizing the total time everyone spends waiting. However, the work by Ashlagi and Root explores a different perspective: a competitive one. In this study, the authors investigate whether a service provider can strategically alter its service rule to capture a majority of the demand without increasing its capacity or lowering its prices. A central question is whether the ubiquitous First-Come-First-Served (FCFS) rule, often perceived as the fairest and most efficient baseline, is vulnerable to strategic competitors.

The research introduces the "Ledger rule," a sophisticated service strategy that allows a queue to beat FCFS by strategically managing "gaps" in its waitlist. This approach challenges the intuition that attracting more demand inevitably leads to higher congestion that drives customers away. Instead, by reallocating waiting times among its users, a queue can maintain a competitive advantage in the eyes of new arrivals.

## The Competitive Queueing Model

The authors model a market with two competing service providers, Queue A and Queue B. Both have identical service capabilities: they process agents at a rate of 1 according to an exponential distribution. Potential customers (agents) arrive at the system according to a Poisson process with an arrival rate $\lambda \in (0, 2)$. The upper bound of 2 ensures that the combined capacity of both queues can handle the incoming traffic, preventing the system from growing indefinitely.

Agents in this model are rational and purely self-interested. Before joining a queue, an arriving agent observes the current state of both waitlists and calculates their expected waiting time. They then join the queue that offers the shorter expected wait. If the expected waiting times are identical, the agent chooses between A and B with equal probability. Once an agent joins a queue, they cannot leave or switch (no reneging or jockeying).

In the baseline scenario, both queues use the FCFS rule. Under FCFS, an agent's expected waiting time in a queue with $b$ existing agents is simply:
$$
E[W] = \sum_{j=0}^{b} 1 = b + 1
$$
Because the service rate is 1, each agent ahead of the new arrival adds exactly 1 unit of time to the expected wait. In this symmetric case, arrivals split equally between the two queues, and each captures $\lambda / 2$ of the demand.

## The Ledger Rule: Gaining a Strategic Edge

The core contribution of this work is the discovery of service rules that allow a provider to capture more than half the market share against an FCFS competitor. The most prominent example provided is the **Ledger rule**. 

Unlike FCFS, which fills slots in a rigid sequence, the Ledger rule strategically places arrivals into specific slots. The "state" of the Ledger queue is defined by which slots (0, 1, 2, ...) are currently occupied. When a new agent arrives, the Ledger queue (let's call it Queue A) looks at its competitor (Queue B), which is using FCFS. If Queue B has $b$ agents, its expected wait is $b+1$. Queue A then tries to assign the new arrival to a slot $j$ such that the expected wait in A is less than or equal to $b+1$.

The strategic innovation of the Ledger rule lies in its "Arrival-placement" and "Service-selection" logic:
1.  **Placement:** It assigns an arriving agent to the *highest* possible empty slot $j$ that still satisfies the condition $j \leq b$. By filling a higher slot, the queue leaves lower slots empty for future strategic use.
2.  **Service:** It typically serves the agent in the lowest occupied slot (the "preemptive" version) or ensures that once an agent reaches the front of the line, they are not bumped (the "non-preemptive" version).
3.  **Gaps:** This placement strategy creates "gaps" in the waitlist. These gaps are essentially placeholders that allow the Ledger queue to offer a competitive wait time to a new arrival even if its total number of agents is higher than the FCFS queue.

By utilizing these gaps, the Ledger rule effectively "saves" its capacity to attract agents at moments when the FCFS queue is slightly more crowded. Over time, this results in the Ledger rule attracting a strictly greater share of the total arrival rate than the $50\%$ baseline.

## Quantifying the Advantage

Through a combination of mathematical proofs and simulations, the authors demonstrate the effectiveness of this strategy. They establish that for any arrival rate $\lambda \in (0, 2)$, the Ledger rule captures a market share strictly greater than $\lambda / 2$.

Interestingly, the advantage is non-monotone; it is small when the system is very empty (few opportunities to strategize) or very full (both queues are almost always busy).

## Theoretical Bounds: How Much Can FCFS Be Beaten?

While the Ledger rule proves that FCFS can be beaten, the researchers also sought to determine the maximum possible advantage any service rule could achieve. They established two significant upper bounds on market share:

1.  **General Work-Conserving Rules:** No service rule, no matter how complex or strategic, can capture more than $1 / \sqrt{2} \approx 0.707$ (roughly $70.7\%$) of the arrivals when competing against FCFS.
2.  **Non-Preemptive Rules:** If the rule is restricted such that agents cannot be moved further back in line once they start waiting (non-preemptive), the limit is even tighter: $1 / \Phi \approx 0.618$ (roughly $61.8\%$), where $\Phi = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.618$ is the golden ratio.

![market-share-bounds](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11710v1/img-1.jpeg)
*{fig:market-share-bounds}: Theoretical upper bounds on the market share any service rule can capture against FCFS as a function of the arrival rate.*

These bounds, illustrated in {fig:market-share-bounds}, provide a fundamental limit to competition in queueing systems. They suggest that while FCFS is not optimal for a provider seeking to maximize demand, it is robust enough that a competitor cannot completely dominate the market through service rules alone.

## Equilibrium and the Price of Commitment

The study also examines what happens when *both* service providers are strategic. If one queue adopts the Ledger rule and gains an advantage, the other will likely respond by changing its own rule. This leads to the analysis of Nash equilibria—states where neither queue has an incentive to change its strategy.

A key finding is that the outcome depends heavily on **commitment**.
*   **With Commitment:** If queues can publicly commit to a service rule (e.g., publishing their scheduling algorithm), FCFS-FCFS is *not* an equilibrium. Either queue could switch to a Ledger-style rule to steal market share. The authors identify a new equilibrium for low arrival rates ($\lambda < 1/2$) called **Protected LCFS**. In this rule, the very first person in the queue is "protected" and served next, but anyone who arrives after them is served in a Last-Come-First-Served (LCFS) order until the protected agent's service is complete. 
*   **Without Commitment:** If queues decide who to serve only at the moment a server becomes free, FCFS-FCFS *is* a stable equilibrium. In this case, agents simply route to the shorter queue, and the specific order of service does not influence future arrivals because the provider cannot "promise" a specific future service order.

## The Cost to Society: Welfare Implications

Perhaps the most surprising result of the research is the impact of competition on the agents themselves. In a standard symmetric FCFS-FCFS system, agents benefit from the shortest possible average wait times. However, when queues compete strategically, they move away from FCFS toward rules like Protected LCFS.

While Protected LCFS results in a $50/50$ market share split (preserving symmetry), it does so at the cost of higher average waiting times for everyone.

This "efficiency tax" arises because strategic rules prioritize market share acquisition over the efficient clearing of the waitlist.

## Conclusion

The work of Ashlagi and Root demonstrates that service order is far from a neutral operational choice; it is a powerful strategic lever. By designing rules like the Ledger rule, a provider can successfully "beat" FCFS and capture a larger slice of the market. However, this competitive advantage comes with a cautionary note: when all providers become strategic, the resulting equilibrium often leads to a system where everyone—both the providers and the customers—is worse off due to increased average congestion. This research provides a new framework for understanding how waitlists function as competitive landscapes in modern service economies.
