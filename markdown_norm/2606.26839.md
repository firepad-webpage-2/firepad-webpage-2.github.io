## Enhancing Visual Navigation through Structured Representations

Vision-based robotic navigation relies on an agent's ability to interpret complex visual scenes to make precise movement decisions. A common approach is end-to-end imitation learning, where a robot learns to map visual observations directly to actions by observing expert demonstrations. While effective, this method often provides only indirect supervision to the robot's vision encoder—the component responsible for "seeing" and understanding the environment. Because the encoder is only guided by the final action loss, it may learn representations that are "action-agnostic," focusing on visually striking but task-irrelevant details like lighting or textures rather than the structural layout necessary for navigation.

![orion-framework](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.26839v1/x3.png)
*{fig:orion-framework}: The ORION framework consists of two stages: (1) Pretraining the vision encoder using Ordinal Neural Collapse (ONC) objectives to organize representations along an ordinal axis, and (2) End-to-end fine-tuning of the full navigation policy.*

This research introduces ORION (Ordinal Neural Collapse for Visual Navigation), a method designed to provide a stronger, task-aware prior for the vision encoder. The core idea is to explicitly organize the encoder's latent space—the mathematical space where it stores visual features—according to the natural ordering of navigation actions. By enforcing a geometric structure known as Ordinal Neural Collapse (ONC), ORION ensures that the encoder learns to distinguish between different goal-relative directions (e.g., turning far left versus turning slightly left) in a consistent and organized manner.

## The Challenge: Indirect Supervision and Action Ambiguity

In standard imitation learning, the vision encoder $f_\theta$ translates an image into a numerical feature vector. This vector is then used by an action decoder to predict the next move. Recent advanced models use diffusion-based decoders, denoted as $a_t = D_\psi(z_t, \dots)$, which can represent complex, multimodal action distributions. This means the robot can consider multiple potential paths at once.

However, a major drawback of joint optimization (training everything at once) is that the encoder receives no direct information about the geometry of the task. In navigation, the goal-relative direction is an "ordinal" variable—it has a natural sequence from far left to far right. Without explicit guidance, the encoder may treat different directions as isolated categories, or worse, fail to separate them effectively in the feature space.

This lack of structure often manifests as "temporal flickering" at decision points like intersections. Because the underlying visual features are ambiguous, the action decoder may produce wildly different path predictions for nearly identical images, causing the robot to jitter or make inconsistent turns. ORION addresses this by establishing a clear geometric relationship between visual features and the ordinal nature of steering.

## Ordinal Neural Collapse (ONC)

The concept of Neural Collapse (NC) originally described a phenomenon where the features of different classes in a trained classifier converge to a perfectly symmetric shape called a Simplex Equiangular Tight Frame (ETF). In this shape, every class is equidistant from every other class. While useful for categorical tasks (like identifying "dog" vs. "car"), this geometry is suboptimal for navigation. A "far left" turn is semantically closer to a "near left" turn than it is to a "far right" turn.

ORION leverages Ordinal Neural Collapse, a variant where features are encouraged to collapse onto a one-dimensional axis in the latent space. Along this axis, class means are arranged sequentially according to their ranks. This captures the continuous and ordered nature of steering commands.

To implement this, ORION partitions the agent's field of view into $K=5$ angular bins (Far Left, Near Left, Straight, Near Right, Far Right) based on the goal-relative heading $\alpha_t \in [-90^\circ, 90^\circ]$. The goal of the pretraining stage is to map images $o_t^n$ and $g^n$ into features $h_t = f_\theta(o_t) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ such that these features follow the ONC geometry.

## The ORION Pretraining Stage

The ORION framework uses a two-stage training process. In the first stage, the vision encoder is pretrained using three specific loss functions that enforce the ordinal structure:

$$
L_{\text{ORION}} = \lambda_{\text{cdnv}} L_{\text{CDNV}} + \lambda_{\text{ord}} L_{\text{ord}} + \lambda_{\text{orth}} L_{\text{orth}}
$$

### 1. Class-Distance Normalized Variance ($L_{\text{CDNV}}$)
This loss promotes class separability by minimizing the ratio of within-class variance to the distance between class means. It ensures that features belonging to the same direction cluster tightly together while different direction clusters move apart.

$$
L_{\text{CDNV}} = \frac{1}{|P|} \sum_{(k,k') \in P} \frac{\sigma^2_k + \sigma^2_{k'}}{2 ||\tilde{\mu}_k - \tilde{\mu}_{k'}||^2}
$$

where $P = \{(k, k') : k < k'\}$ is the set of class pairs, $\sigma^2_k = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{i=1}^{N_k} ||h_i - \mu_k||^2$ is the variance of class $k$, and $\tilde{\mu}_k = \mu_k - \bar{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents the centered class mean relative to the global mean $\bar{\mu} = \frac{1}{K} \sum \mu_k$.

### 2. Supervised Ordinal Projection ($L_{\text{ord}}$)
This loss explicitly maps the class means onto a one-dimensional "ordinal axis." Each class is assigned a target position $t_k = k - \frac{K-1}{2} \in \mathbb{R}$ based on its rank. The loss encourages the projection $p_k = \tilde{\mu}_k^\top w$ of each class mean onto a learned direction vector $w \in \mathbb{R}^d, ||w|| = 1$ to match these targets.

$$
L_{\text{ord}} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} (p_k - t_k)^2
$$

### 3. Orthogonal Compactness ($L_{\text{orth}}$)
To make the representation truly one-dimensional for the task, this loss penalizes any feature variance that exists in directions perpendicular to the ordinal axis $w$.

$$
L_{\text{orth}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{t=1}^{N} ||(I - ww^\top)(h_t - \mu_{y_t})||^2
$$

By combining these, ORION forces the vision encoder to strip away task-irrelevant visual information and focus on the spatial features that determine the robot's heading relative to its goal.

## Experimental Evaluation

The researchers evaluated ORION in both simulated environments (using the Stanford 2D-3D-S and CitySim datasets) and challenging real-world settings (Indoor, Campus, and Field).

![simulated-environments](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.26839v1/x4.png)
*{fig:simulated-environments}: Sample navigation paths in simulated indoor and outdoor environments. ORION (red) achieves more direct and successful paths compared to standard diffusion-based methods.*

### Simulation Performance
In the Gazebo CitySim environment, which features complex street layouts, ORION showed significant improvements over the baseline NoMaD architecture. On "Multi-Intersection" routes—where the robot must make several correct turns in a row—ORION achieved a 74% success rate, compared to just 48% for the baseline. 

A critical finding was the reduction in "heading jerk," a measure of how much the robot's steering fluctuates. ORION reduced this jitter by over 70%, indicating much smoother and more confident navigation. This confirms that the structured features help the action decoder make more consistent decisions.

### Real-World Testing
The performance gains translated effectively to physical robots. In a real-world campus environment with complex intersections, ORION successfully reached its goal in 80% of trials, while the baseline struggled with temporal flickering at decision points, often turning the wrong way or getting stuck. In a "Field" environment with uneven terrain and sparse visual landmarks, ORION achieved nearly 49% progress toward the goal, outperforming other methods that were easily distracted by task-irrelevant visual features.

## Analysis of Learned Representations

To understand why ORION works, the researchers analyzed the distribution of features after training. In the baseline NoMaD model, visual features for different directions often overlapped significantly, making it hard for the policy to distinguish a "near left" turn from "straight." 

In contrast, ORION's features showed clear separation. When projected onto the ordinal axis, the distributions for each class formed distinct, non-overlapping peaks arranged in the correct order. This structure was preserved even after the second stage of fine-tuning, proving that ORION provides a stable and lasting geometric prior.

## Conclusion

ORION demonstrates that explicitly structuring the visual representation space can significantly improve the performance and reliability of robotic navigation. By moving beyond simple "end-to-end" training and imposing an ordinal geometric prior, the system learns to ignore visual distractors and focus on the core spatial relationships needed for control. 

The success of ORION across varied simulated and real-world environments suggests that task-specific geometric priors are a powerful tool for embodied AI. While this work focused on the ordinal nature of steering, the principle of aligning latent geometry with task structure could likely be extended to other robotic tasks, such as manipulation or complex multi-objective navigation.
