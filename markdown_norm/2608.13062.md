## Introduction to Moiré Magnetic Chaos

The study of chaos in magnetic systems has traditionally focused on macroscopic variables—such as the total magnetization of a sample or the specific positions of magnetic textures like vortices and skyrmions. In these classical cases, the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem dictates that a system must possess at least three dynamical degrees of freedom to exhibit autonomous chaotic behavior. Because simple magnetic systems often lack these inherent degrees of freedom, researchers typically apply external driving forces, such as spin-transfer torques or time-varying magnetic fields, to push the system into a chaotic regime.

*{fig:figure-1-moir-magnetic-textures-and-frustration-in-twisted-bilayer-cri3}: (a) Map of the interlayer exchange coupling showing the moiré superlattice with isolated antiferromagnetic (AFM) patches. (b) Magnetization maps of the top and bottom layers showing the binary polarization of these patches within a ferromagnetic background.*

This research explores a different paradigm: the emergence of magnetic chaos in twisted two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals magnets, specifically twisted bilayer $\text{CrI}_3$. When two layers of these materials are stacked with a small twist angle, such as $\theta = 1.61^\circ$, they form a moiré superlattice. This superlattice creates a spatial modulation of the interlayer exchange coupling, leading to alternating regions of ferromagnetic (FM) and antiferromagnetic (AFM) alignment. 

The core finding of this work is that the high-dimensional frustration inherent in these moiré patterns allows for the emergence of "moiré magnetic chaos." This chaos arises autonomously, without any external driving force, during the system's relaxation toward its equilibrium state. This phenomenon is rooted in the mesoscopic domain variables created by the moiré structure, providing a vast manifold of nearly degenerate metastable states.

## The Physical Model of Twisted Bilayer CrI3

To investigate these dynamics, the researchers employed a Heisenberg spin model to describe the magnetic energy of the twisted bilayer system. The energy $E$ is defined by several competing interactions:

$$E = -\sum_{\langle i,j \rangle} J_{\text{intra}, ij} \vec{S}_i \cdot \vec{S}_j - \sum_i A (S_i^z)^2 - \sum_{i,j} J_{\text{inter}, ij} \vec{S}_i \cdot \vec{S}_j + E_{\text{dipolar}}$$

In this expression, $J_{\text{intra}} > 0$ represents the intralayer ferromagnetic exchange that keeps spins within each layer aligned. The term $A > 0$ denotes single-ion anisotropy, which favors an out-of-plane orientation for the magnetic moments. The critical term is $J_{\text{inter}, ij}$, the interlayer exchange coupling. Unlike in standard bilayers, this coupling varies spatially based on the local stacking geometry of the two twisted layers. The resulting map shows a dominant FM background interspersed with isolated AFM "patches" arranged in a hexagonal moiré lattice. 

The dynamics of these spins are simulated using the Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert (LLG) equation, which describes how the magnetization $\vec{m}$ evolves over time in response to an effective magnetic field:

$$\frac{\partial \vec{m}}{\partial t} = -\gamma \vec{m} \times \vec{H}_{\text{eff}} + \alpha \vec{m} \times \frac{\partial \vec{m}}{\partial t}$$

Here, the first term describes the precession of the magnetic moments, and the second term accounts for the damping (controlled by the parameter $\alpha$) that leads the system to settle into a metastable state. The complexity of the $J_{\text{inter}, ij}$ map introduces local magnetic frustration at each AFM patch. Each patch faces a choice: it can align its top and bottom layers antiferromagnetically to satisfy the local coupling, but doing so creates a boundary of unfavorable alignment with the surrounding FM background. This leads to two degenerate "polarizations" for each patch, creating an exponentially large number of possible global magnetic configurations.

## Quantifying Sensitivity to Initial Conditions

A hallmark of chaotic systems is "damage spreading," where a tiny change in the initial state leads to a vastly different final outcome. To test this in twisted $\text{CrI}_3$, the authors performed micromagnetic simulations at $T = 0 \text{ K}$. They initialized the system in a nearly uniform out-of-plane state ($m_z = 0.99$) and allowed it to relax. They then repeated the simulation, but with a single spin in the center of the grid tilted by an extremely small angle $\epsilon$ (ranging from $10^{-8}$ to $10^{-3}$).

The difference between the reference trajectory and the perturbed trajectory was measured using the root-mean-square (RMS) magnetization difference:

$$\delta m(t) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_s} \sum_{i=1}^{N_s} |\vec{m}_i(t) - \vec{m}'_i(t)|^2}$$

The results were striking. Even the smallest perturbation ($\epsilon = 10^{-8}$) caused the two trajectories to diverge rapidly. By the time the systems reached a metastable state, roughly half of the magnetic domains in the perturbed system had flipped their polarization compared to the reference. This was quantified by a Hamming fraction $H/N_d$ near 0.49, indicating that the two states were as different as if the domain orientations had been chosen by independent coin flips.

![figure-2-damage-spreading-and-sensitivity-to-initial-conditions](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13062v1/fig2_damage_lyapunov.png)
*{fig:figure-2-damage-spreading-and-sensitivity-to-initial-conditions}: (a, b) Difference maps showing how a single-spin perturbation causes widespread changes in domain patterns. (c) The Lyapunov exponent and magnetization difference as a function of the perturbation magnitude $\epsilon$.*

To formally characterize this sensitivity, the authors calculated the finite-amplitude Lyapunov exponent:

$$\lambda(\epsilon) = \frac{1}{t} \ln \left( \frac{\delta m(t)}{\epsilon} \right)$$

While this value appeared to decrease with larger perturbations due to the saturation of the magnetization difference in a bounded space, a more rigorous calculation using the Benettin algorithm yielded a maximal Lyapunov exponent $\lambda_{\text{max}} = 290.8 \pm 0.1 \text{ ns}^{-1}$. This corresponds to a Lyapunov time of $\tau_\lambda \approx 3.4 \text{ ps}$, signifying incredibly rapid divergence.

## Statistical Independence of Moiré Domains

If the domain patterns are truly selected through a chaotic process, the resulting configurations across many different relaxation runs should appear statistically random and uncorrelated. The researchers performed 1000 independent relaxation runs, each starting from the same global state but with distinct, infinitesimal random in-plane fluctuations.

![figure-3-statistical-distribution-of-magnetic-domain-patterns](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13062v1/fig3_rng.png)
*{fig:figure-3-statistical-distribution-of-magnetic-domain-patterns}: (a) A visualization of the binary domain states across 1000 runs. (b) The distribution of pairwise Hamming distances, which matches a binomial distribution for fair coins. (c) Pairwise correlation coefficients centered at zero.*

For each of the $N = 157$ internal AFM patches, the researchers measured the probability of the patch adopting a specific polarization. They calculated the binary Shannon entropy $H_n$:

$$H_n = -p_n \ln p_n - (1 - p_n) \ln (1 - p_n)$$

The measured entropy was $99.9\%$ of the theoretical maximum, meaning each patch acts as a perfectly unbiased random bit. Furthermore, the distribution of Hamming distances between all pairs of configurations perfectly matched an ideal binomial distribution. This confirms that there are no significant spatial correlations between the patches; each domain chooses its state independently of its neighbors. This lack of correlation, verified by Pearson correlation coefficients $\rho_{ij}$ centered at zero, is a direct consequence of the flat energy landscape and the weak coupling between the mesoscopic degrees of freedom in the moiré lattice.

## Thermal Robustness and Field Persistence

For moiré magnetic chaos to be experimentally observable or technologically useful, the chaotically selected patterns must be stable against environmental noise. The authors tested the robustness of these configurations against both temperature and external magnetic fields.

Using stochastic LLG simulations (Langevin dynamics), they heated a relaxed configuration from $0 \text{ K}$ to $29 \text{ K}$. The stability was measured using a domain order parameter $O(T)$, which represents the spatial standard deviation of the time-averaged magnetization:

$$O(T) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{xy}} \sum_{\vec{r}} \left( \langle m_z(\vec{r}, t) \rangle_t - \bar{m}_z \right)^2}$$

![figure-4-thermal-stability-of-the-magnetic-domain-patterns](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13062v1/fig4_thermal.png)
*{fig:figure-4-thermal-stability-of-the-magnetic-domain-patterns}: (a) Magnetization maps at various temperatures. (b) The domain order parameter $O(T)$ showing stability up to approximately 10 K before melting.*

The domain patterns remained stable and recognizable up to approximately $10 \text{ K}$. Beyond this, the domain order began to decline as thermal energy allowed moments to overcome the local exchange barriers, eventually "melting" the pattern entirely by $29 \text{ K}$.

Similarly, the persistence of the patterns under applied magnetic fields was tested using a survival metric $S(B)$:

$$S(B) = \frac{\sum_{\vec{r}} m_z(\vec{r}, B) m_z(\vec{r}, 0)}{\sqrt{\sum_{\vec{r}} m_z(\vec{r}, B)^2 \sum_{\vec{r}} m_z(\vec{r}, 0)^2}}$$

![figure-5-persistence-of-domain-patterns-under-external-magnetic-fields](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13062v1/fig5_field.png)
*{fig:figure-5-persistence-of-domain-patterns-under-external-magnetic-fields}: (a) Magnetization maps under in-plane and out-of-plane fields. (b) The survival metric $S(B)$ showing that the patterns persist even under strong (2 T) fields.*

The patterns proved remarkably resilient. Even at $2 \text{ T}$, an out-of-plane field only slightly suppressed the reversed domains, and an in-plane field preserved the majority of the domain structure even as the background magnetization canted into the plane.

## Significance and Unique Contributions

This work differs from existing studies of magnetic chaos in three fundamental ways. First, it demonstrates **autonomous** chaos. Previous systems required continuous external energy to remain chaotic; here, the chaos is intrinsic to the relaxation of the moiré lattice. Second, it identifies a **high-dimensional** chaotic manifold. While most magnetic chaos involves a few degrees of freedom (like the $x$ and $y$ coordinates of a vortex core), moiré magnetic chaos involves hundreds of mesoscopic domain variables ($N = 157$ in this study), allowing for the generation of complex, high-entropy bitstrings. Finally, it highlights the role of **structural engineering**. Unlike traditional spin glasses where chaos arises from random disorder, this chaos is a direct consequence of the periodic, engineered moiré geometry.

The practical implications are significant. The system's ability to produce independent, unbiased, and uncorrelated random patterns suggests its use as a high-speed hardware random number generator (RNG). With a Lyapunov time of $\sim 3.4 \text{ ps}$, such a device could potentially generate random bits at rates exceeding $10 \text{ Gbit/s}$ per flake. Furthermore, because these patterns are stable at cryogenic temperatures and observable via magnetic imaging, twisted bilayer $\text{CrI}_3$ provides a unique platform for studying the intersection of nonlinear dynamics and 2D materials science.
