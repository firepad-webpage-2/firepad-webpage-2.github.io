## Understanding Tail Linkages in Carbon Markets

The European Union Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) is a cornerstone of global climate policy, serving as the largest market for carbon permits. In this system, European Union Allowances (EUAs) are the primary instrument, representing the right to emit one tonne of carbon dioxide equivalent. As the market has matured, its price dynamics have become increasingly integrated with broader energy and financial systems. While researchers have extensively studied how these markets interact on average, a significant gap remains in understanding how they behave during periods of extreme stress—specifically, how "spikes" and "crashes" propagate through the system.

This research addresses this gap by applying graphical models for extremes to a high-dimensional system of twenty financial and energy variables. Traditional models, such as Gaussian Graphical Models (GGMs), are designed to capture average correlations. However, during market crises, standard correlations often fail to describe the true systemic risk. The study utilizes Hüsler-Reiss (HR) graphical models to identify conditional dependence specifically in the "tails" of the distribution. This approach allows for a precise mapping of how extreme movements in one variable, like natural gas prices or equity indices, relate to extreme movements in EUA futures, once the influence of other variables is accounted for.

The analysis spans two critical regulatory periods: Phase 3 (2013–2020) and Phase 4 (2021–2025) of the EU ETS. These phases have been marked by distinct market regimes, including the COVID-19 pandemic, the Russo-Ukrainian war, and major energy supply disruptions. By comparing the tail dependence structure across these periods, the study provides a detailed view of how the architecture of risk has evolved as the carbon market becomes more financialized and sensitive to geopolitical shocks.

## Methodological Framework: From Average to Extreme

To bridge the gap between typical market behavior and extreme events, the study employs a rigorous two-step statistical procedure. First, it establishes a baseline using a standard Bayesian Gaussian Graphical Model (GGM). In this model, conditional independence between two variables—meaning one does not provide additional information about the other when all other variables are known—is represented by a zero entry in the precision matrix $\Sigma^{-1}$. This results in a graph where an edge represents a partial correlation that is statistically different from zero.

However, because financial returns are rarely Gaussian and often exhibit "fat tails," the research moves beyond this baseline to model joint extremes. The data, consisting of $n = 20$ variables, are first pre-filtered using AR-GARCH models to remove serial autocorrelation and time-varying volatility. The resulting standardized residuals $\mathbf{Z}$ are then transformed to standard Pareto margins to focus purely on the dependence structure, ensuring that marginal distribution differences do not bias the results.

The core of the extreme value analysis is the Hüsler-Reiss (HR) graphical model. This model characterizes the joint tail of the distribution through a symmetric variogram matrix $\Gamma$. The entries $\Gamma_{ij}$ measure the strength of the pairwise extremal dependence. To find a sparse and interpretable network, the researchers minimize a penalized log-likelihood function, applying an $L_1$ penalty (similar to the graphical lasso) that forces smaller dependencies to zero. This identifies the most significant conditional tail links. The analysis is performed separately for the upper $p = 0.20$ fraction of data to study spikes and the lower fraction to study crashes.

Finally, to understand the unconditional relationship between variables, the study calculates the empirical extremal correlation matrix $\hat{\chi}$. The extremal correlation between two variables $i$ and $j$ is defined as:

$$\chi_{ij} = \lim_{u \to 1} P(U_i > u \mid U_j > u)$$

This value represents the probability that one variable experiences an extreme event given that another does. While $\hat{\chi}$ measures the total co-movement, the graphical model identifies which of those movements are direct rather than mediated through other variables in the system.

## Structural Contrasts: The Centrality of Carbon

One of the most striking findings of the research is the total reversal of structural roles between the average-dependence network and the tail-dependence network. In the standard GGM, which represents average conditions, EUA futures (labeled MO1) are relatively peripheral. They exhibit a low degree of connectivity (only 3 edges in both Phase 3 and Phase 4), suggesting that during normal market conditions, EUA prices are somewhat isolated from broad financial market fluctuations. Conversely, major equity indices like the STOXX Europe 600 (SXXP) are highly central in the GGM.

In the extreme networks, this hierarchy is inverted. EUA futures move from the periphery to the very center of the system. In the full-sample positive extreme network, MO1 has a degree of 17 out of a possible 19 and the highest eigenvector centrality score. This indicates that when the system experiences extreme shocks, carbon prices become a critical node through which shocks propagate. At the same time, equity indices and foreign exchange pairs become peripheral in the tails. For example, SXXP’s degree drops significantly in the extreme networks compared to the standard model.

The extreme networks are also significantly denser than the average networks. The positive extreme network for the full sample has 128 edges (a density of 0.674), which is 58% more edges than the corresponding standard GGM. This implies that extreme movements are much more pervasive across the financial-energy system than average daily fluctuations. The community structure also shifts; while the GGM shows clear sector-based clusters, the extreme networks show a "blurring" of these boundaries, particularly during market crashes, where variables from different sectors collapse into large, interconnected blocks.

## The Evolution of Risk Across Regulatory Phases

The transition from Phase 3 to Phase 4 of the EU ETS represents a major shift in the regulatory and economic environment. The research finds that the dependence structures have responded asymmetrically to this transition. In terms of average dependence, the network thinned significantly, with the GGM seeing a 27% decline in edge density. This suggests a general decoupling of markets during typical trading days.

However, the extreme networks proved much more resilient. The density of the tail networks contracted by only 6–11%. This means that while the markets might appear more independent on average, the channels for systemic risk and contagion during crises remain largely intact. In Phase 4, the gap between the density of the tail network and the standard network actually widened, implying that models based on average correlations are increasingly likely to underestimate tail risk in the current market regime.

EUA’s specific neighborhood in the network also restructured. In Phase 3, the crash-tail (negative extremes) neighborhood for EUA included a wide variety of financial links. In Phase 4, this neighborhood contracted and became more focused on commodities and foreign exchange. Specifically, connections to equity and bond indices weakened, while links to coal, oil, and the Euro exchange rate persisted or expanded. This suggests that while carbon has become "financialized," its most critical risks during extreme stress are still firmly anchored in the energy commodity complex and fundamental macroeconomic indicators.

## Drivers of Network Topology

To formally investigate what drives the formation of these connections, the authors estimated Exponential Random Graph Models (ERGMs). These models treat the entire network as a single observation and attempt to explain the likelihood of an edge existing between two nodes based on their attributes (like sector) and structural patterns (like triangles).

The researchers compared three nested models: a null model ($M_0$), a model adding sector-level factors ($M_1$), and a model including within-sector homophily and triadic closure ($M_2$). The results highlight several key principles:

1.  **Sector Homophily**: In all networks, there is a strong "nodematch" effect. Variables within the same sector (e.g., two energy commodities or two equity indices) are much more likely to be connected. Interestingly, this effect is two to three times stronger in the extreme networks than in the standard GGM, indicating that sector boundaries become even more defining during crises.
2.  **Equity Peripherality**: The "nodefactor" for the equity sector is consistently negative and significant in the tail networks. This confirms that equity indices form fewer tail connections than would be expected by chance, reinforcing their peripheral role in extreme events.
3.  **Contagion Patterns**: The research identified a significant "triadic closure" term (the tendency for connections to form triangles) in the Phase 3 negative extreme network. This is a signature of clustered contagion, where shocks hit a group of related variables simultaneously. However, this effect vanished in Phase 4. The disappearance of triadic closure suggests a shift from clustered, synchronous shocks (like the 2020 COVID crash) to a more diffuse, widespread form of market stress in the post-2021 energy crisis environment.

## Significance for Risk and Regulation

The findings have direct implications for how different stakeholders approach the EU carbon market. For risk managers and compliance entities, the study demonstrates that using average-dependence metrics for hedging is systematically flawed. Because EUA moves from the periphery to the center during extremes, a hedge that works on a typical day may fail completely during a market spike or crash. Effective tail hedging requires prioritizing connections to the energy commodity core—specifically coal and natural gas—rather than broader equity or currency indices.

For regulators, the widening gap between average and extreme connectivity is a warning. Traditional stress tests and systemic risk monitoring tools that rely on correlations or partial correlations from standard models will likely underestimate the speed and extent of shock propagation in the current EU ETS phase. The research suggests that the architecture of risk has become more diffuse and less clustered, requiring monitoring tools that can capture high-dimensional conditional tail dependence across the entire energy-financial spectrum.

In summary, this work highlights that the most consequential linkages in the carbon market are those that appear only when things go wrong. By mapping these "hidden" edges, the study provides a more complete picture of the systemic risks facing the EU ETS as it continues to evolve.
