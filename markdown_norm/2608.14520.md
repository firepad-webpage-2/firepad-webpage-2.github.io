## Foundations of Lagrangian–Eulerian Schemes

Numerical methods for solving hyperbolic conservation laws are fundamental to simulating physical phenomena such as fluid flow, wave propagation, and pollutant transport. A scalar conservation law in one dimension is typically expressed as:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \partial_x H(u) = 0$$

where $u(x, t)$ represents a conserved quantity (like mass or concentration) and $H(u)$ is the flux function. To solve this equation numerically, researchers often choose between Eulerian methods, which use a fixed grid in space, and Lagrangian methods, where the grid moves with the fluid. Lagrangian–Eulerian (L–E) schemes combine these approaches, often by defining space-time control volumes whose boundaries move over time.

A critical decision in designing L–E schemes is determining the velocity at which these boundaries move. For many problems, the natural choice is the characteristic speed given by $\frac{dx}{dt} = H'(u)$. However, a significant family of methods focuses on maintaining local conservation within space-time regions by selecting a different velocity: the flux-to-state ratio. This ratio, defined by $\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{H(u)}{u}$, ensures that no mass crosses the lateral boundaries of the control volume.

The recent work by Furtado, Pereira, and Yeh provides a critical analysis of a specific Lagrangian–Eulerian scheme described in a paper by Abreu et al. (2023). The primary focus of this analysis is to clarify the origins and mathematical identity of what Abreu et al. term "no-flow curves." The authors demonstrate that these curves are not a new conceptual advancement but are mathematically identical to "integral curves" introduced decades earlier in the Locally Conservative Eulerian–Lagrangian Method (LCELM).

## The Mathematical Identity of Control Volumes

The core contribution of this comment is the rigorous demonstration that the "no-flow curves" and the resulting "no-flow regions" are the same continuous mathematical objects as the "integral curves" and "integral tubes" established in the literature since 2000. 

In the work of Douglas, Pereira, and Yeh (DPY), the LCELM was introduced to handle transport in porous media. For a source-free scalar conservation law, the lateral boundaries of their space-time control volumes were defined by the ordinary differential equation (ODE):

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{H(u)}{u}$$

The comment provides a "dictionary" to map the notation used in the original LCELM work to the standard scalar notation used in modern hyperbolic conservation law research. In the original LCELM papers, the conserved quantity was represented by $\Phi S$ and the flux by $\Lambda_w U$. By applying the mapping $\Phi S \leftrightarrow u$ and $\Lambda_w U \leftrightarrow H(u)$, the defining equations become term-by-term identical. 

The authors further show that the geometric construction in Abreu et al. (2023) defines a region $D_j^{n,n+1}$ bounded by curves $x = \gamma(t)$ such that:

$$\dot{\gamma}(t) = \frac{H(u_{\gamma}(t))}{u_{\gamma}(t)}$$

This is the exact same ODE used in the DPY work and its later specialized version, the Forward-Tracked Locally Conservative Eulerian–Lagrangian Method (FLCELM). Whether the curves are tracked backward in time (as in DPY) or forward in time (as in FLCELM and Abreu et al.), the underlying continuous geometric object—the curve whose slope is the ratio of flux to state—remains unchanged.

## The Physical Meaning of "No-Flow"

The term "no-flow curve" is descriptive of the physical property these curves possess. The comment formally establishes this property through Proposition 1, which states that for a curve $x = \gamma(t)$, the condition for zero normal mass flux across the boundary is equivalent to the flux-to-state ratio.

Mathematically, the net flux across a moving boundary $x = \gamma(t)$ is given by the difference between the physical flux $H(u)$ and the flux induced by the movement of the boundary itself, which is the product of the boundary velocity $\dot{\gamma}(t)$ and the value of the conserved quantity at the boundary $u_{\gamma}(t)$. Setting this net flux to zero yields:

$$H(u_{\gamma}(t)) - \dot{\gamma}(t) u_{\gamma}(t) = 0$$

If $u_{\gamma}(t) \neq 0$, this algebraic equation can be rearranged to solve for the velocity:

$$\dot{\gamma}(t) = \frac{H(u_{\gamma}(t))}{u_{\gamma}(t)}$$

This derivation highlights that the "no-flow" designation is a direct consequence of the ODE choice. When the boundaries of a space-time control volume are defined by these curves, the conservation of mass within that volume becomes an identity of the geometry. No mass enters or leaves through the sides; mass only enters through the bottom ($t^n$) and leaves through the top ($t^{n+1}$). This property simplifies the discrete mass balance equations, as the lateral flux terms vanish.

The authors also note a caveat for "vacuum states" where $u = 0$. In such cases, the zero-flux equation $H(0) - \dot{\gamma}(t) \cdot 0 = 0$ is satisfied for any finite velocity $\dot{\gamma}(t)$, assuming the flux normalization $H(0) = 0$. This implies that at points where the conserved quantity vanishes, the "no-flow" requirement does not uniquely determine the path of the curve, necessitating additional modeling choices or numerical regularizations.

## Evolution of Terminology and Attribution

A significant portion of the comment is dedicated to a chronological review of how these concepts have been named and attributed in the scientific literature. The authors document a shift in language across a series of papers involving overlapping author groups.

From the introduction of LCELM in 2000 until roughly 2020, the construction was consistently referred to as "integral tubes" or "integral curves" and credited to Douglas, Pereira, and Yeh. The phrase "no-flow curves" initially appeared as a secondary description for the boundaries of these integral tubes.

However, starting around 2021, the comment observes that the term "no-flow curves" began to be presented as a novel or substantially improved concept. In some instances, the foundational DPY work was cited but the "no-flow" geometry was simultaneously described as an extension or a new development, even when the defining mathematical equations were identical. In other cases, attribution to the original 2000 work was reduced or omitted.

The authors of the comment argue that this shift obscures the scientific lineage of the method. By clarifying that "no-flow curves" are the same as the previously established "integral curves," the comment aims to ensure that future researchers can access the full body of existing theory, including prior proofs of conservation and geometric properties, without being misled by changing nomenclature.

## Delineating Genuine Novelty

While the comment is critical of the claims regarding the novelty of the continuous geometric construction, it is careful to identify the aspects of the work by Abreu et al. (2023) that are genuinely new. The authors distinguish between the *continuous* definition of the control volumes and the *discrete* algorithms used to solve the equations on a computer.

The genuine contributions of Abreu et al. (2023) acknowledged by the comment include:
1.  **Discrete Scheme Design:** The specific formulation of a non-staggered, fully discrete Lagrangian–Eulerian scheme.
2.  **CFL Condition:** The derivation of a "weak" CFL condition that governs the stability and time-stepping of the numerical method.
3.  **Convergence Analysis:** The application of the "weak asymptotic method" developed by Danilov, Omel’yanov, and Shelkovich. This rigorous analytical framework is used to prove the convergence of the numerical solution to the exact solution of the conservation law.

By separating the continuous geometry (which is part of the established literature) from the discrete implementation and rigorous analysis (which are new), the comment provides a balanced view of the research. It allows the community to appreciate the technical advancements in the numerical analysis while maintaining an accurate historical record of the underlying physics-based geometry.

## Implications for Scientific Discourse

The significance of this work extends beyond a simple dispute over terminology. It addresses broader themes of scholarly integrity, clarity in communication, and the cumulative nature of scientific progress.

For practitioners and students, the comment serves as a roadmap to the development of locally conservative methods. It prevents the fragmentation of knowledge that occurs when the same concept is treated as two different things under different names. By understanding that "no-flow curves" are "integral curves," a researcher can leverage decades of existing work on LCELM to improve modern L–E schemes.

Furthermore, the document emphasizes the importance of precise attribution. In computational mathematics, where new methods are often built upon foundational geometric or physical principles, distinguishing between a new *principle* and a new *application* or *discretization* of that principle is vital for maintaining a clear and honest scientific record. The comment ensures that the "integral curve" construction—a foundational element that allows Lagrangian–Eulerian methods to achieve exact local conservation—is correctly attributed to the original work that introduced and justified this specific choice of boundary velocity.
