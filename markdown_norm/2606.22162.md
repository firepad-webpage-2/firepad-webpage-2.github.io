## The Challenge of Multi-Scale Credit Risk

Understanding how different industrial sectors default together—a phenomenon known as default clustering—is a central problem in portfolio credit risk. Financial institutions and regulators must model these dependencies to assess the risk of large-scale losses and to price credit-linked derivatives. Traditionally, this dependence is represented using static tools like correlation matrices or copulas, which describe the relationship between sectors at a specific time horizon, such as one year.

However, a significant empirical challenge exists: the dependence structure observed in monthly credit data often looks remarkably different from the structure observed in annual data. Static models, which are calibrated to a single horizon, do not naturally explain why or how these relationships change as data is aggregated over time. This discrepancy suggests that multi-sector dependence is not a fixed property but a dynamic one that evolves across scales.

Recent research by Shintaro Mori and Masato Hisakado addresses this by investigating how persistent, monthly credit-state dynamics are transformed through a process called "temporal coarse-graining." By aggregating short-term latent default probabilities into longer-horizon distributions, the researchers demonstrate that annual-scale dependence can be generated from monthly fluctuations. This work establishes a link between scales, showing that long-horizon correlations and copulas are "effective" quantities emerging from the persistence of underlying credit cycles.

## Empirical Foundations of Sectoral Default Dynamics

The study utilizes a dataset from Standard & Poor's (S&P) spanning January 1981 to September 2021, covering monthly default counts for 13 distinct sectors. Before building a formal model, the researchers performed several empirical diagnostics to identify the underlying patterns in the data.

One key observation is "variance scaling." In a world where monthly defaults were independent, the variance of the default rate over $k$ months would decay proportionally to $1/k$. Instead, the data shows a much slower decay, indicating that credit states are highly persistent—when a sector is in a high-risk state, it tends to stay there for several months.

Furthermore, the researchers applied Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to the monthly sectoral correlation matrix. They found that the dependence structure is dominated by a "low-rank" architecture. The first empirical eigenvector (the primary mode of co-movement) represents a "market-wide" default risk, where all sectors generally move in the same direction. The second eigenvector represents a "sector-rotation" mode, where some sectors face increased stress while others see relative relief. Crucially, as the time horizon $k$ increases from one month to twelve months, the eigenvalues associated with these modes increase, suggesting that temporal aggregation amplifies the influence of these dominant patterns.

## The Dynamic Low-Rank AR(1)–Binomial Model

To capture these dynamics, the authors developed a Bayesian state-space model. Unlike static models that look at one year in isolation, this model operates at a monthly scale and assumes that the observed default counts are driven by latent (hidden) credit states.

For each sector $s$ and month $t$, the observed default count $L_{t,s}$ is modeled as a Binomial distribution, where $N_{t,s}$ is the number of firms in that sector:

$$
L_{t,s} | p_{t,s}, N_{t,s} \sim \text{Binomial}(N_{t,s}, p_{t,s})
$$

The monthly default probability $p_{t,s}$ is linked to a latent credit state $y_{t,s}$ using the standard normal cumulative distribution function $\Phi$:

$$
p_{t,s} = \Phi(y_{t,s})
$$

The latent state $y_{t,s}$ is decomposed into three parts: a sector-specific baseline $\mu_s$, a combination of shared common factors $F_{r,t}$ with specific weights (loadings) $\lambda_{sr}$, and an idiosyncratic error term $\epsilon_{t,s}$:

$$
y_{t,s} = \mu_s + \sum_{r=1}^{R} \lambda_{sr} F_{r,t} + \epsilon_{t,s}
$$

The shared factors $F_{r,t}$ are the engine of the model. Each factor follows a stationary first-order autoregressive process, $AR(1)$, which allows it to have memory of its previous state:

$$
F_{r,t+1} = \phi_r F_{r,t} + \eta_{r,t+1}
$$

In this equation, $\phi_r$ is a persistence parameter between 0 and 1, and $\eta_{r,t+1}$ is a random shock. By setting the number of factors $R$ to 2, the model focuses on the most significant drivers of risk: the market-wide factor and the sector-rotation factor. The directions of these factors (the loadings $\lambda_{sr}$) are fixed to the empirical eigenvectors found in the monthly data, ensuring the model remains grounded in observed sectoral relationships.

## Temporal Coarse-Graining and Copula Generation

The most distinctive feature of this research is how it moves from monthly states to annual predictions. This is achieved through "temporal coarse-graining." If we know the monthly default probabilities $p_{j,s}$ for each month $j$ within a block of $k$ months, we can calculate the probability that a firm defaults at least once during that entire period $p^{(k)}_s$:

$$
p^{(k)}_s = 1 - \prod_{j=1}^k \{1 - p_{j,s}\}
$$

By simulating paths of the monthly latent factors, the model produces a "mixing distribution" for these $k$-month probabilities. This distribution captures how the persistence of the $AR(1)$ factors translates into annual dependence. 

Instead of assuming a specific functional form for the annual dependence (like a Gaussian or $t$-copula), the researchers derive "posterior-implied copulas." These are obtained by rank-transforming the simulated probabilities. The study finds that for sector pairs with positive common-component covariance (like Metal and Telecom), coarse-graining sharpens the positive dependence. Conversely, for sectors that often move in opposite directions (like Real Estate and Telecom), the process enhances "tail-exclusion," where it becomes highly unlikely for both sectors to experience extreme stress simultaneously.

## Identifying Stress Episodes through Latent Factors

By estimating the model using Bayesian methods, the researchers can extract the posterior mean trajectories of the two latent factors, $F_1$ and $F_2$. This provides a historical narrative of credit stress. 

As shown in {fig:posterior-mean-trajectory-f1-f2-plane}, the factors allow us to distinguish between different types of financial crises. The market-wide factor $F_1$ typically increases during any major stress event. However, the second factor $F_2$ reveals the sectoral "flavor" of the crisis. In the aftermath of the dot-com bubble (around 2001), $F_1$ was high but $F_2$ was negative, indicating that the stress was disproportionately concentrated in sectors like Telecom. During the Global Financial Crisis (around 2009), $F_1$ reached even higher levels, but $F_2$ was positive, reflecting the massive stress centered on Real Estate and Financial Institutions.

## Forecast Evaluation and Practical Significance

To test the model's practical utility, the authors performed a rolling 12-month-ahead forecast evaluation. They compared the dynamic factor models (a one-factor version, B2, and a two-factor version, B3) against static baselines (a simple binomial model, B0, and a beta-binomial model, B1).

The results showed that static models are often poorly calibrated for long-horizon forecasting. The static binomial model, for example, failed to capture enough variation, resulting in zero coverage for its 90% predictive intervals. In contrast, the dynamic models provided much better calibration. 

Key findings from the forecast evaluation include:
*   **Improved Dispersion:** The dynamic models produced annual predictive distributions with appropriate "spread," avoiding the under-dispersion common in static models.
*   **Portfolio Accuracy:** The one-factor dynamic model (B2) generally provided the best log-score for total portfolio default counts, highlighting the efficiency of the market-wide factor for aggregate risk.
*   **Sectoral Precision:** The two-factor model (B3) excelled in the Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS) at the sectoral level. While the first factor handles the overall volume of defaults, the second factor improves the allocation of those defaults across different industries.

The significance of this work lies in its demonstration that long-term credit risk dependence is a consequence of short-term temporal persistence. By modeling the latent dynamics of credit states, risk managers can generate horizon-dependent correlation structures that are more flexible and accurate than static alternatives. This framework provides a scale-consistent way to understand how monthly economic shocks aggregate into the annual losses that define institutional risk.
