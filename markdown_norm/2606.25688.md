## The Calibration-Estimation Trade-off in Structural Modeling

In structural economics, researchers often encounter a fundamental challenge: their models contain more parameters than can be reliably estimated from available data. To resolve this, a common practice is to "calibrate" some parameters—fixing them at specific values based on previous literature or theoretical priors—while "estimating" the remaining ones. However, the decision of which parameters to fix and which to estimate (the partition selection) is frequently arbitrary, driven by convention or computational convenience rather than formal econometric principles.

As illustrated in the figure below, different choices for which parameters are fixed can lead to vastly different results for the model's primary object of interest. If a researcher fixes a parameter to which the model is highly sensitive, even a small error in that calibration can lead to significant bias in the final conclusions.

This paper by Joan Alegre Canton introduces a formal econometric criterion to guide this decision. By minimizing the "worst-case" local bias induced by potential calibration errors, researchers can select a calibration-estimation partition that makes their results more robust and transparent.

## Defining the Partition-Selection Problem

To formalize this problem, let $\eta$ be a $d_\eta$-dimensional vector containing all the structural parameters of the model. Typically, the researcher is interested in a specific output of the model, such as a welfare measure, a policy effect, or an impulse response. We denote this object of interest as $\gamma = \Gamma(\eta)$, where $\gamma$ is a $d_\gamma$-dimensional vector.

A "partition" $S$ is a choice of which indices in $\eta$ to estimate. We denote the estimated parameters as $\alpha$ and the calibrated (fixed) parameters as $\beta$, such that $\beta$ corresponds to the complement set $S^c$. The structural model provides a set of equations $g(\eta) = 0$, which are used for estimation.

Given a fixed value for $\beta$, the estimated parameters $\alpha(\beta; S)$ are found by minimizing a distance criterion:

$$
g(\alpha, \beta)'W g(\alpha, \beta)
$$

where $W$ is a weighting matrix. This estimation process induces a map for the object of interest:

$$
\gamma(\beta; S) = \Gamma(\alpha(\beta; S), \beta)
$$

The central question is: which partition $S$ should be chosen so that $\gamma(\beta; S)$ is least sensitive to the specific value chosen for $\beta$?

## A Local Minimax-Bias Criterion

The paper proposes a sensitivity statistic, $K_S$, which measures the largest possible change in the object of interest caused by a small, normalized error in the calibrated parameters.

To compute this, the researcher first identifies a reference point $\eta^*$, which is a preliminary estimate where the model equations are satisfied ($g(\eta^*) = 0$). Using the Implicit Function Theorem on the first-order conditions of the estimation problem, the local response of the estimated parameters to changes in the calibrated parameters is:

$$
\frac{\partial \alpha(\beta^*; S)}{\partial \beta} = - (G_\alpha'W G_\alpha)^{-1} G_\alpha'W G_\beta
$$

Here, $G_\alpha$ and $G_\beta$ are the Jacobians of the model equations with respect to the estimated and calibrated parameters, respectively. By applying the chain rule, we can find how the object of interest $\gamma$ reacts to perturbations in $\beta$:

$$
\frac{\partial \gamma(\beta^*; S)}{\partial \beta} = \Gamma_\alpha \left( \frac{\partial \alpha(\beta^*; S)}{\partial \beta} \right) + \Gamma_\beta
$$

However, parameters often have different units (e.g., an interest rate vs. a substitution elasticity), making direct comparisons difficult. The researcher introduces a diagonal normalization matrix $\Sigma_S$ containing values $\Delta_j$ that represent "plausible" ranges for each parameter. The scalar sensitivity statistic $K_S$ is then defined as:

$$
K_S(\eta^*, \Sigma_S) = \sqrt{|S^c|} \cdot \| \left( \frac{\partial \gamma(\beta^*; S)}{\partial \beta} \right) \Sigma_S \|_2
$$

where $\|\cdot\|_2$ denotes the spectral norm (the largest singular value). This statistic provides a local minimax-bias interpretation: for a small magnitude of normalized miscalibration $\epsilon$, the worst-case bias is approximately:

$$
\text{WorstBias}(\beta^*, \epsilon; S) = \epsilon K_S + o(\epsilon)
$$

By choosing the partition $S^*$ that minimizes $K_S$, the researcher is selecting the model configuration that is most robust to calibration errors within the defined plausible ranges.

## Identification and Admissibility

Not all partitions are valid. Before minimizing $K_S$, the researcher must filter out "inadmissible" partitions. A partition $S$ is considered admissible if it satisfies three main conditions:

1.  **Non-triviality**: The object of interest must actually depend on the parameters being estimated.
2.  **Conditional Local Identification**: The estimated parameters $\alpha$ must be uniquely determined by the data given the fixed values $\beta$. Mathematically, this requires the Jacobian $G_\alpha$ to have full column rank.
3.  **Stability**: To ensure the criterion is driven by calibration bias rather than sampling noise, the sampling variance of the estimate should be asymptotically negligible.

The paper operationalizes the identification check by using a hard-thresholding rule. A partition is rejected if the smallest singular value of the weighted Jacobian $W^{1/2}G_\alpha$ falls below a threshold $\tau_n = \left( \frac{\log n}{n} \right)^{1/2}$, where $n$ is the sample size. This prevents the selection of partitions that are "nearly" unidentified, which would lead to unstable and unreliable estimates.

## Empirical Application: The Nakamura-Steinsson Model

The methodology is applied to the influential New Keynesian model by Nakamura and Steinsson (2018). The object of interest is the "information effect" parameter $\gamma$, which captures how monetary policy shocks influence public expectations about the state of the economy.

The model involves 10 structural parameters, including the discount factor $\rho$, the elasticity of marginal cost $\omega$, the elasticity of substitution $\theta$, and the Phillips curve slope $\kappa \zeta$. In the original study, several of these were calibrated while others were estimated using Classical Minimum Distance (CMD).

Applying the $K_S$ criterion revealed significant heterogeneity in sensitivity across different partitions. The research identified 60 admissible partitions. The "least-sensitive" partition $S^*$ estimated the set $\{\delta, \sigma, \rho_1, \rho_2, \gamma, b\}$ and fixed $\{\rho, \omega, \theta, \kappa \zeta\}$.

![worst-case-bias-comparison-in-the-nakamura-steinsson-model](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.25688v1/img-9.jpeg)
*{fig:worst-case-bias-comparison-in-the-nakamura-steinsson-model}: Worst-case information effect $\gamma$ as a function of miscalibration size $\epsilon$. The $S^*$ partition (blue) is significantly more robust than the original partition (red).*

Key findings from this application include:
*   **Original vs. Optimal**: The original partition used in the 2018 study was found to be "inadmissible" under the formal rank threshold and was significantly more fragile than $S^*$. Its sensitivity $bK$ was roughly 30 times larger than that of the optimal partition.
*   **Concentrated Sensitivity**: For the optimal partition, 99.6% of the worst-case bias was driven by the calibrated discount factor $\rho$. Parameters like $\omega$ and $\theta$, often thought to be critical, contributed almost nothing to the fragility of the information effect estimate.
*   **Fragility Drivers**: High sensitivity was often caused by calibrating parameters related to the persistence of monetary shocks ($\rho_1, \rho_2$) rather than estimating them.

## Simulation Evidence and Finite Sample Performance

To validate the $K_S$ criterion, Monte Carlo simulations were conducted using the Nakamura-Steinsson model as a data-generating process. The simulations compared the performance of the $K_S$-selected partition against others across different sample sizes ($n=150$ and $n=500$) and different levels of miscalibration.

![monte-carlo-results-for-different-partitions](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.25688v1/img-3.jpeg)
*{fig:monte-carlo-results-for-different-partitions}: Distribution of estimates for $\gamma$ under 5% miscalibration ($n=150$). The $S^*$ partition (blue) stays closest to the true population value (vertical line).*

The results confirmed that $S^*$ consistently achieved the lowest absolute bias and the lowest Mean Squared Error (MSE). Interestingly, increasing the sample size did not solve the problem of miscalibration. While a larger $n$ reduced the sampling variance, it made the bias component of the error even more dominant. This underscores that in large-sample structural models, the choice of calibration strategy is often more important for accuracy than the precision of the data itself.

## Conclusion and Significance

This research transforms the often-ignored task of partition selection into a rigorous econometric procedure. By providing a scalar statistic $K_S$ that measures worst-case bias, the paper allows researchers to:

1.  **Justify Calibration Choices**: Move beyond "convention" to a formal minimax-bias justification.
2.  **Identify Fragility**: Pinpoint which specific calibrated parameters are responsible for the uncertainty in the model's conclusions.
3.  **Improve Robustness**: Systematically select model configurations that are least likely to be overturned by small errors in fixed parameters.

The method is computationally efficient, requiring only local derivatives at a reference point, making it a practical diagnostic tool for a wide range of structural models. Ultimately, this approach enhances the transparency and credibility of structural economic analysis by making the consequences of calibration choices explicit and minimizing their potential for bias.
