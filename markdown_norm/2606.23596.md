## 1. Introduction: The Internal Anatomy of the Market

Asset pricing research frequently evaluates factor models based on how well they explain the returns of the aggregate market or a set of external "anomaly" portfolios. However, a model can appear successful at the aggregate level while masking significant pricing errors within its own components. This research introduces a "body–tail" test to look inside the market portfolio, examining whether the factors that price the aggregate market also price its constituent parts in an internally consistent manner.

The core principle involves decomposing a value-weighted market portfolio into two segments along the capitalization axis: a "body" consisting of large-cap stocks and a "tail" consisting of small-cap stocks. Because these two portfolios are constructed directly from the same investible universe as the market, their combination must precisely reconstruct the aggregate market return. This design allows for a diagnostic test of whether the systematic pricing errors (alphas) of the body and tail offset each other, revealing hidden inconsistencies that an aggregate regression would miss.

This work specifically examines the tension between two primary criteria for evaluating factor models: the ability to span a maximum Sharpe ratio (mean-variance efficiency) and the ability to produce zero pricing errors (alphas) across test assets. The study evaluates prominent models, including the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM), the Fama–French three, five, and six-factor models, and the q-factor model (q5), finding that a model's dominance in mean-variance spanning does not necessarily translate to internal pricing consistency.

## 2. The Limits of Aggregate Market Pricing

In standard asset pricing practice, a model is often vetted by regressing the market portfolio on the model’s factors. If the model includes a market factor, the resulting alpha is almost always close to zero. This "pass" is frequently taken as a sign of model health. However, this study argues that an aggregate pass is an insufficient sanity check.

A value-weighted market portfolio is dominated by a few large firms (the body), while the vast majority of firms (the tail) have a very small collective weight. If a model generates a large negative alpha for the body and a large positive alpha for the tail, these errors can cancel each other out in the aggregate value-weighted calculation. The resulting aggregate alpha might be statistically insignificant even if the model fails to correctly price the two distinct economic segments of the market. By splitting the market into these segments, the body–tail test provides an "internal" diagnostic that does not require the use of external, potentially arbitrary, test assets.

## 3. Constructing the Body–Tail Diagnostic

To implement this test, the researcher constructs an investible U.S. equity universe from CRSP data, applying filters for market capitalization and liquidity to ensure the portfolios are tradable. At each annual formation date, the stocks are sorted by market capitalization. The market is then split according to a ratio $p$, where $p$ represents the cumulative market capitalization share. For example, if $p = 0.90$, the "body" contains the largest stocks making up 90% of the market value, and the "tail" contains the remaining 10%.

The study uses nine different split ratios:
$p \in \{0.50, 0.60, 0.75, 0.80, 0.85, 0.90, 0.95, 0.975, 0.99\}$

The essential feature of this decomposition is the "reconstruction identity." The daily excess return of the aggregate market, $R^e_{M,d}$, is the sum of the value-weighted body and tail excess returns:

$$R^e_{M,d} = w^B_{p,d-1} R^e_{B,p,d} + w^T_{p,d-1} R^e_{T,p,d}$$

Where $w^B_{p,d-1}$ and $w^T_{p,d-1}$ are the weights of the body and tail portfolios at the end of the previous day. For each split ratio $p$, the research estimates time-series regressions for each leg $\ell \in \{B, T\}$:

$$R^e_{\ell,p,d} = \alpha_{\ell,p} + \beta'_{\ell,p} f_d + \varepsilon_{\ell,p,d}$$

The primary hypothesis tested is whether the pricing errors are jointly zero ($H_0: \alpha_{B,p} = \alpha_{T,p} = 0$). If a model prices the market correctly at the aggregate level but fails this test, it indicates an internal inconsistency in how the model accounts for the risk-return profiles of large versus small firms.

## 4. Evaluating Spanning vs. Pricing Consistency

A significant contribution of this paper is demonstrating the divergence between "spanning" and "pricing" performance. In the context of asset pricing, a model "spans" another if its factors can explain the returns of the other model's factors, effectively offering a higher maximum Sharpe ratio. 

The study confirms that the q-factor model (q5) significantly dominates the Fama–French six-factor model (FF6) in spanning tests. When q5's non-market factors (Size, Investment, ROE, and Expected Growth) are added to FF6, they substantially increase the squared maximum Sharpe ratio, with a daily $\Delta SR^2 = 3.143$. In contrast, adding FF6 factors to q5 provides a much smaller improvement ($\Delta SR^2 = 0.549$).

Despite this spanning superiority, the body–tail test reveals that q5 is a significant outlier in pricing consistency. While most other models (CAPM, FF5, FF6) pass the joint zero-alpha test across nearly all split ratios, q5 rejects the null hypothesis at every single split ratio $p$ at the daily frequency. This indicates that the very factors that allow q5 to achieve a high Sharpe ratio may simultaneously introduce systematic pricing errors across the capitalization structure of the market.

## 5. Empirical Evidence: The Daily Frequency Puzzle

The most striking result of the paper is the consistent pricing failure of the q5 model at the daily frequency. Across all nine split ratios, the q5 model produces a systematic pattern: negative alphas for the market "body" and positive alphas for the market "tail." 

This pattern is not a mechanical artifact of the splitting process. To prove this, the researcher conducts "random split" placebo tests. Instead of sorting by size, stocks are assigned to groups A and B randomly until they match the target weights of the body and tail. Under these random splits, q5 passes the zero-alpha test at a rate consistent with the 5% significance level. This confirms that the pricing error is specifically tied to the economic ordering of the market by market capitalization.

Furthermore, the paper explores whether these errors are a chronic feature of the model or varies over time. The daily q5 body–tail pattern is found to be episodic, appearing strongly in the 1980s and early 2000s but nearly disappearing in other decades like the 1990s and the 2010s. This suggests that the internal pricing inconsistency is linked to specific market regimes.

## 6. The Impact of Observation Frequency

A critical finding of the research is that internal pricing consistency is frequency-dependent. When the daily returns are compounded into monthly returns and evaluated against monthly factors, the results change significantly.

At the monthly frequency, the sharp rejections of the q5 model largely attenuate. In the common sample period, the q5 joint rejection rate drops from 9 out of 9 ratios at the daily level to only 1 out of 9 at the monthly level. Conversely, other models like the Fama–French three-factor model (FF3) show more weakness at the monthly frequency than they do at the daily frequency.

This discovery suggests that factor models are frequency-conditional approximations of the true stochastic discount factor (SDF). A model that appears consistent at a monthly horizon may exhibit systematic mismatches at a daily horizon. This challenges the common assumption that model properties are invariant to the frequency of data used for estimation.

## 7. Identifying the Source of Pricing Inconsistencies

The paper goes beyond identifying the existence of pricing errors by pinpointing their source within the factor models. Through "factor-block ablations"—where specific factors are removed from a model to see if the pricing error disappears—the author identifies the profitability and growth components of the q5 model as the primary culprits.

The Expected Growth (EG) factor is found to be the most significant driver of the body–tail sensitivity in q5. Removing EG from the model reduces the number of split-ratio rejections from 9/9 to 2/9. The Return on Equity (ROE) factor also contributes to the pattern.

Using a loading-premium decomposition, the researcher demonstrates that these factors imply a lower expected return for the small-cap "tail" than is actually realized in the data. Even though the tail has positive exposure to the size factor (ME), the strong negative contributions from the ROE and EG factor exposures lead the q5 model to "under-predict" the tail's return, resulting in the observed positive alphas. This identifies a fundamental mismatch between how these specific factors account for the risk of small-cap stocks compared to large-cap stocks at the daily frequency.

## 8. Conclusion and Research Implications

The "Anatomy of the Market" research provides a new lens for evaluating the health of asset pricing models. By moving from aggregate market regressions to internal body–tail decompositions, it reveals that a model's high Sharpe ratio or aggregate success can coexist with systematic internal pricing failures.

The study's findings have several implications for future research:
- **Model Choice:** The "best" factor model depends on the researcher's criteria. A model like q5 may be superior for mean-variance spanning but inferior for maintaining pricing consistency across the capitalization spectrum at high frequencies.
- **Frequency Matters:** The frequency at which factors and portfolios are measured is not a neutral choice; it can fundamentally alter the perceived validity of a model.
- **Diagnostic Innovation:** The body–tail test offers a transparent, internally anchored method for model evaluation that could be extended to other dimensions, such as liquidity or industry, to further probe the consistency of our leading asset pricing frameworks.

Ultimately, the work highlights that the market portfolio is not just a benchmark but a complex structure whose internal "anatomy" can provide deep insights into the limitations of our current economic models.
