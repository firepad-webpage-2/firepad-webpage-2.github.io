## Network Discovery in Directed Environments

Modern data analysis often involves understanding the relationships between different entities, whether they are neurons in a brain, stocks in a market, or intersections in a city. These entities and their connections are mathematically represented as graphs. While much of the foundational work in graph signal processing (GSP) focused on undirected graphs—where a connection between node A and node B is identical to a connection between B and A—many real-world systems are inherently directed.

The problem of inferring these directed relationships from observed data is known as directed graph topology inference. This paper presents a framework to recover the structure of a directed graph (digraph) by observing signals that have been diffused through the network. This diffusion is modeled as a graph filter, which acts as a mathematical operator that spreads information across the edges of the network.

![the-proposed-two-step-identification-framework-illustrating-the-transition-from-diffusion-filter-identification-to-digraph-topology-inference](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27455v1/x1.png)
*{fig:the-proposed-two-step-identification-framework-illustrating-the-transition-from-diffusion-filter-identification-to-digraph-topology-inference}: The proposed two-step identification framework, illustrating the transition from diffusion filter identification to digraph topology inference.*

Unlike many previous approaches that assume "white" input signals (where all nodes receive independent, equal-intensity noise), this work addresses the more complex and realistic scenario of "colored" inputs. In this case, the input signals can have arbitrary correlations, making the recovery of the underlying graph significantly more challenging because the statistical properties of the observations are no longer simple reflections of the graph's structure.

## The Diffusion Model and Graph Shift Operators

To understand how the framework works, we must first define the relationship between the graph and the data. A graph $G = (V, E)$ consists of $N$ nodes. The relationships between these nodes are captured by a Graph Shift Operator (GSO), denoted as $S$. For a directed graph, $S$ is typically an asymmetric matrix where the entry $S_{ij} \neq 0$ if there is a directed edge from node $j$ to node $i$ (i.e., $(j, i) \in E$).

The signals we observe, $y$, are modeled as the output of a linear diffusion process: $y = Hx$, where $x$ is the input signal and $H$ is a graph filter. This filter is defined as a polynomial of the GSO:

$$H = \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} h_l S^l$$

In this model, $H$ represents how a signal spreads across the graph over $L-1$ time steps. Crucially, the covariance of the observed output signal, $C_y$, is related to the covariance of the input signal, $C_x$, through the filter: $C_y = H C_x H^T$.

The primary challenge identified by the authors is that when the graph is directed and the input is colored (meaning $C_x$ is not a simple identity matrix), the filter $H$ and the output covariance $C_y$ do not share the same eigenvectors. This prevents researchers from using standard spectral decomposition methods to find the graph structure. The paper solves this by splitting the problem into identifying the filter $H$ first, and then using $H$ to find the best-fitting $S$.

## Identifying the Diffusion Filter

The first stage of the methodology is the identification of the diffusion filter $H$. The researchers assume that we have access to $M$ different processes, each with a known input covariance $C_{x,m}$ and an observed output covariance $C_{y,m}$. The goal is to find a single filter $H$ that satisfies the relationship for all processes:

$C_{y,m} = H C_x,m H^T$ for $m = 1, \dots, M$

This is a system of quadratic matrix equations. The authors prove that if only one process is available ($M=1$), there are infinitely many possible filters. However, as $M$ increases, the number of valid solutions shrinks. Specifically, they demonstrate that with $M=4$ processes and sufficient diversity in the input signals, the filter $H$ can be uniquely identified (up to a global sign).

To solve this in practice with noisy data, the paper formulates a manifold-constrained optimization problem:

$$\min_{H, \{U_m\}} \frac{1}{2} \sum_{m=1}^M \|H - \hat{C}_{y,m}^{1/2} U_m C_{x,m}^{-1/2}\|_F^2$$

Here, $U_m$ are orthogonal matrices belonging to the Stiefel manifold ($U_m^T U_m = I$). The algorithm alternates between updating $H$ and updating the matrices $U_m$ using Riemannian gradient descent, which is a specialized optimization technique for searching over curved surfaces like manifolds.

## Inferring the Network Topology

Once an estimate of the filter $H$ is obtained, the second stage is to recover the sparse graph-shift operator $S$. The key insight used here is the commutation property: if $H$ is a polynomial in $S$, then they must commute, meaning $HS = SH$.

The problem is then to find the sparsest possible matrix $S$ that satisfies this commutation property while also obeying the physical constraints of a graph (e.g., non-negative weights and no self-loops). This is formulated as a convex optimization problem:

$$\min_{S \in \mathcal{S}} \|S\|_1 + \frac{\mu}{2} \|\hat{H}S - S\hat{H}\|_F^2$$

The first term, the $\ell_1$-norm of $S$, encourages sparsity, ensuring that the inferred graph doesn't have unnecessary edges. The second term penalizes the violation of the commutation property, allowing the model to be robust to errors in the initial estimate of $H$. The set $\mathcal{S}$ represents the structural constraints, such as $S_{ii} = 0$ (no self-loops) and $S_{ij} \geq 0$ (non-negative edge weights). This optimization is solved using a proximal gradient method, which handles the non-differentiable $\ell_1$ term efficiently.

## The Joint "Closed-Loop" Framework

While the two-step approach is computationally efficient, the authors introduce a more advanced "Closed-Loop" (CL) approach. In this version, rather than solving for $H$ and then $S$, the framework solves for both simultaneously.

![the-joint-identification-framework-closed-loop-where-the-estimation-of-the-filter-and-the-graph-topology-are-coupled-to-improve-accuracy](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27455v1/x2.png)
*{fig:the-joint-identification-framework-closed-loop-where-the-estimation-of-the-filter-and-the-graph-topology-are-coupled-to-improve-accuracy}: The joint identification framework (Closed-Loop), where the estimation of the filter and the graph topology are coupled to improve accuracy.*

The joint objective function combines the filter identification loss and the topology inference loss into a single optimization:

$$\min_{S, H, \{U_m\}} \|S\|_1 + \frac{\lambda}{2} \sum_{m=1}^M \|H - \hat{C}_{y,m}^{1/2} U_m C_{x,m}^{-1/2}\|_F^2 + \frac{\mu}{2} \|HS - SH\|_F^2$$

By solving this jointly, the estimate of the graph structure $S$ can help refine the estimate of the filter $H$ throughout the iterations, and vice versa. The paper provides a formal proof that this complex, non-convex optimization converges to a stationary point. This joint approach is particularly beneficial when the number of available data samples is low, as it leverages the structural coupling between $H$ and $S$ to compensate for noise.

## Performance and Real-World Applications

The effectiveness of the proposed method was tested on both synthetic and real-world datasets. In synthetic experiments, the "Closed-Loop" method consistently outperformed the sequential "Open-Loop" method. For example, as the number of available data samples increases, the error in the inferred graph decreases much more rapidly for the joint approach.

![comparison-of-estimation-error-for-open-loop-ol-and-closed-loop-cl-methods-across-different-numbers-of-input-processes-m](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27455v1/fig_exp1_error_vf.png)

The framework was applied to two distinct real-world problems:

1.  **Urban Mobility (Uber in NYC):** Using pickup data across Manhattan, the method was used to infer the underlying flow of traffic. Unlike previous undirected models, this framework successfully identified directed patterns. For instance, it captured the dominant flow of traffic from Manhattan toward residential areas and airports during the afternoon and evening hours.

2.  **Financial Portfolio Optimization:** The method was used to infer the latent dependency graph between stocks. By understanding these directed influences, the authors constructed investment portfolios. Portfolios based on the directed graphs inferred by this method achieved higher cumulative returns than standard benchmarks and methods that assume undirected relationships.

## Conclusion

The study of directed graphs is essential for accurately modeling complex systems where influences are not symmetric. This paper provides a robust mathematical and algorithmic foundation for inferring digraph topologies from diffused signals, even when those signals are excited by complex, correlated inputs. By formalizing the identifiability of asymmetric filters and providing an integrated optimization framework, the authors bridge a significant gap in the graph signal processing literature. The computational complexity of the algorithms remains manageable at $O(N^3)$, making the framework applicable to networks of moderate size while paving the way for future research into larger, more dynamic network structures.
