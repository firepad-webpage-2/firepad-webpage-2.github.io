## Automating Digital Dentistry through Robotics

Intraoral scanning is the cornerstone of modern digital dentistry. By replacing traditional, uncomfortable physical impressions with digital optical impressions, it enables a range of workflows including prosthodontics, implants, and orthodontics. These scanners provide high-resolution three-dimensional (3D) data essential for computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM). However, despite the benefits, the process remains highly manual. Clinicians must maneuver a hand-held scanner within the cramped confines of a patient's mouth, managing viewing angles, distances, and path consistency. This labor-intensive task is prone to operator error, often resulting in incomplete tooth coverage or inconsistent data quality.

RobOralScan introduces a robotic system designed to automate this process. By framing intraoral scanning as an active perception problem, the system uses a robotic arm equipped with an intraoral sensor to autonomously navigate the dental arch. Unlike static, pre-defined scan paths, RobOralScan employs a reinforcement learning (RL) policy that responds to real-time feedback. This allows the robot to dynamically adjust its trajectory based on the areas already scanned and the regions that require more detail, ensuring a comprehensive reconstruction of the patient's teeth and gingiva.

## The Intraoral Scanning Challenge

The primary difficulty in automating dental scanning lies in the combination of a small scanner field-of-view and a highly constrained workspace. Traditional robotic path planning often relies on a complete prior model of the environment. In a clinical setting, however, every patient's oral anatomy is unique, and the robot must "explore" the dental arch as it scans.

Current automated approaches often fall into two categories: scripted anatomy-guided sweeps and classical "Next-Best-View" (NBV) algorithms. Scripted sweeps follow a fixed path based on average dental anatomy. While efficient, they fail to adapt to individual variations, often missing surfaces on crowded or misaligned teeth. NBV algorithms, common in generic 3D reconstruction, maximize information gain by moving the sensor to areas with the highest uncertainty. While theoretically sound, these generic objectives often struggle in the dental domain because they do not prioritize the specific surfaces—such as the buccal (cheek-side) or lingual (tongue-side) tooth faces—that are critical for clinical use.

RobOralScan addresses these limitations by formulating the task as a feedback-driven sequential control problem. The robot acts as an agent in an environment where its goal is to maximize the coverage of individual teeth while maintaining safe and efficient motion.

## Geometric Memory and Observation Space

To make informed decisions, the RL policy needs to understand what it has already seen and what remains to be scanned. RobOralScan implements a "Geometric Memory" that provides a global context of the scanning progress. At each time step $t$, the system maintains a tri-state volumetric memory, denoted as $\sigma_t$. This is a 3D voxel grid of size $H \times W \times D$ that represents the scanning workspace. Each voxel in the grid is classified into one of three states:

1.  **Occupied:** The voxel contains surface points from the accumulated point cloud $\bar{P}_t$.
2.  **Free:** The voxel has been traversed by the scanner’s line-of-sight but contains no surface, indicating empty space.
3.  **Unknown:** The voxel has not yet been observed by the scanner.

This tri-state representation is superior to simple binary occupancy (occupied vs. empty) because it explicitly identifies unexplored "frontiers." By distinguishing between space known to be empty and space that is simply unknown, the policy can more effectively target regions that have been missed.

In addition to the geometric memory, the policy receives a proprioception vector $o_{prop_t}$. This vector contains 21 dimensions of data, including the robot's joint positions, joint velocities, and the tool center point (TCP) position and orientation. The combined observation $o_t$ allows the policy to reason about both the dental reconstruction progress and its own physical configuration.

## Adaptive Action Selection

The RL policy, $\pi_\theta$, does not plan a full trajectory in advance. Instead, it generates incremental 6-Degrees-of-Freedom (DoF) relative pose offsets $a_t$. These offsets include translation ($\delta x, \delta y, \delta z$) and rotation ($\delta \phi, \delta \theta, \delta \psi$) defined in the scanner's current local frame.

This incremental approach is essential for intraoral scanning because the environment is too tight for large, sweeping motions. The policy can perform fine-grained adjustments to "peek" around a tooth or get closer to a difficult-to-reach interproximal space. These relative actions are then converted into robot joint movements using differential inverse kinematics, ensuring the scanner reaches the target pose smoothly.

## Learning Balanced Tooth-wise Coverage

A key contribution of RobOralScan is its reward function, which is designed to ensure that the robot does not just scan the "easy" parts of the mouth. If a policy only maximizes global coverage, it might ignore a single tooth that is hard to reach while perfectly scanning the others. In dentistry, however, a missing tooth surface can make the entire scan clinically useless.

The total reward $r_t$ is a weighted sum of four specific components:

$$r_t = r_{cov_t} + r_{eq_t} + r_{safe_t} + r_{eff_t}$$

The coverage reward $r_{cov_t}$ provides basic incentives for discovering new surfaces. The equity reward $r_{eq_t}$ is more complex; it uses a tooth-wise equity score $S_t$ to prioritize scanning the least-covered teeth:

$$S_t = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K c_{k,t} + \text{mean\_tail}(\{c_{k,t}\}_{k=1}^K) \right)$$

where $c_{k,t}$ is the coverage of tooth $k$ at time $t$. By including the "mean\_tail" (the average coverage of the least-scanned teeth), the reward forces the policy to go back and finish teeth that are lagging behind. 

Furthermore, the system includes scan safety rewards $r_{safe_t}$ to keep the scanner within an optimal working distance and workspace efficiency rewards $r_{eff_t}$ to discourage erratic or unnecessarily long paths.

## Progressive Training Curriculum

Training a policy to handle all these constraints simultaneously is difficult due to the "sparse reward" problem—the robot might wander for a long time before accidentally achieving high coverage on all teeth. To solve this, the researchers use a progressive training scheme. 

The training is divided into stages with increasing difficulty. In the early stages, the robot is only required to achieve a modest global coverage. As it succeeds, the requirements become stricter, eventually demanding high per-tooth coverage across the entire arch (e.g., at least 85% coverage for every individual tooth). This curriculum allows the agent to learn basic navigation before fine-tuning the precision required for complete dental reconstruction.

## Performance and Real-World Validation

The researchers evaluated RobOralScan against several baseline methods. Quantitative results showed that RobOralScan significantly outperformed classical NBV and scripted methods. In metrics such as the Chamfer Distance (which measures the accuracy of the reconstruction) and the lower-tail coverage (the coverage of the "worst" tooth), the proposed method showed a clear advantage. For instance, the "Coverage Completion Only" baseline—which lacks the equity reward—failed to achieve complete tooth-wise success, often leaving difficult teeth under-scanned.

To demonstrate the practical utility of the system, the researchers conducted a "sim-to-real" experiment. They took the policy trained entirely in a simulator and deployed it on a physical Franka Research 3 robotic arm equipped with a real intraoral scanner. Despite the differences in sensor noise and mechanical latencies, the robot successfully performed a zero-shot scan of a dental phantom model. As shown in {fig:roboralscan-hardware-validation}, the system was able to progressively build a full-arch reconstruction, proving that the learned scanning strategy is robust enough for physical hardware.

## Conclusion

RobOralScan provides a framework for automating one of the most tedious tasks in digital dentistry. By combining tri-state geometric memory with a tooth-aware reward structure and a progressive learning curriculum, the system achieves a level of autonomous coverage that exceeds traditional automated methods. 

The shift from manual scanning to robotic automation offers the potential for more consistent, high-quality digital impressions, regardless of the clinician's experience level. While future work will need to address the complexities of a live patient's mouth—including soft tissue movement and tongue interference—this research establishes a foundation for intelligent, adaptive robotic assistants in the dental office.
