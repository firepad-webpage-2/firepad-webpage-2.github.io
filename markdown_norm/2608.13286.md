## Foundations of Multivalued Fixed Point Theory

Fixed point theory is a cornerstone of functional analysis, providing tools to determine if a mapping $T$ has a point $x$ such that $x = Tx$. Such points, called fixed points, are essential for solving various equations across mathematics, engineering, and economics. Traditionally, this theory began with single-valued mappings in complete metric spaces, most notably through the Banach Contraction Principle. However, many real-world problems require the study of multivalued mappings, which assign a set of possible outputs to a single input.

This research paper expands upon these foundations by investigating fixed points for a sophisticated class of multivalued mappings within $b$-metric spaces. Specifically, it focuses on Hardy–Rogers type contractions, which are generalized conditions that incorporate multiple distance metrics between points and their images. By moving beyond simple constant contraction factors and exploring the behavior of iterated mappings, the authors provide a more flexible framework for ensuring the existence and uniqueness of fixed points.

## The b-Metric Space and Hausdorff Distance

A central element of this work is the use of $b$-metric spaces. In a standard metric space, the triangle inequality dictates that the distance between two points is always less than or equal to the sum of the distances through a third point. A $b$-metric space relaxes this requirement by introducing a multiplier $s \geq 1$. Formally, a $b$-metric $d$ on a set $X$ satisfies the property:

$$d(x, z) \leq s[d(x, y) + d(y, z)]$$

for all $x, y, z \in X$. This relaxation allows the space to model scenarios that a standard metric cannot, such as certain function spaces or "snowflake" metrics. 

When dealing with multivalued mappings $T: X \to C(X)$, where $C(X)$ is the collection of nonempty closed subsets of $X$, measuring the "distance" between two sets becomes necessary. The authors utilize the generalized Hausdorff $b$-metric, denoted as $H(A, B)$, to quantify the separation between two sets $A$ and $B$. In this framework, a point $z$ is considered a fixed point if it is contained within the set produced by the mapping: $z \in Tz$.

## Hardy–Rogers Type Multivalued Mappings

The core contribution of this paper involves a generalized contractive condition named after Hardy and Rogers. While standard contractions rely on a single constant relating the distance between inputs to the distance between outputs, a Hardy–Rogers type mapping considers five different distance components. 

The authors define a mapping as a Hardy–Rogers type multivalued mapping if there exist five nonnegative control functions $a, b, c, e, f: [0, \infty) \to [0, 1)$ such that for all $x, y \in X$, the following inequality holds:

$$H(Tx, Ty) \leq a(d(x, y))d(x, y) + b(d(x, y))D(x, Tx) + c(d(x, y))D(y, Ty) + e(d(x, y))D(x, Ty) + f(d(x, y))D(y, Tx)$$

In this expression:
- $d(x, y)$ is the distance between the input points.
- $D(x, Tx)$ and $D(y, Ty)$ represent the distances from the points to their respective image sets.
- $D(x, Ty)$ and $D(y, Tx)$ represent cross-distances between a point and the image set of the other point.

A distinguishing feature of this research is that $a, b, c, e,$ and $f$ are not constants but distance-dependent control functions. This allows the contraction behavior to vary depending on the distance between the inputs, providing a more localized and adaptable model. The authors impose a global constraint on these functions to ensure convergence: $a(t) + b(t) + c(t) + 2s \min\{e(t), f(t)\} < 1$.

## Establishing Existence and Uniqueness

The primary theorem (Theorem 3.2) established by the authors provides conditions under which a Hardy–Rogers type multivalued mapping is guaranteed to have a fixed point. The proof follows a constructive approach: starting from an arbitrary point $x_0$, a sequence $x_0, x_1, x_2, \dots$ is generated such that $x_{n+1} \in Tx_n$. The authors prove that this sequence is Cauchy—meaning its terms get arbitrarily close to each other—and thus converges to a limit point $z$ in the complete $b$-metric space.

The critical step is determining if this limit point $z$ is indeed a fixed point ($z \in Tz$). The paper identifies three alternative criteria that ensure this:
1. **h-upper semicontinuity:** A topological property weaker than standard continuity. If the mapping $T$ is $h$-upper semicontinuous, the sequence's convergence is sufficient to identify $z$ as a fixed point.
2. **Limit condition on $c$ and $e$:** If $s \lim_{t \to 0^+} c(t) + s \lim_{t \to 0^+} e(t) < 1$.
3. **Limit condition on $b$ and $f$:** If $s \lim_{t \to 0^+} b(t) + s \lim_{t \to 0^+} f(t) < 1$.

Notably, these limit conditions are significant improvements over prior research. Earlier works often required the multiplier $s$ to be squared (e.g., $s^2$), creating a more restrictive condition. By reducing the power of $s$, the authors extend the theorem's applicability to a broader range of mappings.

Furthermore, the paper addresses the concept of a "strict fixed point," where the output set contains only the point itself: $Tx = \{x\}$. They prove that under certain conditions, if a strict fixed point exists, it is unique, and it is the only fixed point for the mapping.

## The p-Order Iteration Scheme

Another layer of generalization introduced in this study is the $p$-order iteration scheme. In many practical applications, a mapping $T$ might not satisfy a contractive condition on its own, but its iterates might. An iterate $T^p$ represents the process of applying the mapping $T$ repeatedly $p$ times.

Theorem 5.1 demonstrates that if the $p$-th iterate $T^p$ satisfies the Hardy–Rogers contractive conditions, then the original mapping $T$ is guaranteed to have a fixed point. This is particularly useful for complex operators that exhibit "eventual" contractive behavior. The authors show that if $z$ is the unique strict fixed point of $T^p$ (meaning $T^p z = \{z\}$), then $z$ must also be the unique fixed point of $T$.

This generalization allows researchers to analyze system stability and equilibrium points even when the immediate transformation does not appear to be a contraction, provided that the long-term behavior of the system (the iterates) is well-behaved.

## Significance of the Relaxed Conditions

The significance of this work lies in the systematic relaxation of requirements for fixed point existence. By replacing constants with distance-dependent functions and weakening the continuity requirements to $h$-upper semicontinuity, the authors create a toolset that is both more general and more precise.

The paper includes several illustrative examples to justify these generalizations. For instance, they provide a scenario where the standard conditions found in previous literature fail because the distance-dependent coefficients approach the limit too quickly relative to $s^2$. However, because the authors' new theorems only require a linear factor of $s$, the fixed point can still be guaranteed.

By incorporating cross-distances ($D(x, Ty)$ and $D(y, Tx)$) and the $p$-iterate framework, this research unifies several disparate branches of fixed point theory. It bridges the gap between mappings that are strictly contractive and those that only become contractive over time or under specific topological conditions. Consequently, these results provide a robust mathematical foundation for solving operator equations in non-standard spaces where traditional metrics and contraction principles are insufficient.
