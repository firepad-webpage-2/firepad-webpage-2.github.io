## The Dual Function of Public Labels

In many socioeconomic environments, public labels serve a two-fold purpose. Consider the example of university grades: they provide an informative signal to potential employers about a student's proficiency, but they also act as a set of targets that incentivize students to study (and perhaps struggle) to achieve them. Such labels, which include professional certifications, credit scores, and product ratings, are ubiquitous.

The paper "Optimal Classification with Costly Self-Selection" by Mark Whitmeyer explores the design of these classification systems. Specifically, it addresses the trade-off between the informational utility that a "receiver" (like an employer) gains from precise labels and the "signaling waste" incurred by "senders" (like students) who must expend effort or resources to earn those labels. The central question the research answers is which distinctions are actually worth making—"which rungs are worth building" on a classification ladder when each rung forces agents to compete and pay a price for entry.

## A Mathematical Framework for Classification

To analyze this problem, the research employs a model involving a sender, a receiver, and an information designer. The sender is characterized by a private "type" represented by its quantile $q \in [0, 1]$. Each quantile corresponds to an underlying quality $x(q)$, where the quality function $x:[0, 1] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is strictly increasing. 

To obtain a specific label or certificate, a sender must choose an action $a \in [0, \infty)$. Performing this action is costly, and this cost depends on the sender's quality. Specifically, the cost is $aS(x(q))$, where $S(\cdot) > 0$ is a strictly decreasing function. This implies that higher-quality agents find it "cheaper" to signal their worth, a standard assumption in signaling theory (the Single-Crossing Property).

The receiver observes the certificate issued by the designer and derives a utility $r(m)$ based on the posterior mean $m$ of the sender's quality. The sender, in turn, receives a private benefit $b(m)$ from the certificate but must subtract the signaling cost, leading to a total payoff of $b(m) - aS(x(q))$.

The designer’s goal is to maximize social welfare, which is the sum of the receiver's utility and the sender's net payoff. By committing to a certification technology $\gamma: [0, \infty) \rightarrow Y$ (which maps actions to certificates), the designer effectively chooses an "admissible signal" $M(q)$, representing the expected quality assigned to each type in equilibrium.

## The Cost of Distinctions

A primary contribution of this work is the derivation of a formula that quantifies exactly how much it "costs" to maintain a specific set of classifications. The paper demonstrates that any equilibrium results in a pair $(M, z)$, where $M(q)$ is the posterior mean for type $q$ and $z(q)$ is the minimum action needed to reach that label.

The research introduces a critical concept called "mimicry pressure," defined as:
$$
L(q) := \frac{\int_q^1 S(x(t))dt}{S(x(q))}
$$
The value $L(q)$ represents the cumulative weight of all types above $q$ relative to the signaling cost of type $q$. Using this, the paper shows that the minimum expected signaling cost $K(M)$ required to implement a given signal $M$ is:
$$
K(M) = \int_{(0,1]} L(q)d[b(M(q))]
$$
This formula reveals that every time the designer creates a "distinction" (a jump in the sender's benefit $b(m)$), it creates an incentive for lower types to mimic higher types. This "pressure" forces the higher types to take more extreme (and costly) actions to prove they belong in the higher category. The total social waste is thus the sum of these "jump" costs, each weighted by the mimicry pressure at the boundary where the distinction is made.

The designer's objective is then simplified to a single optimization problem:
$$
\sup_{M \in \mathcal{M}} \left\{ \int_0^1 r(m(q))dq - \int_{(0,1]} L(q)d[b(M(q))] \right\}
$$
where $M$ is the posterior mean function (the "ladder" of labels).

## The Inefficiency of Perfect Information

One might assume that "more information is better" and that the optimal system should perfectly distinguish every type—a scenario called "full certification," where $M_F(q) = x(q)$. However, this research proves that full certification is generally not optimal.

The core reason is the "bottom-rung" problem. If you try to distinguish between the very lowest types (the "worst" students or the "lowest-rated" products), the informational gain for the receiver is often very small because these types are already quite similar. However, the cost to keep these types separate—the signaling effort required—is a "first-order" cost. 

Specifically, the paper shows that by pooling a small interval of types at the bottom $[0, \epsilon]$ into a single "failing" or "entry-level" category, the designer achieves a strict welfare improvement. The loss in information is negligible (second-order), while the savings in signaling costs across the entire population are substantial (first-order). In practical terms, this suggests that the lowest rung of any classification ladder should be a "wide" pool rather than a series of fine distinctions.

## Lower Censorship and Finite Categories

The study further characterizes the structure of the optimal classification rule. In many cases, the best approach is "lower censorship." This involves creating one large pool at the bottom, $[0, c^*]$, and then providing perfectly distinct labels for every type above $c^*$. 

Under certain conditions—specifically when the mimicry pressure $L(q)$ is convex, the receiver's value function $r$ is convex, and the sender's benefit $b$ is weakly concave—this single-cutoff strategy is optimal. It creates a "default" label for lower types, allowing them to avoid costly signaling altogether, while maintaining high-resolution information for high-quality agents.

Furthermore, the paper addresses the question of why we don't see infinite categories in the real world. In many signaling games, adding a new, tiny distinction between two labels might consume more resources in competition than it provides in informational value. The research identifies a property called "local wastefulness." If sufficiently small distinctions always reduce welfare, then any optimal classification must involve only a finite number of categories. This explains why grading systems use discrete letters (A, B, C) or star ratings (1-5) rather than providing a continuous percentile rank for every single agent.

## Generalizations and Tournaments

The findings in this paper are notably robust. While the primary model assumes the receiver cares about the mean quality, the results generalize to any receiver payoff function $V$ that is continuous and depends on the full distribution of types. Whether the receiver is trying to hire the best person, avoid the worst person, or match agents to specific tasks, the principle that blurring the bottom of the distribution improves welfare remains true.

The paper also extends its logic to signaling tournaments. In a tournament, an agent's prize is determined by their rank relative to others. Even in this competitive setting, the "cost of distinctions" logic applies. Just as in a grading system, a tournament designer who wishes to maximize total welfare should avoid making fine distinctions among the lowest performers. By "erasing" the bottom distinctions, the designer reduces the aggregate effort wasted by competitors who have little chance of winning the top prizes anyway, without significantly affecting the outcome for the elite performers.

## Significance of the Research

This research provides a rigorous theoretical foundation for the design of "coarse" classification systems. It moves beyond the idea that coarseness is a mere convenience or a result of limited cognitive capacity. Instead, it shows that coarseness is an essential tool for social efficiency. 

By explicitly linking the "mimicry pressure" from lower types to the aggregate waste in a system, the work provides a blueprint for institutional designers. The primary lesson is clear: when labels are earned through effort, trying to be perfectly precise is often counterproductive. The most effective systems are those that realize when a distinction is "not worth building" and choose to leave the lower end of the quality spectrum intentionally blurred.
