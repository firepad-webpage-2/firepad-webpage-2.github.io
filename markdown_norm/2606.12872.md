## Understanding Scheduled Event Risk in Option Markets

Modern financial markets are highly sensitive to scheduled macroeconomic announcements, such as interest rate decisions from the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), Consumer Price Index (CPI) inflation data, and Nonfarm Payroll (NFP) employment reports. These events often trigger immediate and significant price movements in equity indices. For investors and market makers, accurately pricing the risk associated with these discrete "jumps" in the market is critical. 

Short-dated options, particularly S&P 500 index (SPX) options that expire daily, provide a high-resolution lens through which these risks can be viewed. Because these contracts can be chosen to expire either just before or just after a scheduled announcement, they allow for the isolation of the "event premium"— the extra cost the market charges for protection against the volatility specific to that event. However, a significant technical hurdle exists: how can we be sure a model is genuinely identifying the risk of a scheduled jump rather than just smoothing over a general increase in market volatility? 

The research presented here addresses this "identification problem" by proposing a rigorous protocol that separates continuous market movements from discrete event-based jumps. By using a held-out pricing test and machine learning benchmarks, the study validates how scheduled event risk is reflected in option prices.

## The Identification Challenge: Spanning vs. Non-Spanning

To value an option, one must model the distribution of the underlying asset's returns until the option's expiration. In standard models, this distribution is often assumed to be continuous. When a scheduled event occurs, the distribution likely experiences a discrete "jump" at a specific, known time $e$.

The fundamental difficulty in identifying this jump lies in the flexibility of modern option pricing surfaces. If a researcher fits a highly flexible implied-volatility surface to all available options on a given day, that surface might naturally "bend" to account for the higher prices of options that span an event. While this results in a good fit, it does not necessarily mean the model has identified the event risk; it might have simply absorbed the premium into the parameters of the continuous volatility model.

To solve this, the paper introduces the **non-spanning identification protocol**. This protocol strictly partitions the information set available at quote time $t$ for a target event occurring at time $e$:

1.  **Non-spanning contracts ($\mathcal{D}_{t, e}^0$):** These are options that expire before the event ($T_i < e$). Because they do not cover the event period, they contain no information about the event jump. These are used exclusively to fit the continuous, "no-event" volatility surface.
2.  **Event-spanning training contracts ($\mathcal{D}_{t, e}^{1, \text{tr}}$):** These options expire after the event ($T_i \geq e$) and are used to calibrate the parameters of the scheduled jump.
3.  **Held-out event-spanning contracts ($\mathcal{D}_{t, e}^{1, \text{ho}}$):** A subset of event-spanning options that are never seen during calibration. They are used only for final evaluation to test how well the model generalizes.

This separation ensures that the continuous volatility component is identified solely from data that cannot "know" about the event, forcing the model to use the explicit jump term to explain any price differences in the event-spanning options.

## Modeling Discrete Jumps

The paper decomposes the log return of the index at expiration $T_i$ into two primary parts:

$$
R_{\theta, i} = X_{t, T_i}^c + J_{e, t} I_{i, t, e}
$$

In this equation, $X_{t, T_i}^c$ represents the continuous market risk accumulated over time, and $J_{e, t}$ is the discrete jump associated with the scheduled event $e$. The indicator variable $I_{i, t, e} = 1\{T_i \geq e\}$ ensures the jump is only added to options that actually span the event date. 

The continuous component $X_{t, T_i}^c$ is modeled as a Gaussian distribution with a variance $v_i$ derived from a variance-rate surface $w_c(k, \tau)$, where $k$ is the log-moneyness and $\tau$ is the time to maturity. The research tests several ways to model the jump $J_{e, t}$:

*   **Event Gaussian:** The jump is modeled as a simple normal distribution $J_{e, t} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_J, \sigma_J^2)$. The model must satisfy a martingale condition, $\mathbb{E}_Q[\exp(J_{e, t})] = 1$, which ensures that the jump itself does not introduce an expected price drift under the risk-neutral measure. This leaves a single free parameter to calibrate for the event: the jump scale $s_e$ (the standard deviation of the jump).
*   **Event Mixture:** Recognizing that event risk might be more complex than a simple bell curve, this model uses a mixture of two Gaussian distributions. This allows for more sophisticated shapes, such as distributions with "fat tails" or even multiple peaks (bimodality), representing scenarios where the market might jump sharply in either direction but is unlikely to stay still.

## Machine Learning and the Transferability of Risk

A key question in financial econometrics is whether the "shape" of risk is consistent across different events. For example, does a CPI announcement always create the same kind of uncertainty, just with different magnitudes?

To investigate this, the study employs **Mixture Density Networks (MDNs)**. These are neural networks trained to output the parameters of a Gaussian mixture distribution. The authors use a "leave-one-event-out" approach: to price a target event (e.g., a specific FOMC meeting), the network is trained on all other historical events in the dataset. 

This model, referred to as the **Event Neural-MDN**, attempts to predict the jump density using only pre-event state information, such as recent market volatility and the characteristics of non-spanning options. This tests whether the jump density shape is "amortized"—meaning it can be learned once and applied to new, unseen events.

The study further refines this with a **Semi-Amortized** variant. This model takes the jump shape predicted by the neural network but allows for one single parameter, the event-specific scale $\gamma_e$, to be calibrated using the target event's own data. This helps determine how much of the pricing improvement comes from knowing the general *shape* of event risk versus knowing the specific *size* of the risk for a particular day.

## Key Findings and Empirical Evidence

The study analyzed SPX options around 101 major macroeconomic events from 2022 to 2025. The results provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of explicit jump modeling under the non-spanning protocol.

### Performance Gains
The models incorporating explicit jumps significantly outperformed the "No-Event" baseline. For instance, the Event Gaussian model reduced the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) in implied volatility (IV) from 0.1080 to 0.0970. The Event Mixture model, which allows for more complex jump shapes, performed even better, lowering the IV MAE to 0.0926 and doubling the "bid-ask containment" (the percentage of time the model price falls within the market's spread).

### Scale vs. Shape
The neural network experiments yielded a crucial insight: the *scale* of the jump is more important than the *shape* for basic pricing accuracy. The pure Neural-MDN (which tried to guess everything about the jump from past events) showed only marginal improvement. However, when the neural shape was combined with a single calibrated scale parameter ($\gamma_e$), the performance jumped to match the Event Gaussian model. That said, the Event Mixture model still provided the best overall fit, suggesting that event-specific "higher-moment" information (like specific skewness or tail thickness) still holds residual value that cannot be fully captured by general cross-event patterns.

### Event Type Heterogeneity
The results were not uniform across all types of news. CPI and FOMC announcements showed the strongest evidence of being priced as discrete jumps. In contrast, Nonfarm Payroll (NFP) reports showed weaker results, with the calibrated jump scale often hitting the lower bound. This suggests that the market may price employment data more as a change in continuous volatility rather than a sharp, deterministic-time jump, or that the risk is simply less pronounced in the options market compared to inflation and interest rate news.

### Impact on Option Strategies
The study also looked at specific combinations of options, such as straddles (bets on volatility) and risk reversals (bets on direction). The jump models primarily improved the pricing of straddles and strangles, which are sensitive to the "convexity" or uncertainty of the event. They did not significantly change the pricing of risk reversals, indicating that these scheduled jumps are primarily perceived as uncertainty shocks rather than directional biases.

## Significance for the Field

This research provides a rigorous framework for validating event-risk models. By proving the necessity of the non-spanning identification protocol, it sets a higher bar for how researchers and practitioners should test their models. The "contaminated-surface stress test" performed in the paper showed that failing to use this protocol leads to spuriously low errors—not because the model is better, but because it is "cheating" by seeing event information it shouldn't have.

For market participants, the study demonstrates that even simple jump models, when properly identified, can materially improve the valuation and hedging of short-dated options. The findings on scale and shape transferability also suggest that while machine learning can help capture the general "vibe" of an announcement, there is no substitute for calibrating the specific magnitude of risk to the current market environment.
