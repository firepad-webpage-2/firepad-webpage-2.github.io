## Human Activity as a Priority Queue

Human dynamics research often treats individuals as agents managing a "to-do list" or a priority queue. In this framework, every action—sending an email, making a phone call, or attending a meeting—is a task waiting to be executed. Traditional models typically assume that tasks are assigned a priority, and the agent simply picks the highest-priority item at each step. This approach has successfully explained the heavy-tailed timing of human activities, where long periods of inactivity are punctuated by bursts of intense action. These patterns are characterized by interevent time distributions following a power law $P(\tau) \sim \tau^{-\alpha}$, with specific exponents like $\alpha = 1$ or $\alpha = 3/2$ appearing across different types of communication.

![interacting-agents](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.14398v1/img-0.jpeg)
*{fig:interacting-agents}: A schematic representing two agents, A and B, who each manage a queue of private tasks (O) and a shared interacting task (I). The shared task only executes when both agents simultaneously select it as their highest priority.*

However, these models often miss a crucial element of human interaction: agency. In reality, shared tasks require coordination. If you consistently try to reach a friend who never responds, you eventually stop trying. Previous models assigned shared tasks a fixed priority, ignoring the fact that individuals adapt their behavior based on their partners' responsiveness. This research addresses that gap by introducing a strategic decision-making layer. Instead of drawing priorities from a fixed distribution, agents adaptively set the priority of shared tasks based on their estimate of a partner's willingness to participate. This shift moves the focus from simple timing statistics to the study of collective states and phase transitions, revealing how social ties are maintained, why they collapse, and what it takes to rebuild them.

## The Strategic Decision Rule

The core innovation of this model is the treatment of priority as a decision variable rather than a random number. Consider an agent $j$ who has $L_j$ total tasks. One of these is a shared interacting task $I$, while the remaining $a_j = L_j - 1$ are private tasks. At each time step, the agent must decide the priority $x_{I,j}$ for the shared task. The priorities for private tasks are drawn from a uniform distribution, and the agent selects the task with the highest priority to execute.

To make an informed decision, the agent maintains an estimate $\hat{p}$ of the partner's probability of selecting the shared task. This estimate is updated using an exponential moving average with a memory constant $\tau_{mem}$. The update rule is defined as:

$$\hat{p} \to \lambda \hat{p} + (1 - \lambda) S$$

where $\lambda = e^{-1/\tau_{mem}}$ is the retention rate and $S$ is an indicator variable (1 if the partner offered the task, 0 otherwise). Based on this estimate, the agent sets an optimal priority $x^*_I$ to maximize their expected payoff, accounting for a reward $R_I$ for successful coordination and a cost $c$ for a wasted effort (an unreciprocated offer). The resulting decision rule is:

$$x^*_I = \min\left\{1, \max\left\{0, \frac{R_I - c}{(1 - \hat{p})/\hat{p}}\right\}\right\}$$

This policy clips the priority between 0 and 1. If the partner is perceived as unlikely to participate, the priority drops to zero, and the agent focuses entirely on private tasks. If the partner is highly responsive, the agent may set the priority to the maximum value of 1.

## Absorbing Phase Transitions

The interplay between these adaptive agents leads to two distinct collective states: a "coupled phase" and a "solitary phase." In the coupled phase, agents maintain high mutual estimates of participation, and the interaction persists. In the solitary phase, the interaction collapses, and the agents stop offering the shared task entirely.

![return-map](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.14398v1/img-0.jpeg)
*{fig:interacting-agents}: The return map $F(p) = p$ illustrating the saddle-node bifurcation. As the reward $R_I$ increases, a pair of fixed points (stable and unstable) emerges, allowing for a sustained coupled phase.*

The transition between these phases is a saddle-node bifurcation. Analytically, this means that for a given reward $R_I$ and cost $c$, there is a critical participation rate $p^*$ above which the interaction is self-sustaining. If the reward falls below a critical value $R_c$, the coupled phase disappears, and the system inevitably falls into the solitary phase. 

Crucially, the solitary phase is "absorbing." In the absence of an exploration rate $\epsilon$ (a small probability of offering a task regardless of belief), once the interaction stops, it never restarts. This is because both agents' estimates $\hat{p}$ eventually decay to zero, confirming their mutual belief that the other is not participating. This provides a formal mathematical explanation for why interrupted social ties often decay permanently if a rhythm of interaction is not maintained.

## Cognitive Depth and the Cost of Rebuilding

The research distinguishes between levels of cognitive sophistication in agents. Level 1 agents simply track their partner's participation. Level 2 agents go a step further: they model their partner's model of them. These Level 2 agents evaluate whether a short-term investment in offering the task (even when the partner's current participation is low) will eventually drive the partner's estimate $\hat{p}$ high enough to restore the coupled phase.

The simulations show that Level 2 agents are much more resilient. In asymmetric pairings, a single Level 2 agent can sustain a relationship that would otherwise collapse between two Level 1 agents. However, restoring a relationship that has already collapsed into the absorbing solitary state requires a significant "commitment length." Even when the partner's belief has hit zero, an agent can restore the tie by unilaterally offering the task for a specific duration. 

The study finds that the minimum patience required to restore a tie, $k_{min} \approx 0.74 \tau_{mem}$, is a fixed multiple of the memory time. This suggests that rebuilding a relationship is not a matter of one-off gestures but requires consistent effort over a period comparable to the time it took for the relationship to be forgotten. Furthermore, agents must have a long enough "discount horizon" $H = 1 / (1 - \gamma) \approx 1.5 \tau_{mem}$ to make this persistent effort a rational choice.

## Networks and the Liability of Hubs

When the model is extended to networks, agents must divide their limited attention among multiple neighbors. In this context, an agent with many connections (a hub) becomes a bottleneck. The effective priority they can give to any single interaction decreases as their degree $k$ increases.

![network-fragmentation](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.14398v1/img-5.jpeg)
*{fig:network-fragmentation}: The size of the giant coupled component $S$ as a function of the cost $c$ for different network types. As costs increase or attention is spread too thin, the network fragments into a solitary phase.*

The research derives a critical degree $k_c$, beyond which an agent can no longer sustain a coupled interaction. This provides a theoretical basis for observed limits on social capacity, such as Dunbar's number. Relationships involving high-degree nodes are the first to collapse, effectively acting as an endogenous "targeted attack" on the network's hubs. While scale-free networks are usually robust to random failures, this model suggests they are inherently fragile to the scarcity of human attention.

## Coordination in Groups

Finally, the paper examines coordination in groups of size $m$, where a task only executes if all $m$ members select it. As the group size increases, the probability of simultaneous coordination drops exponentially. The critical degree $d_c(m)$ (the number of groups an agent can participate in) falls steeply as $m$ increases.

For groups of $m \geq 3$, the model predicts that sustained coordination is extremely difficult to maintain. Even with high rewards, the noise in individual belief estimates can easily lead to a "coordination failure" that drives the group into the absorbing solitary phase. This provides a queueing-theory explanation for why large, unorganized conversational groups often naturally split into smaller clusters of 2-4 people.

## Conclusion

By moving from fixed task priorities to adaptive decision-making, this research demonstrates that the fundamental structure of human activity is governed by phase transitions. The existence of an absorbing solitary phase explains the fragility and irreversibility of social tie decay. The constraints imposed by attention scarcity and memory duration define the limits of our social networks and the difficulty of coordinating in groups. Ultimately, the work suggests that maintaining social ties is a delicate balance of reciprocal participation, where the cost of silence is the eventual collapse of the interaction itself.
