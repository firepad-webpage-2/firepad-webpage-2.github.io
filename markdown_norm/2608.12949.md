## Introduction to Bayesian A/B Testing

In the world of online product development, A/B testing serves as the scientific foundation for decision-making. By comparing a treatment variant against a control, companies can measure the impact of new features, UI changes, or algorithmic updates. Traditionally, these experiments have relied on frequentist statistics, utilizing p-values and confidence intervals to manage the risk of making incorrect decisions. However, Bayesian inference has emerged as an increasingly popular alternative, favored for its intuitive interpretation and the flexibility it offers in updating beliefs as data arrives.

Despite this popularity, the term "Bayesian A/B testing" is often used to describe a wide array of different methodologies, some of which provide very different statistical guarantees. A fundamental challenge for practitioners is that a "Bayesian" label alone does not specify how well a method will control for false positives or how accurately it will estimate treatment effects. Without a systematic understanding of these differences, teams may inadvertently adopt procedures that fail to manage the risks inherent in their experimentation program.

Research from Spotify’s experimentation team addresses this confusion by categorizing Bayesian A/B testing into a three-tier hierarchy of statistical guarantees. This structure allows researchers to evaluate methods based on their specific risk management properties: posterior coherence, false positive rate control, and false discovery rate calibration. By breaking down Bayesian configurations into these tiers, the work clarifies how choices like prior selection and stopping rules dictate the reliability of an experiment’s results.

## The Hierarchy of Statistical Guarantees

The core contribution of this work is the organization of Bayesian procedures into three levels, each building upon the previous one to offer stronger frequentist-style guarantees.

**Tier 1: Posterior Coherence.** This tier encompasses any procedure that generates a valid posterior distribution $\pi(\delta | \text{data})$ using a proper prior $\pi(\delta)$. At this level, the primary guarantee is that beliefs are updated consistently according to the Likelihood Principle. While this allows for an intuitive interpretation of the "probability that the treatment is better than the control," it does not naturally provide protection against the errors that occur when one repeatedly checks the data (a practice known as "peeking").

**Tier 2: Bounded False Positive Rate (FPR).** The second tier introduces the requirement that the experiment's False Positive Rate be bounded below a specific threshold, such as $\alpha$, even if the experimenter monitors the results continuously. This is achieved by moving away from simple posterior probabilities and instead using Bayes factor stopping rules. Because Bayes factors behave as non-negative martingales under the null hypothesis, they allow for "anytime-valid" inference.

**Tier 3: Bounded False Discovery Rate (FDR) and Calibrated Shrinkage.** The most advanced tier utilizes Empirical Bayes (EB) methods to calibrate the prior based on historical experiment data. By learning the distribution of true effects within a specific experimentation program, this tier ensures that the False Discovery Rate—the proportion of significant results that are actually null—is controlled. Additionally, it provides "calibrated shrinkage," which pulls noisy estimates toward zero to provide more accurate predictions of the true treatment effect $\delta$.

## Tier 1: The Danger of Naive Peeking

In many commercial experimentation platforms, the default Bayesian approach involves calculating the posterior probability that the treatment effect $\delta$ is positive, where $\delta = \mu_T - \mu_C$. A common rule is to stop the experiment and "ship" the treatment if $P(\delta > 0 | \text{data}) > 0.95$. 

However, if an experimenter uses a flat or uninformative prior and checks the results multiple times as data accumulates, this Tier 1 approach leads to significant inflation of the False Positive Rate. Simulations show that if an experimenter stops as soon as the probability hits 0.95, the actual chance of claiming a win for a treatment that has no real effect can rise as high as 30% or more, far exceeding the intended 5%.

This occurs because Tier 1 guarantees only "coherence"—that the posterior is a valid summary of the data *given the prior*—but does not account for the selection bias introduced by the stopping rule itself. This highlights a critical lesson: a posterior distribution alone is insufficient for risk management in environments where data is monitored sequentially.

## Tier 2: Using Bayes Factors for Sequential Testing

To solve the problem of FPR inflation, Tier 2 configurations utilize the Bayes Factor ($BF_{10}$), which is the ratio of the likelihood of the data under the alternative hypothesis ($H_1$) to the likelihood under the null hypothesis ($H_0$). Formally, for a given prior $\pi(\delta)$, the Bayes factor is defined as:

$$BF_{10} = \frac{\int \pi(\text{data} | \delta) \pi(\delta) d\delta}{\pi(\text{data} | \delta = 0)}$$

The key advantage of the Bayes factor is its relationship to Ville’s inequality. Under the null hypothesis, the sequence of Bayes factors as data arrives is a martingale. This mathematical property ensures that the probability of the Bayes factor ever exceeding a threshold $1/\alpha$ is at most $\alpha$. 

Consequently, stopping an experiment when $BF_{10} > 1/\alpha$ provides a strict bound on the False Positive Rate, regardless of how often or for how long the experimenter looks at the data. This makes Tier 2 methods "always-valid," providing a Bayesian counterpart to frequentist sequential testing methods like the mixture Sequential Probability Ratio Test (mSPRT).

## Tier 3: Empirical Bayes and Program-Wide Control

While Tier 2 protects against individual false positives, Tier 3 aims to optimize the entire experimentation program. It does this by using a mixture prior that reflects the reality of most product development: most ideas have no effect, and the ones that do typically have small effects. A common structure for this prior is a "spike-and-slab" model:

$$\pi(\delta) = (1-p)\delta_0 + p N(0, V^2)$$

Here, $(1-p)$ represents the fraction of experiments that are truly null (the spike at $\delta_0$), and $p$ represents the fraction that have a non-null effect following a normal distribution with variance $V^2$ (the slab). By estimating the parameters $\hat{p}$ and $\hat{V}$ from a corpus of historical A/B tests, practitioners can implement Tier 3 guarantees.

Tier 3 provides two major benefits:
1.  **FDR Control:** Because the prior accurately reflects the frequency of null results in the program, the resulting posterior probabilities are "calibrated." If the model says there is a 90% chance of a real effect, then across many experiments, approximately 90% of them will indeed be real.
2.  **Calibrated Shrinkage:** When an experiment shows a large observed effect $\hat{\delta}$, Tier 3 methods realize that such a large effect is statistically unlikely given historical data. The posterior mean "shrinks" the estimate toward zero, providing a more realistic prediction of the variant's performance once shipped.

## The Role of Corpus Quality

The power of Tier 3 depends entirely on the quality of the historical data used to train the Empirical Bayes prior. The research identifies several failure modes that can undermine these guarantees:
*   **Winner-selected corpora:** If a company only records the results of experiments that were "successful" or significant, the estimated $\hat{p}$ will be biased toward 1. This makes the prior too optimistic and destroys the FDR control.
*   **Pooled programs:** Combining data from highly successful teams with data from teams that struggle to find wins creates a "mismatched" prior. This leads to inaccurate shrinkage—over-shrinking the big wins and under-shrinking the null results.
*   **Corpus Size:** Simulations suggest that at least $K_c \approx 200$ historical experiments are needed for the parameter estimates $\hat{p}$ and $\hat{V}$ to stabilize.

## Comparing Bayesian and Frequentist Approaches

One of the most important takeaways from this research is that Bayesian and frequentist methods are not as distinct as often portrayed. In many cases, they are numerically equivalent or share the same operational characteristics.

For example, a Bayesian test using a flat prior in a fixed-sample design produces a posterior mean identical to the frequentist maximum likelihood estimate $\hat{\delta}$. Similarly, the Bayes factor stopping rule used in Tier 2 is mathematically identical to the frequentist mSPRT. The choice of a prior in the Bayesian framework is functionally equivalent to choosing a mixing distribution in the frequentist sequential framework.

When compared to Group Sequential Tests (GST), which are common in frequentist practice, Bayesian Tier 2 and Tier 3 methods offer the advantage of not requiring a pre-specified maximum sample size $n_{max}$. While GST can be slightly more powerful because it optimizes for a specific end-point, Bayesian methods provide greater flexibility for "anytime" decisions while maintaining comparable efficiency in terms of sample size and error control.

## Decision Theory and Business Value

Finally, the work explores the decision-theoretic side of Bayesian inference. Unlike frequentist methods that focus purely on error rates, Bayesian methods can incorporate costs. One can define a stopping rule based on "expected opportunity loss"—stopping when the risk of shipping a bad variant (or failing to ship a good one) falls below a threshold $\epsilon$.

However, the research shows that these decision-theoretic rules (Tier 1) often ignore the cost of "false positives" in a way that leads to high shipping rates for null features. If there is any non-zero cost ($s > 0$) to shipping and maintaining a new feature, methods that require stronger statistical evidence (Tier 2/3) actually result in lower total "regret" for the business. This demonstrates that the statistical rigor of Bayes factors and empirical priors is not just a theoretical preference but a way to protect the long-term value of a product.

By adopting this hierarchical view, experimentation teams can move beyond the "Bayesian vs. Frequentist" debate and focus on the practical task of selecting the tier of evidence that best matches their product's risk profile and historical performance.
