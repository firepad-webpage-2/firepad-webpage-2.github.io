## The Challenge of Prospective Causal Inference

Policy evaluation often relies on retrospective analysis: what was the effect of a tax change implemented last year, or how did a new healthcare mandate impact a specific region after it was enacted? In the study of panel data—where multiple units are observed over time—the most common methodologies for these questions are Synthetic Controls (SC) and Synthetic Interventions (SI). These methods essentially perform a "back-calculation," reconstructing what would have happened to a treated unit had it remained in a control state, or vice versa, during an observed period.

However, decision-makers are frequently confronted with a different, prospective problem: if we implement a policy *tomorrow* for a unit that has never experienced it before, what will happen? This is the core of causal forecasting. Traditional time-series forecasting is insufficient here because it typically assumes the continuation of an existing regime. If a city has always kept its stadiums closed, a standard forecast will predict case rates under the assumption that stadiums stay closed. It cannot inherently predict the "regime shift" that occurs if the stadium suddenly opens. Conversely, causal inference methods like SC and SI cannot forecast beyond the observed data horizon because they rely on the outcomes of "donor" units during the target period. If the target period is in the future, even the donor outcomes are unknown.

This research introduces the Two-Way Synthetic Forecasting (TWSF) approach to bridge this gap. By integrating the cross-sectional logic of synthetic controls with the temporal modeling of time-series analysis, TWSF enables researchers to predict potential outcomes for a unit under a treatment it hasn't yet experienced, at a time point beyond the current dataset.

## Structural Assumptions for Identification

To make a forecast under a counterfactual regime change possible, we must assume the data follows an underlying structure. The TWSF framework utilizes a potential outcomes model where each unit $i$ at time $t$ has a potential outcome $Y_{it}(d)$ for a treatment status $d \in \{0, 1\}$. The research assumes these outcomes can be decomposed into a signal and noise:

$$
Y_{it}(d) = L_{it}(d) + \epsilon_{it}(d)
$$

The signal $L_{it}(d)$ is modeled using a latent factor structure:

$$
L_{it}(d) = u_i^\top v_t(d)
$$

In this model, $u_i$ represents time-invariant unit factors (the "DNA" of the unit), and $v_t(d)$ represents latent time factors that vary depending on whether the unit is treated or not. This structure is key because it assumes that the relationship between units (governed by $u_i$) remains consistent even if the policy changes.

To enable forecasting into the future, the research adds a temporal assumption: the latent time factors $v_t(1)$ for the treated state must have a low-rank Hankel representation. In simpler terms, this means the future trajectory of the treatment effect follows a predictable pattern (like a trend or a cycle) that can be learned from the history of other units that have already undergone the treatment. 

The identification strategy effectively uses "spatial" (cross-unit) information to understand how the target unit relates to donor units in the control state, and "temporal" information from the donors to understand how the treatment state evolves over time. By combining these, the future potential outcome $Y_{N,T+1}(1)$ for the target unit $i=N$ can be identified.

## The Two-Way Synthetic Forecasting (TWSF) Framework

The TWSF estimator functions through a four-step process that systematically de-noises the data and learns the necessary weights for the forecast.

1.  **De-noising**: Real-world data is noisy. The algorithm first uses Hard Singular Value Thresholding (HSVT) to find a low-rank approximation of the observed outcome matrices. This step is essentially a form of Principal Component Regression (PCR) that discards the idiosyncratic noise $\epsilon_{it}(d)$ to focus on the underlying signal.
2.  **Learning Spatial Weights ($\beta$)**: The algorithm looks at the pre-treatment period $T_0$ where everyone was in the control state. it finds a set of weights $\beta$ that best reconstructs the target unit's trajectory using a combination of the donor units. This answers the question: "Which combination of cities most closely mimics my target city's behavior under normal conditions?"
3.  **Learning Temporal Weights ($\alpha$)**: Next, the algorithm examines the donor units that actually received the treatment. It learns a forecasting rule $\alpha$ that predicts the next time step's outcome based on the recent history of treated outcomes. This is similar to multivariate Singular Spectrum Analysis (mSSA), which extracts temporal patterns from a pool of time series.
4.  **The Combined Forecast**: The final step is the "Two-Way" combination. The algorithm applies the temporal forecasting rule to the recent history of the donors and then aggregates those donor-specific forecasts using the spatial weights learned in step 2.

The resulting forecast $\hat{\theta}$ can be expressed mathematically as:

$$
\hat{\theta} = \langle \hat{\alpha}, W^\top \hat{\beta} \rangle
$$

where $W$ represents the recent treated history of the donor pool. This elegant bilinear form shows that causal forecasting is a simultaneous prediction across both dimensions of the panel data.

## Statistical Inference and Orthogonalization

A significant contribution of this work is the development of a "debiased" or "orthogonalized" version of the TWSF estimator. In machine learning and statistics, when an estimator depends on other estimated parameters (like our weights $\beta$ and $\alpha$), the errors from those initial estimates can bias the final result and make it difficult to calculate accurate confidence intervals.

The researchers address this by constructing an augmented score function. They estimate "Riesz representers" $\hat{q}_\beta$ and $\hat{q}_\alpha$, which are auxiliary parameters that help correct for the first-order sensitivity of the forecast to errors in the weights. This approach allows the researchers to establish asymptotic normality, meaning that for a large enough sample, the estimation error follows a bell curve (Gaussian distribution).

This is not just a theoretical win; it is what allows the framework to provide valid pointwise confidence intervals. When a policymaker sees a forecast, they don't just see a single line; they see a range of probable outcomes. This quantification of uncertainty is essential for assessing the risk of a new policy.

## Evaluating Policy: NFL Stadiums and COVID-19

The paper applies TWSF to a high-stakes public health question: what would have happened if various NFL cities had opened their stadiums to fans during the 2020 COVID-19 season? 

During that season, some cities opened stadiums (the donors), while others remained closed (the targets). Retrospective studies have looked at the data after the season ended, but TWSF allows us to step back into the shoes of a decision-maker in Fall 2020. 

### Validation
To test the method's accuracy, the researchers first performed "back-tests" or validations. They chose cities that eventually opened their stadiums later in the season and used TWSF to forecast their case counts. The results showed that the TWSF forecasts (the red dashed lines in validation plots) closely tracked the actually observed case counts (the black lines), demonstrating that the model could successfully learn the dynamics of the virus's spread in a "treated" (open stadium) environment.

### Counterfactual Forecasts
For cities like Chicago, Los Angeles, and New York, which kept their stadiums closed, the researchers generated prospective forecasts of what *would* have happened if they had opened. In most cases, the forecasts suggested that opening stadiums would not have led to a massive surge beyond the observed closed-stadium rates. The observed case counts often fell within the 90% confidence intervals of the "open" forecast.

This suggests that the rigorous health protocols implemented by the NFL—such as mandatory masking, social distancing, and pod seating—likely mitigated the risks of fan attendance. However, the forecasts also showed sensitivity to the timing of the decision. Forecasting an opening during a local surge (like in Green Bay or Buffalo) showed higher predicted rates, reflecting the dynamic nature of the epidemic.

## Performance and Theoretical Guarantees

The research provides strong theoretical backing for the TWSF estimator. It proves "pointwise consistency," meaning as you collect more data (more units and more time points), the forecast converges to the true potential outcome. Notably, the error bounds for this PCR-based approach achieve a convergence rate of $O(d^{-1})$, where $d$ is a measure of the panel's dimensions. This is an improvement over previous results in the literature, which typically showed slower rates.

Simulation studies further support these findings. Across different forecast horizons ($h \geq 1$), the "recursive" estimator (which iterates a one-step forecast forward) showed particular stability in smaller datasets. As the dimensions of the panel increased, the empirical coverage of the confidence intervals reached the target 90% mark, confirming that the inference procedure is well-calibrated.

## Conclusion

The Two-Way Synthetic Forecasting approach represents a shift in how we can use panel data for policy design. By moving from "what happened" to "what will happen," it provides a toolkit for proactive decision-making. Whether predicting the spread of a virus under different social distancing rules or forecasting the economic impact of a proposed local tax, the ability to generate counterfactual forecasts with quantifiable uncertainty is a significant advancement for applied researchers. 

While the current framework assumes a low-rank structure and independent noise, it sets the stage for future research into more complex temporal dynamics and staggered policy adoptions. For now, TWSF offers a robust, theoretically grounded bridge between the previously distinct worlds of causal inference and time-series forecasting.
