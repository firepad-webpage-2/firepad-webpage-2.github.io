## Introduction to Narrow-Line Seyfert 1 Galaxies

Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) are the energetic centers of galaxies, powered by the accretion of matter onto supermassive black holes. Within this population, Narrow-line Seyfert 1 (NLS1) galaxies occupy a unique niche. They are primarily distinguished from their Broad-line Seyfert 1 (BLS1) counterparts by the width of their optical emission lines. Specifically, NLS1s are defined by a Full Width at Half Maximum of the H$\beta$ line such that $FWHM_{\text{H}\beta} < 2000 \text{ km s}^{-1}$ and a flux ratio of $[\text{O III}] \lambda 5007 / \text{H}\beta < 3$. These characteristics are widely interpreted as evidence of relatively lower black hole masses accreting at high, sometimes super-Eddington, rates.

Historically, the radio properties of NLS1s have been a subject of significant debate. While the majority are classified as radio-quiet, a small but important fraction shows evidence of powerful relativistic jets, sometimes emitting high-energy gamma rays. Understanding whether NLS1s are intrinsically different from BLS1s in their radio emission mechanisms is essential for refining AGN unification models. This research provides a large-scale, uniform comparison between these two classes, leveraging recent large-area sky surveys to minimize selection biases and classification errors that hindered previous studies.

## Sample Selection and Multi-wavelength Framework

A critical challenge in comparative AGN studies is ensuring that the classification of "narrow-line" vs "broad-line" is consistent across the entire sample. This work utilizes a parent catalog derived from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey Data Release 17 (SDSS DR17), encompassing over 70,000 unique objects. These objects were processed using the Bayesian AGN Decomposition Analysis for SDSS Spectra (BADASS) code, which allows for a more reliable disentanglement of the various spectral components.

The final samples consist of 22,590 NL1 galaxies and 51,997 BL1 galaxies, all at redshifts $z < 0.8$. To facilitate a more granular analysis, the authors further divided these groups based on their optical absolute magnitude. Objects with $M_B < -23$ are categorized as type 1 quasars (NLQ1 and BLQ1), while those with $M_B \geq -23$ are considered Seyfert 1 galaxies (NLS1 and BLS1). 

The radio data for this comparison come from two primary sources: the Faint Images of the Radio Sky at Twenty-Centimeters (FIRST) survey at $1.4 \text{ GHz}$ and the Very Large Array Sky Survey (VLASS) at $3 \text{ GHz}$. By cross-matching the optical positions with these radio catalogs, the researchers could calculate individual radio properties rather than relying on population-averaged assumptions.

## Analyzing the Radio Spectral Index

One of the primary contributions of this study is the calculation of individual two-point radio spectral indices for a large number of sources. The spectral index $\alpha$ is defined by the power-law relation $S \propto \nu^{\alpha}$, where $S$ is the flux density at frequency $\nu$. By comparing the $1.4 \text{ GHz}$ data from FIRST with the $3 \text{ GHz}$ data from two separate epochs of VLASS, the authors were able to characterize the shape of the radio emission for each galaxy.

The findings reveal a subtle but statistically significant difference: NL1 galaxies tend to have steeper radio spectra than BL1 galaxies. The median spectral index for the NL1 sample was found to be approximately $-0.49$ to $-0.45$, whereas the BL1 sample showed a median of $-0.32$ to $-0.26$. Even when narrowing the comparison to just the NLS1 and BLS1 Seyfert subgroups, the NLS1s remained systematically steeper.

To ensure these results were not artifacts of measurement error, the authors performed a Monte Carlo perturbation test. They varied the flux density of each source within its reported error bounds and recalculated the median $\alpha$ thousands of times. The difference between the populations persisted, confirming its robustness. Furthermore, a redshift-matching technique was applied to account for the fact that NL1 and BL1 galaxies are distributed differently across cosmic time. Even when comparing subsets with identical redshift distributions, the NL1 sample retained its steeper spectral characteristics. This suggests that the radio-emitting structures in NLS1s may be more extended or dominated by aging electron populations compared to the more compact, core-dominated structures often found in BLS1s.

## The Complexity of Radio Loudness

Radio loudness ($RL$) is a standard metric used to compare the radio emission of an AGN relative to its optical brightness. In this study, the authors recalculated $RL$ by estimating the $5 \text{ GHz}$ radio flux density using each source's unique spectral index and comparing it to the extinction-corrected optical flux density at $4400 \text{ \AA}$.

The analysis showed that NL1 galaxies are generally less radio-loud than BL1 galaxies. The median $RL$ for NL1s was roughly half that of the BL1 sample. However, an unexpected trend emerged when comparing quasars and Seyferts. In both the narrow-line and broad-line categories, the optically fainter Seyfert galaxies exhibited *higher* median $RL$ values than the optically brighter quasars.

This counterintuitive result—that smaller, fainter galaxies appear more radio-loud—likely stems from the difficulty of isolating the AGN's optical light from its host galaxy. In fainter Seyfert galaxies, the host galaxy contributes a larger proportion of the measured optical flux, which can artificially inflate the denominator of the $RL$ ratio. The authors suggest that this highlights the limitations of using a simple $RL$ parameter for comparing different types of AGN, especially when they occupy galaxies of varying sizes and brightnesses.

## Radio Power and Source Morphology

To complement the $RL$ analysis, the authors examined the intrinsic $1.4 \text{ GHz}$ radio power, denoted as $P_{1.4 \text{ GHz}}$. This parameter represents the total radio energy emitted by the source, corrected for its distance and redshift.

The distributions of radio power showed that BL1 galaxies are consistently more powerful radio emitters than NL1 galaxies across almost all optical luminosity bins. The median $\log(P_{1.4 \text{ GHz}})$ in units of $10^{20} \text{ W Hz}^{-1}$ was $4.3$ for the total NL1 sample and $4.8$ for the BL1 sample. While the radio power generally increases with optical brightness, the NL1 galaxies remained radio-deficient relative to their BL1 peers at similar optical magnitudes.

The study also investigated radio compactness, defined as the ratio of peak intensity to total flux density. A ratio near 1 indicates a point-like, compact source, while lower values suggest extended emission like radio lobes or jets. Both samples were found to be predominantly compact at $1.4 \text{ GHz}$ (with medians $> 0.95$), but the VLASS data at $3 \text{ GHz}$ revealed more resolved structure in a significant fraction of sources, indicating that higher-frequency, higher-resolution observations are necessary to fully map the radio morphology of these objects.

## Distinguishing AGN Activity from Star Formation

A long-standing question in the study of radio-quiet AGN is how much of their radio emission comes from the central black hole and how much comes from star formation in the host galaxy. To address this, the authors compared star formation rates estimated from radio data ($SFR_{\text{FIRST}}$) with those derived from infrared data ($SFR_{\text{W3}}$) using the WISE $12 \mu\text{m}$ band.

If radio emission were driven solely by star formation, these two rates should be roughly equal. However, for the majority of the sources (79% of NL1s and 91% of BL1s), the radio-derived SFR was significantly higher than the infrared-derived SFR.

$$\n\log(SFR_{\text{FIRST}}) > \log(SFR_{\text{W3}})\n$$

This inequality suggests a substantial contribution from an AGN-driven jet or wind. The median $SFR_{\text{FIRST}}$ was on the order of $1000 M_{\odot} \text{ yr}^{-1}$, a value that is physically implausible for star formation in typical galaxies, further confirming that the radio emission is dominated by the AGN.

## Summary of Population Differences

The results of this comprehensive analysis point toward fundamental differences in the radio properties of narrow-line and broad-line Seyfert 1 galaxies. The key findings include:

1.  **Lower Detection Rates:** NL1 galaxies are less likely to be detected in radio surveys at a given sensitivity limit compared to BL1 galaxies.
2.  **Steeper Spectra:** The radio emission in NL1s is characterized by steeper spectral indices, potentially indicating different jet physics or evolutionary states.
3.  **Radio Faintness:** NL1s exhibit lower median radio power and radio loudness than BL1s, regardless of their optical classification as Seyferts or quasars.
4.  **AGN Dominance:** Despite the lower power, the radio emission in detected NLS1s is still primarily driven by the central engine rather than star-forming processes.

By using large, uniformly processed catalogs, this research provides a statistical foundation for future studies. The subtle differences in spectral shape and detection rates suggest that while NLS1s and BLS1s may be part of the same physical family, the conditions at the heart of NLS1s—likely high accretion onto smaller black holes—lead to a distinct and generally weaker radio-emitting environment. Future work with even higher resolution radio surveys, such as future epochs of VLASS and the Square Kilometre Array, will be instrumental in determining whether these differences are related to the age of the jets or the density of the surrounding galactic medium.
