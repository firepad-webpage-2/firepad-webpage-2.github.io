## The Challenge of Global Solutions in Economics

In quantitative macroeconomics and finance, researchers often seek to solve dynamic stochastic models that describe how economies evolve over time. These models frequently involve high-dimensional state spaces, occasionally binding constraints (like the zero lower bound on interest rates), and significant nonlinearities—especially during rare events such as financial crises or natural disasters. Traditionally, solving these models globally (across the entire possible state space) has been computationally intensive.

In recent years, unsupervised deep-learning solvers, such as Deep Equilibrium Nets (DEQN), have emerged as a powerful alternative. These methods use neural networks to represent equilibrium policies and train them by minimizing structural equilibrium conditions (like Euler equations). However, these solvers suffer from a "self-confirming" bias. They typically enforce equilibrium conditions only on the states generated by their own simulated policy. While the resulting solution might be accurate along a typical simulated path, it remains untested and uncertified in "off-path" regions—the very regions where rare shocks or policy counterfactuals occur.

![figure-1-visualizing-the-coverage-measure-construction-the-training-distribution-expands-from-the-policy-s-own-path-to-include-stressed-and-local-perturbations](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.23463v1/bm_coverage_2d.png)

"Equilibrium World Models" (EWMs) address this limitation. Instead of relying solely on the policy's own experience, an EWM "rehearses" a broader range of imagined experiences. This approach ensures that the model's structural conditions are enforced across a wider distribution of states, effectively moving the computational solution from a self-confirming state toward a true rational-expectations equilibrium.

## Broadening Equilibrium Enforcement

The core innovation of EWMs is the transition from pathwise training to a broader "coverage measure," denoted by $\mu_\kappa$. In traditional deep-learning solvers, the training distribution is restricted to the ergodic distribution of the current policy, $\mu_{\pi_\theta}$. If the policy hasn't "seen" a disaster, it doesn't learn how to react to one.

EWMs redefine the population objective by constructing a mixture distribution:

$$\rho_1 \mu_{\pi_\theta} + \rho_2 \mu_{\text{stress}}^\kappa + \rho_3 \mu_{\text{local}}^\kappa$$

1.  **Ergodic Component**: The standard path generated by the current policy.
2.  **Stress Component**: States generated by taking "rare" or "stressed" seeds (like a sudden collapse in capital or a massive productivity shock) and rolling them forward through the exact model transition $\Gamma$.
3.  **Local Component**: Small, admissible perturbations applied to states along the simulated path to ensure local robustness.

By adjusting the parameter $\kappa$, researchers can control the "reach" of the solver. At $\kappa = 0$, the method resembles a traditional pathwise solver. As $\kappa$ increases, the solver certifies the equilibrium conditions over a larger portion of the reachable state space. This process ensures that the policy is accurate not just on average, but also in the critical tails of the distribution.

## World Components: Amortizing Computational Complexity

Enforcing equilibrium conditions over a broad range of states is computationally expensive. Evaluating expectations in economic models (the "continuation" part of a value or Euler equation) often requires high-dimensional quadrature—summing up possible future outcomes weighted by their probabilities.

EWMs introduce "World Components"—audited neural surrogates—to make this tractable. These are not replacements for the economic model; they are computational instruments designed to speed up the solver.

### Continuation Surrogates

The costliest part of the policy update is often the conditional expectation:

$$Q^{\pi_\theta} (x) = \mathbb{E} [ g(x, a, \epsilon, x') | x ]$$

An EWM trains a continuation surrogate, $bW_\psi(x) \approx Q^{\pi_\theta}(x)$, via regression against a sparse set of exact quadrature evaluations. During the main policy optimization loop, the solver queries the surrogate instead of performing full quadrature, significantly reducing the computational budget.

### Action-Conditioned Continuations

In some models, an agent's current action $a$ directly influences the probability of future states (e.g., investing in disaster prevention). In these cases, the continuation must be explicitly conditioned on the action: $bW(x, a) \approx Q^{\pi_\theta}(x, a)$. This allows the surrogate to capture the marginal benefit of an action, which a state-only surrogate would "average away."

### Distributional Encoders

In heterogeneous-agent models, the state of the economy includes the entire distribution of wealth or productivity across the population. This is an inherently high-dimensional object. EWMs use a distributional encoder $h_\psi$ to compress this distribution $\mu$ into a low-dimensional embedding $z = h_\psi(\mu)$. This embedding is trained as a Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA), where the encoder learns to capture features of the distribution that are most useful for predicting future states via the exact distributional transition $\Phi$.

## The Training and Audit Discipline

EWMs follow a rigorous alternating optimization process. The policy $\pi_\theta$ and the world component $bW_\psi$ (or $h_\psi$) are updated in turns.

- **Policy Step**: Minimize the surrogate residual $R_L = R(x, \pi_\theta(x), bW_\psi(x))$ over the coverage measure $\mu_\kappa$.
- **World Step**: Update the surrogate or encoder to better approximate the true model dynamics or expectations.

Crucially, EWMs maintain an "audit discipline." The success of the solver is never measured by the surrogate's internal loss. Instead, it is measured by a held-out exact residual $R_H(x) = R(x, \pi_\theta(x), Q^{\pi_\theta}(x))$, calculated using exact quadrature on states the model did not see during training. This ensures that the neural surrogates remain faithful to the underlying structural economic model.

## Theoretical Foundations

The paper provides a theoretical bridge between computational heuristics and rational-expectations equilibria. A key result is an error decomposition showing that the total equilibrium error on a test set is bounded by the optimization error, the approximation error of the neural network, the surrogate's fit error, and a "coverage error."

By explicitly expanding the coverage measure $\mu_\kappa$, EWMs directly target the coverage error. The authors prove that as the coverage expands to fill the reachable state space and approximation errors vanish, the EWM policies converge toward a rational-expectations equilibrium. This formalizes the idea that EWMs aren't just faster solvers; they are solving a more complete version of the economic problem.

## Scaling to High-Dimensional Macroeconomics

The practical utility of EWMs is demonstrated in an International Real Business Cycle (IRBC) model with up to $N=32$ countries, resulting in a 65-dimensional state space. 

Standard pathwise solvers often struggle with initialization in these complex models. In the IRBC experiments, a pathwise solver failed to converge to a verified solution from any of its ten random starts. EWMs, by contrast, converged in nearly every instance. This robustness stems from the fact that the coverage measure "forces" the solver to see and resolve equilibrium conditions in off-path regions early in training, preventing it from getting stuck in self-confirming but incorrect regions of the parameter space.

![figure-3-convergence-robustness-in-the-irbc-model-ewms-blue-consistently-find-verified-solutions-where-standard-pathwise-methods-red-fail](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.23463v1/irbc_seed_basin.png)

Furthermore, the continuation surrogate allowed the solver to scale efficiently. In the 32-country model, the surrogate reduced the number of expensive quadrature evaluations by a factor of over 100, making a previously intractable problem solvable on standard hardware.

## Distributional Embeddings in Heterogeneous-Agent Models

In models with heterogeneous agents (like the Bewley economy), the aggregate state is a distribution of wealth. Traditionally, economists have used hand-picked moments (like mean wealth) to approximate this state. EWMs replace this manual step with a learned embedding.

The JEPA-trained encoder $h_\psi$ learns to extract the "decision-relevant" features of the wealth distribution. When tested against ground-truth simulations, these learned embeddings significantly outperformed traditional moments and raw histograms in terms of accuracy ($L_1$ distance). The embeddings captured critical features like wealth variance and the Gini coefficient, which are essential for clearing markets and determining household behavior, without requiring the researcher to specify them in advance.

## Significance and Impact

Equilibrium World Models represent a shift in how deep learning is applied to economics. Rather than treating neural networks as black-box function approximators that simply replicate a policy's own experience, EWMs use them as audited components within a strictly defined structural framework.

By broadening the coverage of equilibrium enforcement, EWMs provide solutions that are certified in the "stressed" regions of the economy that are most important for policy analysis. The framework's ability to scale to high dimensions while maintaining robustness to initialization makes it a versatile tool for modern quantitative macroeconomics. Ultimately, EWMs provide a pathway to more reliable, transparent, and computationally efficient analysis of the complex dynamic systems that define global economies.
