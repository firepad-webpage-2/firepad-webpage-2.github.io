## Understanding Labor Mismatch: Education vs. Skills

Labor mismatch is a central concept in labor economics that describes the vertical discrepancy between a worker’s competencies and the requirements of their job. This mismatch typically manifests in two ways: a worker may have more qualifications or skills than the job requires (over-matched) or fewer than required (under-matched). Historically, researchers have relied on educational attainment as a proxy for skills, primarily because education data is readily available in census and employment surveys. However, the theoretical and empirical assumption that education and skills are interchangeable has come under increasing scrutiny.

The research by Vsevolod Iakovlev addresses a fundamental question in the field: Does educational mismatch accurately reflect skill mismatch, or do these two concepts lead to different empirical conclusions regarding worker earnings? By utilizing the OECD Survey of Adult Skills (PIAAC), which provides direct cognitive assessments alongside traditional education data, this study provides a comprehensive comparison of how these different types of mismatch associate with wages across 26 countries. The work clarifies whether the choice of measurement framework significantly alters the estimated wage penalties for over-qualified workers and wage premiums for under-qualified ones.

## Data and Measurement Frameworks

The core of this analysis relies on the First Cycle of the OECD Survey of Adult Skills (PIAAC), conducted between 2011 and 2012. Unlike previous datasets that relied solely on self-reporting, PIAAC measures literacy, numeracy, and problem-solving skills on a 500-point scale. This allows for a robust comparison between a worker's formal credentials and their actual cognitive performance.

The researcher categorizes mismatch measures into two broad families:

### Education-Based Measures
These focus on formal schooling and include:
*   **Job Analysis (JA):** This approach compares the worker's highest qualification level to the skill level requirements assigned to their occupation by the International Labour Organization (ILO).
*   **Realised Matches (RM):** A distribution-based measure where the "required" education for a job is defined by the mean or modal education level of all workers in that specific occupation.
*   **Indirect Self-Assessment (ISA):** Workers are asked to report the number of years of formal education they believe are actually required to perform their current job, which is then compared to their attained education.

### Skill-Based Measures
These focus on the application of cognitive abilities:
*   **Direct Self-Assessment (DSA):** Workers report whether they have the capacity to handle more demanding tasks or if they need more training. 
*   **Pellizzari-Fichen (PF):** A more recent approach that defines skill requirements as the range (minimum and maximum) of skill scores found among "well-matched" workers (identified via self-assessment) in the same occupation.
*   **Allen-Levels-van-der-Velden (ALV):** This measure compares a worker's skill score with their actual use of that skill in the workplace. A worker is considered mismatched if the difference between their skill score and skill engagement score exceeds a certain threshold (e.g., $1.5$ standard deviations).

## The Measurement Selection Process

A significant contribution of this paper is its systematic approach to choosing which measures are most relevant for economic analysis. Given the multitude of ways to define "mismatch" (e.g., using mean vs. mode for RM, or different percentile cutoffs for PF), the researcher employs Lasso (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regression. 

Lasso is a penalized regression technique that helps identify which specific mismatch indicators contribute the most to predicting worker earnings while minimizing errors. This selection process revealed that different specifications—such as a 1-year vs. a 5-year gap in education requirements—can lead to vastly different conclusions. Ultimately, the researcher selected a robust subset of measures, including Job Analysis, Realised Matches (mode-based), and several skill-based indicators for literacy and numeracy, while excluding Direct Self-Assessment due to its low correlation with other metrics and inherent reporting errors.

## Econometric Strategy: Controlling for Market-Level Factors

To estimate the relationship between mismatch and earnings, the study utilizes an adapted Mincer earnings function. The Mincer function typically models earnings as a function of education and experience. In this study, it is formulated as an error components model to account for unobserved factors that might vary across different labor markets (defined as country-specific industries).

The primary equation is:

$$\ln w_{ij} = \alpha + \beta_o \text{over}_{ij} + \beta_u \text{under}_{ij} + \gamma X_{ij} + \mu_j + \eta_{ij}$$

In this model, $\ln w_{ij}$ represents the natural logarithm of hourly earnings for a worker $i$ in market $j$. The terms $\text{over}_{ij}$ and $\text{under}_{ij}$ are binary indicators for whether the worker is over-matched or under-matched, respectively. The vector $X_{ij}$ includes various individual control variables such as age, gender, migration status, and cognitive skill scores. 

A critical component of this strategy is $\mu_j$, which captures market-level unobserved heterogeneity. For example, some industries might have higher productivity or specific local labor conditions that drive both the likelihood of mismatch and higher average wages. By using a Fixed Effects (FE) approach (specifically the Mundlak formulation), the researcher can separate the individual-level effects of mismatch from these broader market trends, providing more accurate estimates of the wage penalties and premiums.

## Core Empirical Findings: Wage Penalties and Premiums

The study confirms that both educational and skill mismatch are significantly associated with earnings, but they are not interchangeable. 

### Individual-Level Effects
When controlling for individual characteristics and market-level factors, over-matching is consistently linked to **wage penalties**. Workers who are over-educated earn between $12.6\%$ and $16.4\%$ less than their well-matched peers in the same occupation. Skill-based over-matching (being "over-skilled") also results in penalties, though the magnitudes vary; the ALV literacy measure, for instance, showed a penalty of approximately $16.1\%$, while the PF measures averaged around $6.2\%$.

Conversely, under-matching is associated with **wage premiums**. Under-educated workers tend to earn about $7\%$ to $10\%$ more than expected for their qualification level, likely because they possess other unobserved skills or experience that allow them to perform in higher-level roles. Skill-based under-matching (being "under-skilled") showed even higher premiums, with the PF measure averaging $12.5\%$.

### The Divergence between Education and Skills
The most striking result is the divergence between education and skill indicators. At a country-wide level, the share of "well-matched" workers by education tends to be higher in countries with lower median earnings. In contrast, for skill-based measures, the share of "well-skilled" workers is positively associated with higher national earnings. This empirical finding suggests that as economies become more productive, the gap between formal education and practical skill requirements may actually widen, making the distinction between the two types of mismatch even more critical for researchers.

## Heterogeneity Across Demographics

The research further explores how these mismatch patterns differ across various demographic groups, revealing that the labor market does not experience mismatch uniformly:

*   **Gender:** The analysis found that women are generally more likely to be over-educated but less likely to be under-educated or over-skilled compared to men.
*   **Migration Status:** Migrant workers face a unique set of challenges. They are significantly more likely to be over-educated and under-skilled, which may reflect difficulties in getting foreign credentials recognized or the language barriers affecting their cognitive assessment scores in the host country.
*   **Age:** Older workers are more likely to be under-educated and under-skilled. This might be due to "credential inflation," where younger workers enter the market with more formal degrees for the same roles that older workers secured decades ago with less formal schooling.

## Implications for Theory and Policy

The findings of this paper have significant implications for how we understand human capital. The clear empirical distinction between education and skill mismatch challenges traditional theories that treat schooling as a perfect proxy for productivity. It supports theories like the "signaling model," where education serves as a signal to employers rather than a direct measure of job-relevant skills.

For policymakers, the research suggests that "one-size-fits-all" solutions to labor mismatch are likely to fail. Addressing over-education might require improvements in career counseling and better information about job requirements to help students choose the right qualifications. On the other hand, addressing skill mismatch might require firm-level interventions, such as job rotation, lifelong learning programs, and support for career transitions, to ensure that the actual abilities of the workforce are being fully utilized.

Furthermore, the study's use of the Mundlak estimator proves that market-level factors—such as industry-specific productivity—can bias simpler models. Future research must continue to account for these unobserved heterogeneities to provide reliable guidance for economic policy. By demonstrating the robustness of these wage effects through Oster's $\delta$ (a measure of how strong unobserved factors would need to be to negate the results), the study provides a high degree of confidence in the reported wage penalties and premiums.
