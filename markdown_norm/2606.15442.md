## Representing Positive Definite Matrices via Reverse Telescoping

Symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrices are fundamental structures in modern data science. They appear as covariance matrices in finance, functional connectivity networks in neuroscience, and diffusion tensors in medical imaging. Formally, a $p \times p$ matrix $\mathbf{\Theta}$ is SPD if it is symmetric and all its eigenvalues are strictly positive. These matrices do not exist in a flat Euclidean space but rather on a curved Riemannian manifold, denoted as $\mathcal{M}_p^+$. 

Modeling and generating SPD matrices is challenging because standard machine learning tools are typically designed for flat spaces. To apply these tools, researchers often map SPD matrices to a flat coordinate system (a "chart"). Common choices include the Cholesky decomposition $\mathbf{\Theta} = \mathbf{T}\mathbf{T}^\top$ or the matrix logarithm. However, these methods often introduce geometric distortions. For example, in the Cholesky chart, the relationship between the volume of the matrix and its coordinates is complex and anisotropic, meaning different coordinates are weighted differently. This makes tasks like calculating probabilities or performing smooth transport across the manifold computationally expensive and mathematically convoluted, especially as the dimension $p$ increases.

This research introduces the **Reverse Telescoping (RT)** coordinate system. The RT system provides a diffeomorphism—a smooth, reversible mapping—between $\mathcal{M}_p^+$ and $\mathbb{R}^{p(p+1)/2}$. Its primary advantage is that it simplifies the intrinsic geometry of the SPD manifold by isolating the matrix's volume (log-determinant) into a single scalar coordinate. This separation allows for more scalable generative modeling and more efficient computation of Riemannian properties.

## The Reverse Telescoping Coordinate System

The RT system is built on the principle of recursive rank-one Schur complements. In essence, any SPD matrix can be decomposed step-by-step into a sequence of smaller matrices by subtracting specific rank-one components.

### Encoding and Decoding
The encoding process takes an SPD matrix $\mathbf{\Theta}$ and transforms it into a specific internal representation $\mathbf{\tilde{\Theta}}$ through $p-1$ recursive steps. Each step involves extracting a column and updating the remaining sub-matrix. The resulting coordinates $\mathbf{x} = (v, \mathbf{d}, \mathbf{r})$ consist of:
1.  **$v$**: The log-determinant of the matrix, representing its global "volume."
2.  **$\mathbf{d}$**: A vector of $p-1$ relative diagonal scales, representing the relative "size" of different dimensions.
3.  **$\mathbf{r}$**: A vector of $p(p-1)/2$ partial covariances, representing the "shape" and orientation.

A key computational benefit is that while the initial encoding and decoding of $\mathbf{\Theta}$ requires $\mathcal{O}(p^3)$ operations, many downstream operations—such as calculating quadratic forms or evaluating likelihoods—can be done in $\mathcal{O}(p^2)$ time once the matrix is in RT coordinates. Furthermore, the RT system provides a unified way to compute both the matrix and its inverse $\mathbf{\Sigma}$ efficiently.

### Isolation of Volume Distortion
In many coordinate systems, the Jacobian—the factor that accounts for how volumes change during the transformation—is a complicated function of all coordinates. In RT coordinates, the Jacobian from $\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{\Theta}$ is remarkably simple:
$$
J(\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{\Theta}) = \exp(v) = \det(\mathbf{\Theta})
$$
This means that all distortion of the ambient volume is concentrated in the scalar $v$. For the inverse matrix, the Jacobian is similarly streamlined:
$$
J(\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{\Sigma}) = \exp(-pv) = \det(\mathbf{\Theta})^{-p}
$$
This isolation is a significant departure from existing charts like the Cholesky or spectral charts, where the Jacobian is globally coupled and anisotropic.

## Geometry and the Fisher-Rao Metric

The Fisher-Rao (FR) metric is the standard affine-invariant metric used to measure distances on the SPD manifold. It is preferred because it treats the space consistently regardless of scaling or rotation. However, the FR metric is usually difficult to work with due to its complex curvature.

### Stage-Local Metric Structure
The RT coordinates reveal a "stage-local" structure in the Fisher-Rao metric. The metric decomposes into an additive sum across the recursive steps of the telescoping process:
$$
ds^2_{FR} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^p (ds_j)^2 + \sum_{j=2}^p \exp(-s_j) (d\mathbf{r}_j - \mathbf{r}_j ds_j)^\top \mathbf{\Sigma}_{j-1} (d\mathbf{r}_j - \mathbf{r}_j ds_j)
$$
In this expression, $s_j$ are log-diagonal terms. Crucially, each term in the sum depends only on the current step $j$ and a "frozen" summary of previous steps ($\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j-1}$). This local coupling is much easier to manage than the global coupling found in Cholesky coordinates. It allows for the efficient computation of natural gradients, which are essential for optimization on manifolds.

### Fisher-Rao Volume and Harmonic Coordinates
The research further shows that the intrinsic volume form of the Fisher-Rao manifold, $dVol_{FR}$, also depends only on the global volume coordinate $v$:
$$
dVol_{FR} = 2^{-p/2} \exp\left(-\frac{p-1}{2}v\right) dv dd dr
$$
By reparameterizing the coordinates to $(v, \mathbf{d}, \mathbf{\beta})$, where $\mathbf{\beta}$ represents normalized regression coefficients, the authors show that $v$ and $\mathbf{\beta}$ are **harmonic coordinates**—meaning their Laplace-Beltrami operator (the manifold equivalent of the Laplacian) is zero ($\Delta_{FR}v = 0$ and $\Delta_{FR}\mathbf{\beta}_j = 0$). This property is rare and highly desirable, as it means these coordinates are perfectly adapted to the geometry of the space.

## Intrinsic Diffusion and Sampling

The simplified Laplace-Beltrami operator allows for the design of intrinsic Langevin diffusion processes. Langevin diffusion is a way to sample from a probability distribution by simulating a particle moving under the influence of a potential and random noise. 

On a curved manifold, this is usually difficult because the noise must be carefully adjusted to account for the curvature. In the $(v, \mathbf{d}, \mathbf{\beta})$ RT coordinate system, however, the intrinsic Brownian motion simplifies into:
*   Standard Brownian motion for the volume $v$.
*   Brownian motion with a constant drift for the diagonal scales $\mathbf{d}$.
*   Drift-free, though anisotropic, Brownian motion for the regression terms $\mathbf{\beta}$.

This allows researchers to implement MCMC-like sampling algorithms on the SPD manifold with almost the same ease as in Euclidean space, facilitating Bayesian inference for high-dimensional covariance matrices.

## Scalable Generative Modeling: Split Volume-Shape Flow

A major contribution of this work is the development of a generative model that scales to high dimensions ($p \geq 100$). The authors propose a **Split Volume-Shape Flow** based on Conditional Flow Matching (CFM).

CFM aims to learn a vector field that transports a simple base distribution (like a Gaussian) to a complex target distribution. Because RT coordinates separate volume ($v$) from shape ($\mathbf{\xi}$), the model trains two separate flows:
1.  **Volume Flow:** Transports the log-determinant $v$.
2.  **Shape Flow:** Transports the shape components $\mathbf{\xi}$ conditioned on the volume.

### Isentropic Hamiltonian Flow
To ensure the shape flow doesn't accidentally change the volume of the matrix, the authors use a **Hamiltonian parameterization**. They define the velocity field $u(\mathbf{\xi}, t; v)$ such that its divergence is zero:
$$
\nabla_{\mathbf{\xi}} \cdot u(\mathbf{\xi}, t; v) = 0
$$
This ensures the flow is "isentropic"—it preserves the volume element. By design, the neural network only learns how to reconfigure the shape of the matrix without needing to waste capacity on maintaining the volume constraint. This approach proved to be the only method capable of handling dimensions as high as $p=200$ in the experiments.

## Practical Applications and Performance

The RT coordinate system was tested on both synthetic benchmarks and real-world brain connectivity data.

### Synthetic Wishart Mixtures
The authors used mixtures of Wishart distributions to create challenging bimodal targets. In these tests, existing state-of-the-art methods like Riemannian SPD-CFM and DiffeoCFM performed well at low dimensions ($p=20$) but struggled or became computationally infeasible as the dimension reached $p=100$ and $p=200$. The RT Hamiltonian flow consistently captured the bimodal structure across all dimensions, demonstrating superior scalability.

### Brain Connectivity (fMRI)
The method was applied to the ABIDE dataset, which contains $39 \times 39$ functional connectivity matrices derived from fMRI scans of subjects with and without Autism Spectrum Disorder. The RT-based models successfully generated synthetic connectivity matrices that matched the statistical properties (mean and variance of correlations) of the held-out real data.

### Fisher-Rao Distance Bounds
Finally, the researchers provided an efficient way to bound the Fisher-Rao distance. Calculating the exact FR distance between two matrices is an $\mathcal{O}(p^3)$ operation involving matrix eigenvalues. The RT system allows for the computation of ellipsoidal bounds in $\mathcal{O}(p^2)$ time. These bounds act as a tight surrogate for the true distance, which is particularly useful for optimization tasks where distances must be calculated repeatedly.

## Conclusion

The Reverse Telescoping coordinate system addresses the geometric and computational bottlenecks inherent in modeling symmetric positive definite matrices. By isolating volume distortion and providing a stage-local metric structure, it simplifies complex Riemannian operations. Its impact is most visible in generative modeling, where it enables the synthesis of high-dimensional SPD matrices that were previously beyond the reach of manifold-aware methods. Whether for sampling via intrinsic diffusion or generating synthetic connectivity networks, the RT framework offers a scalable and mathematically principled approach to working with SPD data.
