## The Impact of Platform Mergers on Consumer Behavior

Horizontal acquisitions in multi-sided markets, such as the digital platform economy, raise complex questions for both firms and regulators. When a dominant platform acquires a smaller competitor, it faces a critical strategic choice: should it fully integrate the acquired service, effectively dissolving the brand, or allow both platforms to coexist? This study investigates this dilemma through the lens of Uber’s acquisition of Postmates in the food delivery sector, which was finalized in late 2020. 

A primary challenge in studying such mergers is that standard market-level data often lacks the granularity required to track individual consumer shifts. By utilizing a proprietary dataset of consumer-level transaction data derived from email receipts, this research provides a high-resolution view of how demand is reallocated among competitors post-acquisition. The data tracks over 10,000 active users across major platforms including DoorDash, Grubhub, Postmates, and UberEats, spanning from June 2019 to April 2022.

The central objective is to determine if the disruption caused by platform integration leads users to migrate to the acquiring platform (UberEats) or if it inadvertently pushes them toward rival platforms. This distinction is vital for understanding whether platform mergers truly consolidate market power or if they create competitive openings for third parties.

## Empirical Methodology: Age–Period–Cohort Decomposition

To isolate the effect of the merger from other confounding factors, the study primarily employs an Age–Period–Cohort (APC) decomposition. This method is particularly suited for panel data where individual behavior might change based on how long they have been using a service (age/tenure), the specific point in time (period), and the characteristics of the group they joined with (cohort).

In the context of the Uber-Postmates merger, the outcome of interest is the budget share allocated to each platform. The study models the average outcome $\bar{Y}_{cat}$ for a cohort $c$ at age $a$ in month $t$ as:

$$\bar{Y}_{cat} = \alpha_a + \phi_c + \gamma_t + \bar{X}'_{cat}\beta + \epsilon_{cat}$$

Where:
- $\alpha_a$ represents age fixed effects, capturing how user behavior evolves as they spend more time on the platform.
- $\phi_c$ represents cohort fixed effects, accounting for differences between users who joined at different times (e.g., pre-pandemic vs. during the pandemic).
- $\gamma_t$ represents period fixed effects, which capture the impact of calendar time, including the merger event.
- $\bar{X}_{cat}$ includes time-varying controls such as COVID-19 restrictions and restaurant supply changes.

The identification of the merger effect involves a multi-step process. First, the period fixed effects $\hat{\gamma}_t$ are detrended to remove underlying seasonality and long-term trends. Then, an ARIMA model is used to forecast a counterfactual trajectory $\hat{r}^{cf}_t$ based on pre-merger data. The "Lift" or causal effect of the merger is defined as the difference between the actual observed period effect $\hat{r}_t$ and this counterfactual. This approach allows the researcher to estimate the total impact of the merger on the user base, rather than just the differential impact across different geographic markets.

## Post-Merger Demand Reallocation

The analysis reveals a significant shift in consumer spending following the acquisition. Postmates users, who had an established history with the platform, drastically reduced their engagement after the merger was finalized. Specifically, the APC results show an average decline of $-40.33$ percentage points (pp) in the budget share allocated to Postmates.

The critical question for Uber was how much of this "lost" spending would be captured by UberEats. The results indicate that UberEats gained $+19.79$ pp in budget share from these users. While this is a substantial gain, it accounts for less than half of the spending that left Postmates. The remainder of the reallocated demand shifted toward rival platforms:
- DoorDash saw an increase of $+7.50$ pp.
- Grubhub gained $+10.49$ pp.

Together, these competitors captured approximately $+17.99$ pp of the budget previously dedicated to Postmates. This suggests that the merger-induced disruption created a "leakage" effect, where a significant portion of the acquired customer base moved to third-party competitors rather than the acquiring platform. Furthermore, the study finds an overall reduction in total delivery spending and order frequency among these users, indicating that some consumers may have reduced their overall reliance on food delivery services entirely following the disruption.

## Consumer Heterogeneity and "Stickiness"

The impact of the merger was not uniform across all users. The study identifies two key factors that determine how likely a consumer is to switch platforms: their initial dependence on the acquired platform and their "multi-homing" behavior.

Consumers with high pre-merger dependence on Postmates (defined as spending more than 70% of their food delivery budget on the platform) experienced the most dramatic changes. For this group, Postmates budget share plummeted by $-56.18$ pp. In contrast, low-dependence users (those spending less than 25% on Postmates) saw a much smaller decline of $-14.82$ pp. This suggests that casual users are more "sticky" or less sensitive to the platform's corporate changes, whereas power users are more likely to re-evaluate their choices when the platform's identity or service changes.

The concept of multi-homing—the practice of using multiple platforms simultaneously—also plays a significant role. Users who were already using other platforms besides Postmates (multi-homers) showed more resilience. Their Postmates budget share declined by $-36.15$ pp, compared to a $-59.58$ pp drop for single-homing users. Multi-homers, having already established accounts and familiarity with competitors, could reallocate their spending with lower switching costs.

The most extreme response was observed among high-dependence, single-homing users in markets where UberEats already had a high market share. This group saw a massive $-89.58$ pp reduction in Postmates spending, indicating a near-total abandonment of the platform.

## Methodological Comparisons: APC vs. Difference-in-Differences

A secondary contribution of the paper is the comparison between the APC decomposition and the more traditional Difference-in-Differences (DiD) approach. The DiD model estimated in the study is:

$$y_{imt} = \alpha + \beta \cdot (\text{Treated}_m \times \text{Post}_t) + X'_{imt}\gamma + \mu_m + \lambda_t + \epsilon_{imt}$$

Where:
- $y_{imt}$ is the percentage of spending on Postmates for user $i$ in market $m$ at time $t$.
- $Treated_m$ is an indicator for MSAs with a high pre-merger UberEats market share.
- $Post_t$ is the post-merger time indicator.
- $\mu_m$ and $\lambda_t$ are market and time fixed effects.

While the DiD model is robust and confirms that the decline was steeper in markets with high UberEats concentration, the study argues that DiD tends to underestimate the total merger effect. This is because DiD only captures the *differential* effect—how much more the treated group changed compared to the control group. 

If the merger caused a widespread change in the platform's app interface, branding, or restaurant availability that affected all users regardless of their local market concentration, DiD would treat this as a "common effect" and subtract it out. The APC approach, by building a counterfactual based on historical individual-level trends, recovers the total lift, capturing both the common and the differential impacts. This methodological comparison highlights that for corporate events with nationwide implications, relying solely on cross-sectional variation might lead researchers to miss the broader scope of the event's impact.

## Strategic and Regulatory Implications

The findings of this research offer several insights for platform strategy and antitrust policy. For platform operators, the results highlight the inherent risks of aggressive brand consolidation. While integrating an acquired platform can lead to operational efficiencies and supply-side synergies, it can also alienate the acquired user base. The fact that nearly half of the reallocated Postmates budget went to competitors suggest that "forced" migration is not always successful. In multi-sided markets where switching costs are low and multi-homing is common, consumers are quick to follow their preferences to rival platforms if they perceive a decline in value or a disruption in service.

From a regulatory perspective, the study suggests that horizontal acquisitions in these markets may not always lead to the degree of market power consolidation that aggregate market share figures might imply. If consumers are highly mobile and competitors can easily capture displaced demand, the anti-competitive effects of such mergers may be partially mitigated by the competitive response of rival platforms.

In conclusion, the decision to "combine or not" involves a delicate balance between internal efficiency and external customer retention. This study demonstrates that in the highly competitive food delivery market, the dissolution of a horizontal brand can lead to a significant redistribution of market share that benefits rivals as much as it benefits the acquirer. Researchers and practitioners must therefore look beyond simple market share calculations to understand the granular, individual-level behaviors that drive the long-term outcomes of platform mergers.
