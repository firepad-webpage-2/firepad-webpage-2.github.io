## Wildfire Susceptibility Mapping in the Age of Earth Foundation Models

Wildfire susceptibility mapping is an essential tool for identifying landscapes prone to future fires. Traditionally, these maps are built using extensive physical datasets—satellite imagery, climate records, and topographic data—that require meticulous harmonization and domain-specific feature engineering. This complexity creates a significant barrier for local governments and emergency responders who may lack the technical resources to process disparate data sources across varying spatial and temporal scales.

The research presented here evaluates a paradigm shift: using general-purpose Earth observation embeddings from AlphaEarth Foundations (AEF) instead of traditional physical variables. AEF is an Earth foundation model trained on vast archives of satellite data to learn compact, 64-dimensional numerical representations—known as embeddings—of the Earth's surface. These embeddings are "analysis-ready," meaning they summarize complex environmental characteristics in a standardized format. The study focuses on Victoria, Australia, a region with a diverse landscape and a history of extreme wildfires, to determine if these general-purpose embeddings can effectively replace hand-crafted physical predictors.

## The Core Principles of AlphaEarth Foundations (AEF) Embeddings

At the heart of this work is the concept of a geospatial embedding. Unlike a traditional satellite band (e.g., Red or Near-Infrared), which measures a specific wavelength of light, an embedding is a vector of numbers representing learned features. AlphaEarth Foundations provides these as 64-dimensional vectors at a $10\text{m}$ resolution globally.

To make these embeddings useful for wildfire susceptibility at a regional scale, the researchers aggregate the high-resolution $10\text{m}$ pixels into a $1\text{km}$ grid. This process involves summing the vectors within the grid cell and then applying $L2$ normalization to ensure the resulting vector maintains a consistent magnitude. This aggregation preserves the rich information captured at the local level while aligning with the coarser resolution often used for climate and wildfire modeling. The resulting $1\text{km}$ vector $\vec{E}_{1\text{km}}$ is defined as:

$$\vec{E}_{1\text{km}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \vec{e}_{i, 10\text{m}}}{\left\| \sum_{i=1}^{n} \vec{e}_{i, 10\text{m}} \right\|_2}$$

where $n$ is the number of $10\text{m}$ pixels within the $1\text{km}$ cell. This provides a multi-modal summary of the landscape that can be fed directly into machine learning models without requiring the user to manually select or process individual physical variables like slope, vegetation type, or moisture levels.

## Probing the Information Content: What Do Embeddings "Know"?

A critical question is whether these abstract numbers actually encode the physical factors that drive wildfires. To test this, the researchers conducted a reconstruction analysis. They attempted to predict 22 traditional physical variables (retrieved from sources like SRTM for topography and MODIS for vegetation) using only the AEF embeddings as inputs to a simple neural network.

The results show that AEF embeddings are highly effective at capturing key environmental drivers. Topographic features such as slope, as well as climatic factors like mean temperature and total precipitation, were reconstructed with a coefficient of determination $\text{R}^2$ exceeding 0.9. This indicates that even though the AEF model was not specifically trained to identify "slope" or "rainfall," these physical characteristics are naturally encoded within its 64-dimensional feature space. However, the models struggled to reconstruct human-centric or highly localized proximity variables, such as distance to roads, suggesting that while embeddings capture the natural landscape well, they may need to be supplemented with specific human-infrastructure data in certain contexts.

## Downstream Modeling Strategies

The study compared two main feature sets: the 22 physical predictors (grouped as $X_{\text{cs}}$ for cross-sectional and $X_{\text{yr}}$ for yearly) and the 64 AEF embedding dimensions ($E_{\text{cs}}$ and $E_{\text{yr}}$). Several machine learning architectures were evaluated to see which could best extract predictive signals from these embeddings:

1.  **Cell-Level Tabular Models:** These include tree-based ensembles like Random Forest, XGBoost, and LightGBM, as well as a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP). These models treat each $1\text{km}$ cell as an independent data point.
2.  **Spatial Neighborhood Models:** Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) were used to process local spatial context. The researchers tested three patch sizes: $9 \times 9$, $17 \times 17$, and $25 \times 25$ grid cells.
3.  **Sequence-Informed Models:** These models utilized yearly data to see if temporal changes in the landscape (e.g., vegetation growth or drought cycles) improved susceptibility predictions.

The CNN architecture is particularly interesting for embeddings. Because the AEF embeddings form a spatially structured field, a CNN can "look" at the arrangement of features in a neighborhood, identifying patterns like the edge of a forest or a steep drainage basin that might be particularly fire-prone.

## Evaluating Performance in Victoria

In the primary study area of Victoria, both physical variables and AEF embeddings achieved strong performance. Most models reached a $\text{ROC-AUC}$ (a measure of how well the model distinguishes between fire and non-fire locations) greater than 0.92. 

The researchers found that while tree-based models slightly preferred traditional physical variables, the neural network architectures (MLP and CNN) performed almost identically regardless of the feature set used. Notably, CNNs using $17 \times 17$ or $25 \times 25$ patches of AEF embeddings provided the most spatially coherent susceptibility maps. These maps successfully identified high-risk zones in the forested and mountainous regions of eastern Victoria. A qualitative validation against actual burned perimeters from the January 2026 bushfires showed that the models successfully flagged the areas where fires eventually occurred.

## Spatial Transferability and Generalization

One of the most significant challenges in wildfire modeling is the "transferability gap." A model trained in one climate zone (e.g., temperate Victoria) often fails when applied to another (e.g., tropical Queensland) because the relationships between physical variables and fire occurrence change.

The researchers tested the models on eight additional sites across Australia, ranging from nearby temperate forests to arid inland deserts. They quantified the performance drop-off using the relative change $\Delta$. The results revealed a distinct advantage for AEF embeddings:
*   **Physical-variable models** suffered a sharp decline in performance even in climatically similar regions like Canberra or the Blue Mountains, with $\text{ROC-AUC}$ dropping by roughly 25%.
*   **AEF embedding models** were significantly more robust. In some cases, like the Canberra site, the embedding-based model actually improved in performance during transfer, and generally maintained high recall.

While both types of models eventually degraded in performance when moved to highly dissimilar environments (like the arid interior of Alice Springs), the embedding-based approach showed superior generalization in temperate and subtropical climates. This suggest that AEF embeddings capture more universal landscape features that translate better across geographic boundaries than raw physical measurements.

## Why This Matters for the Research Landscape

This paper demonstrates that foundation model embeddings provide a viable, "analysis-ready" alternative to traditional wildfire modeling workflows. By using pre-computed embeddings, researchers and practitioners can bypass the time-consuming stages of data collection, harmonization, and feature engineering.

The significance of this work lies in three areas:
1.  **Efficiency:** Embedding-based workflows significantly lower the technical barrier for high-quality susceptibility mapping, enabling faster deployment in resource-constrained settings.
2.  **Scalability:** The superior transferability of AEF-based models suggests that we can build regional models that generalize better across entire countries or continents, rather than requiring a bespoke model for every small jurisdiction.
3.  **Benchmarking:** AEF embeddings can serve as a powerful baseline. If a researcher develops a custom physical-variable model that cannot outperform a simple AEF-based CNN, it indicates that their custom features are not adding unique value beyond what is already captured in general-purpose foundation models.

In conclusion, AlphaEarth Foundations embeddings provide a robust representation of the environmental factors driving wildfire risk. While they are not a "magic bullet" that eliminates the need for all localized data, they offer a scalable and highly effective foundation for the next generation of wildfire risk assessment tools.
