## Understanding Fairness in Actuarial Modeling

In industries like insurance and healthcare, Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) serve as the backbone for critical decision-making processes. They are used to predict risks such as the likelihood of a car accident, the frequency of hospital visits, or the total cost of claims. However, these models often face scrutiny regarding fairness. A model might inadvertently produce disparate outcomes for different demographic groups, such as those defined by ethnicity or insurance status.

Traditional approaches to ensuring fairness often rely on "fairness through unawareness"—simply removing sensitive variables like race or gender from the dataset. Unfortunately, this is rarely sufficient. Other variables, such as zip code or education level, often act as proxies, carrying sensitive information into the model. Furthermore, the mathematical structure of GLMs themselves can amplify existing disparities. Research by Tierny, Charpentier, and Hu provides a diagnostic framework to decompose these disparities into specific statistical channels, allowing modelers to understand exactly where discrimination originates within a fitted model.

## The Linear Model Benchmark

To understand the complexity of GLMs, one must first look at the simpler case of linear models. In a standard linear regression, the prediction is a direct, weighted sum of the input features. If we have a sensitive attribute $s$ (like ethnicity) and other non-sensitive features $x$, the linear predictor is expressed as:

$$
f_{lin}(x, s) = \langle x, \beta \rangle + \gamma_s + \beta_0
$$

Here, $\beta$ represents the weights for non-sensitive features, $\gamma_s$ is the specific weight assigned to the sensitive group, and $\beta_0$ is the baseline intercept. To measure the disparity between groups, the researchers use a criterion based on the first two moments (mean and variance) of the predictions. Under the assumption that the scores for each group follow a Gaussian distribution, $f_{lin}(X, S) | S=s \sim N(m_s, b_s^2)$, the total disparity $U^2(f_{lin})$ can be perfectly split into four parts:

1.  **Direct Mean Channel:** The disparity caused directly by the sensitive coefficient $\gamma_s$. It is measured as $Var_S(\gamma_S)$.
2.  **Indirect Mean Channel:** Disparities arising because different groups have different average profiles for the non-sensitive variables $x$. This is measured as $Var_S(\langle \mu^{(S)}, \beta \rangle)$.
3.  **Interaction Channel:** This captures whether the direct and indirect effects reinforce or cancel each other out, calculated as $2 Cov_S(\gamma_S, \langle \mu^{(S)}, \beta \rangle)$.
4.  **Indirect Structural Channel:** Disparities caused by differences in how "spread out" the scores are within each group, denoted as $Var_S(B)$.

By identifying these four channels, a researcher can tell if a model is biased because it explicitly uses a sensitive attribute, or because it relies on other variables that differ between groups.

## The Challenge of Nonlinearity in GLMs

While linear models are intuitive, actuarial data is rarely linear or Gaussian. For example, the number of insurance claims is a count (0, 1, 2...), which is often modeled using a Poisson distribution. The risk of a disease is a probability (between 0 and 1), usually modeled with a Logistic regression. These models fall under the category of Generalized Linear Models (GLMs).

In a GLM, the latent score $\eta = \langle x, \beta \rangle + \gamma_s + \beta_0$ is transformed by a nonlinear "inverse link function" $h$ to produce the final prediction:

$$
f(x, s) = h(\eta)
$$

This nonlinearity introduces a major hurdle for fairness diagnostics. A small, additive difference in the latent score $\eta$ might be exploded into a massive difference in the final prediction $f$ if $h$ is an exponential function (as in Poisson regression). Conversely, the difference might be squeezed if $h$ is a logistic function.

The researchers address this by proposing a two-layer approximation. The first layer uses a "delta approximation" to estimate how the mean and variance of the latent score translate through the function $h$ for each group. The second layer expands these approximations to provide a leading decomposition that accounts for the curvature of the link function.

## The Six Channels of Disparity in GLMs

The core contribution of this work is the extension of the four linear channels into a six-channel decomposition for GLMs. By expanding the proxy criterion $D_1(f)$, the researchers identify how the nonlinear link function $h$ reshapes disparity:

*   **Scaled Linear Channels:** The original four channels (Direct, Indirect, Interaction, and Structural) are still present but are now scaled by the squared derivative of the link function at the global mean, $\{h'(\bar{m})\}^2$. This means the link function acts as a volume knob, turning the latent disparities up or down.
*   **Curvature Coupling:** A new term $2 h'(\bar{m}) h''(\bar{m}) \bar{b} Cov_S(M, B)$ that links the group means to the dispersion of the scores. If a group has both a higher average score and a wider spread of scores, the curvature of the link function can exacerbate the total disparity.
*   **Curvature Amplification:** Perhaps the most significant addition, $\{h''(\bar{m})\}^2 \bar{b}^2 Var_S(M)$ represents the extra disparity created purely by the "bend" in the link function. Even if the internal spread of scores is identical for all groups, different average latent scores will result in different output dispersions because the function is steeper in some regions than others.

This framework shows that even if a modeler removes the sensitive attribute and ensures covariate profiles are balanced, the choice of a nonlinear model (like a Poisson model for counts) can itself create new statistical disparities.

## Diagnostic Insights and Mitigation

The researchers tested their framework using the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS) data, looking at disparities between Hispanic and non-Hispanic groups, as well as insured and uninsured individuals. 

In their analysis of Poisson and Tweedie models (which use an exponential link function), they found that the **Direct Mean** channel was usually the dominant source of disparity. Interestingly, the **Curvature Amplification** term was always positive, confirming that the nonlinear nature of these models inherently increases the observed disparity compared to a purely linear version of the same model.

To address these disparities, the paper proposes a post-processing technique called **Latent Moment Alignment**. The goal is to adjust the latent scores $\eta$ so that every group has the same mean and variance before the link function $h$ is applied. The "fair" latent score is calculated as:

$$
\eta^{fair} = \bar{m} + \frac{\bar{b}}{b_s} \{\eta - m_s\}
$$

The final fair prediction is then $f^{fair}(x, s) = h(\eta^{fair}(x, s))$. In empirical tests, this method was highly effective, often reducing the disparity $U^2(f)$ by nearly $100\%$. Crucially, the researchers also monitored the "cost" of this fairness—how much the model's accuracy (measured by $R^2$ or Root Mean Square Error) decreased. In many cases, the reduction in disparity was achieved with only a minor loss in predictive performance, providing a clear map for the trade-offs involved in ethical model design.

## Impact on Actuarial Practice

This research provides a much-needed diagnostic toolkit for the insurance and healthcare sectors. It moves the conversation beyond binary labels of "biased" or "unbiased" and provides a granular map of *why* a model behaves the way it does.

For a compliance officer or an actuary, this means:
1.  **Identifying Proxies:** If the "Indirect Mean" channel is large, it indicates that the model is picking up sensitive information through other variables, prompting a review of the feature set.
2.  **Evaluating Model Choice:** If the "Curvature" terms are high, the modeler might reconsider whether the chosen link function is unnecessarily amplifying group differences.
3.  **Governance:** The decomposition allows for a more transparent discussion with regulators, showing whether a disparity is a result of explicit coefficients or structural differences in the population's data.

By providing a mathematical bridge between the latent scores of Generalized Linear Models and the real-world scale of their predictions, this work ensures that fairness diagnostics keep pace with the complex statistical tools used in modern risk assessment.
