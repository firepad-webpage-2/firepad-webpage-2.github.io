## A Sequential Theory of Automation

Traditional economic models of automation, such as the widely used task-based framework, treat production as a collection of independent tasks. In these models, a firm decides whether to automate a specific task—such as data entry or assembly—based on whether a machine has a comparative advantage over a human for that isolated unit of work. However, this approach overlooks a fundamental reality of modern production: work is rarely a set of disconnected actions. Instead, it is a sequence of interdependent steps where the output of one step serves as the input for the next.

The research by Demirer et al. introduces a theory of "AI chaining" to address this gap. The researchers argue that the primary impact of AI is not merely the automation of individual tasks but the reorganization of entire workflows. Because AI performance can be "jagged"—highly capable in some steps while failing in adjacent ones—the cost of "handing off" work between humans and AI becomes a central economic constraint. By modeling production as a sequence of $m$ steps ($s_1, \dots, s_m$), the theory explains how firms endogenously define what constitutes a "task" and how those tasks are grouped into "jobs."

## The Mechanics of AI Chaining

At the heart of the framework is the concept of an AI chain. In traditional automation, a human might be "augmented" by a tool, meaning they use the tool to complete a step and then immediately verify the result. In an AI chain, the technology executes a contiguous block of steps autonomously. The human worker only intervenes at the very end of the chain to verify the final output.

The probability that AI successfully completes an individual step $i$ is denoted by $q_i$. This success rate is a function of the AI's general quality $\alpha$ and the specific difficulty of the step $d_i$, formulated as:

$$q_i = \alpha^{d_i}$$

When steps are chained together, the human verification cost becomes a fixed cost for the entire sequence, rather than a marginal cost for each individual step. However, the expected time required for this verification increases if the chain is long or the steps are difficult, because the human must account for the cumulative probability of error across the sequence. For a chain of steps starting at $\ell$ and ending at $r$, the expected time cost $T_{\text{chain}}$ is:

$$T_{\text{chain}} = \frac{t_{Ar}}{\prod_{i=\ell}^r q_i}$$

where $t_{Ar}$ represents the time needed for a human to review the final step. This mathematical structure leads to a critical theoretical result: the failure of traditional comparative advantage. A firm might choose to automate a step using AI even if a human is technically more efficient at that specific step in isolation. They do this to maintain the "chain," avoiding the high cost of a human-to-AI hand-off that would be required if they inserted a manual step in the middle of a sequence.

## Endogenous Job Design and Hand-off Costs

The theory extends beyond AI deployment to the very definition of a "job." Firms face a fundamental trade-off between specialization and coordination. A highly specialized division of labor allows workers to master a narrow set of skills, lowering their individual wage requirements. However, this creates many boundaries between workers, each of which incurs a "hand-off cost"—a penalty in time and accuracy when information moves from one person to another.

The firm’s objective is to minimize the total wage bill, which the researchers model as the sum of the skill requirements and time costs across all jobs in the production process:

$$\text{Total Cost} = \sum_{j \in \text{Jobs}} S_j (T_j + T_{Hj})$$

In this equation, $S_j$ represents the skill breadth required for job $j$, $T_j$ is the time spent on actual production tasks, and $T_{Hj}$ is the time lost to hand-offs. 

AI shifts this balance. By automating contiguous sequences of work, AI can reduce the number of human-to-human hand-offs, potentially favoring more "generalist" human roles that oversee broad AI-driven workflows. Conversely, if AI is only capable of sporadic steps, it may force a finer, more fragmented division of labor. The researchers show that the optimal configuration—deciding which steps to chain and how to group the remaining manual work into jobs—can be solved efficiently. For a workflow of $m$ steps, the optimal AI strategy can be found in $O(m^2)$ time, while the joint optimization of AI and human job design can be approximated in $O(m^2 \epsilon^{-2} \log^2(mB))$ time, where $\epsilon$ is the approximation error and $B$ is a cost bound.

## Reorganization Thresholds and the Productivity J-Curve

A significant contribution of this work is providing a micro-foundation for the "productivity J-curve." Economists have long observed that new technologies often result in stagnant productivity initially, followed by a sharp acceleration. The theory of AI chaining explains this through "reorganization thresholds."

As shown in {fig:total-execution-cost-and-marginal-benefit}, marginal improvements in AI quality $\alpha$ might not yield significant cost savings if the firm maintains its existing job structure. However, once $\alpha$ reaches a specific threshold, it becomes optimal for the firm to completely redesign its workflow—for example, by merging two manual jobs into one AI-managed role or by extending an AI chain across a previously manual gap. These discrete shifts create large, sudden jumps in productivity and labor demand, explaining why AI's impact may appear minimal until a "tipping point" is reached.

## Empirical Validation: Fragmentation and Adjacency

To test the theory, the researchers combined O*NET data (which describes occupational tasks) with real-world AI usage data from Anthropic’s Claude and GPT-generated task sequences. They tested three primary predictions:

1.  **AI Chaining:** Do AI-executed steps actually appear in contiguous blocks? The data shows that AI-executed tasks are clustered in sequences far more often than would occur by chance. The observed average chain length was significantly higher than in randomized placebo datasets.
2.  **Fragmentation:** Does the "dispersion" of AI-able tasks matter? The researchers developed an Empirical Fragmentation Index (EFI) to measure how scattered AI-friendly tasks are within an occupation's workflow. They found that occupations with high fragmentation—where AI-able steps are interrupted by manual ones—have significantly lower rates of actual AI adoption, even when the total number of AI-able tasks is the same.
3.  **Adjacency Effects:** Does the status of a neighbor influence a task? Using Detailed Work Activities (DWAs) to compare similar tasks across different jobs, the study found that a task is much more likely to be executed by AI if its immediate predecessor or successor in the workflow is also handled by AI.

These findings suggest that "exposure" to AI (whether a task *can* be done by AI) is an incomplete metric. The "executability" of AI depends heavily on the surrounding workflow.

## Macroeconomic Implications

Finally, the paper bridges the gap between these firm-level organizational changes and the broader economy. While each individual firm might have a rigid, Leontief-style production function (where inputs must be used in fixed proportions), the researchers show that when you aggregate many diverse firms, the economy-wide production function takes a Constant Elasticity of Substitution (CES) form. 

This aggregation depends on the distribution of AI quality across the economy, represented by $\phi(\alpha)$. This result is significant because it allows macroeconomists to use standard tools to study AI's impact on aggregate wages and capital, while still respecting the complex, sequential micro-realities of how work is actually reorganized at the firm level. By focusing on the "chaining" of work, the research provides a new lens through which to view the ongoing transformation of the labor market in the age of artificial intelligence.
