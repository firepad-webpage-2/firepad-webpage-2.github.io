## Introduction

Understanding who turns out to vote is a cornerstone of political analysis. Whether for electoral administrators aiming to allocate resources or researchers studying democratic representation, the ability to estimate turnout across fine-grained demographic groups—such as age, gender, and education levels—is vital. However, traditional methods for calculating these figures often face a trade-off between the depth of information provided by individual surveys and the reliability of official aggregate counts.

The research by Niitsoo, Rebane, and Jüristo addresses this by introducing a unified Bayesian hierarchical framework. Their approach systematically integrates three distinct data sources: individual-level survey responses, official aggregate turnout margins (like total votes per region), and census population counts. By combining these, the models aim to correct for common survey biases, such as over-reporting and non-ignorable non-response, while avoiding the ecological fallacy that plagues inferences drawn purely from group-level data.

## The Challenge: Why Turnout is Hard to Estimate

Estimating turnout for specific subgroups (e.g., "university-educated women under 30 in a specific county") is difficult because the two primary data sources both have significant flaws.

**Survey Data** provides rich individual details. By asking people if they voted and recording their demographics, researchers can build complex models of behavior. However, surveys are increasingly plagued by two issues:
1.  **Over-reporting:** Due to social desirability bias, many people claim they voted even when they did not.
2.  **Non-ignorable Non-response:** The people who choose to answer surveys often differ systematically from those who do not. If high-propensity voters are more likely to take a survey, the results will be skewed.

**Aggregate Data**, such as official election results, is the "gold standard" for total turnout. We know exactly how many people voted in each region. However, these totals do not tell us *who* voted. Attempting to guess individual behavior from these totals is known as **Ecological Inference (EI)**. The risk here is the **Ecological Fallacy**: just because a high-income neighborhood has high turnout doesn't mean it was necessarily the high-income residents who voted.

Current solutions often involve **Multilevel Regression and Poststratification (MRP)**, which uses a model to predict voting probabilities for every demographic "cell" and then weights them by their actual population size. While powerful, standard MRP often ignores the aggregate totals, or adjusts for them using *ad hoc* post-hoc shifts that can break the statistical consistency of the model.

## A Unified Bayesian Hierarchy

The paper proposes a sequence of three models that build upon one another to integrate these data sources more effectively.

### 1. Basic Probit (BP)
The foundation is a standard multilevel regression model. For an individual $i$ in a demographic cell $c$, the latent voting propensity $\eta_{o_i}$ is modeled as:

$$
\eta_{o_i} = \beta_{o_0} + \sum_d \beta_{o_{d,j[i]}}
$$

Here, $\beta_{o_0}$ is the baseline propensity, and $\beta_{o_{d,j[i]}}$ represents the effect of category $j$ within demographic dimension $d$ (e.g., age or education). The actual binary voting outcome $O_i \in \{0, 1\}$ is then modeled using the probit link function $\Phi$:

$$
O_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\Phi(\eta_{o_i}))
$$

This structure uses hierarchical priors, which allow for "partial pooling." This means that if we have very little data for a specific group, the model "borrows" strength from the average behavior of related groups, leading to more stable estimates for sparse demographic cells.

### 2. Poll-and-Margin (PM)
The **PM model** is a major contribution of this work. It treats the survey data and the official aggregate margins as part of a single joint likelihood. Instead of calibrating the survey results after the fact, the PM model requires the shared parameters to satisfy both the survey observations and the known population totals simultaneously.

To reconcile the inflated turnout numbers often found in surveys, the PM model introduces a survey-bias term $\beta_{o_{or}}$. This term shifts the propensity for survey respondents:

$$
\eta_{o^*_i} = \eta_{o_i} + \beta_{o_{or}}
$$

This allows the individual-level survey data to dictate the "shape" of the demographic differences, while the aggregate official totals (the margins) anchor the overall turnout levels.

### 3. Full Selection (FS) Model
The most advanced version is the **FS model**, which adds a selection correction. It explicitly models the process of *choosing to participate in the survey*. This is a Heckman-style correction integrated into the Bayesian framework. It jointly models two latent variables: the outcome (voting) $O^*_i$ and the selection (survey participation) $S^*_i$:

$$
O^*_i = \eta_{o^*_i} + \epsilon_{o_i}
$$
$$
S^*_i = \eta_{s_i} + \epsilon_{s_i}
$$

The error terms $\epsilon_{o_i}, \epsilon_{s_i}$ are assumed to have a correlation $\rho$. If $\rho$ is positive, it means that the unobserved factors that make someone more likely to vote also make them more likely to answer a survey.

## Identifying the "Unidentifiable"

A significant hurdle in selection models is that they are often "weakly identified"—meaning the data doesn't clearly point to a single best answer for parameters like the error correlation $\rho$ or the survey-bias $\beta_{o_{or}}$. Typically, researchers use "exclusion restrictions" (variables that affect selection but not the outcome) to solve this, but such variables are rare in election surveys.

The authors overcome this by leveraging three pieces of information simultaneously:
1.  **Survey Outcomes:** Who voted among those who answered.
2.  **Official Margins:** Total turnout counts that are free from selection bias.
3.  **Census Data:** The total population $N_c$ for each cell, which the model treats as the "contacted" group in a random-contact survey design.

By using the census as the denominator for the survey sample, the model can estimate the selection probability $\pi_{s_c}$ for each cell $c$. Specifically, the number of respondents $n_c$ is modeled as a draw from the total sample size $N_S$ following a multinomial distribution:

$$
n_c \sim \text{Multinomial}(N_S, (\pi_{s_c})_c)
$$

The framework also uses informative priors—statistically grounded "best guesses"—for $\rho$ and $\beta_{o_{or}}$ based on historical political science data. This provides enough structure for the model to "learn" the correction even without specialized instruments.

## Key Findings and Results

The authors tested these models through extensive simulations and an application to the 2023 Estonian parliamentary election.

### Simulation Performance
In controlled simulations where the "true" turnout was known, the PM and FS models consistently outperformed the current state-of-the-art approach by Ghitza and Gelman. The distance from the truth (measured by Kullback-Leibler divergence) was up to 57% lower for the FS model.

Furthermore, the joint Bayesian models provided much better **uncertainty calibration**. While standard survey models were often "overconfident" (claiming to be sure but being wrong), the PM and FS models produced credible intervals that were 3 to 5 times narrower than traditional calibration methods while still correctly containing the true value nearly $90\%$ of the time.

### Real-World Application: Estonia 2023
When applied to the 2023 Estonian election, the survey data showed a massive over-reporting bias: $91.6\%$ of respondents claimed to have voted, while the actual adjusted turnout was $70.7\%$. The BP model failed significantly here, as it could not reconcile these two facts. The PM and FS models, however, successfully used the regional margins to bring the estimates in line with reality.

The study also highlighted a sensitivity: the FS model is highly dependent on how accurately the selection mechanism is modeled. If there is random noise in who chooses to answer a survey that the demographics can't explain, the advantages of the selection correction can diminish.

## Significance of the Work

This paper represents a shift from *ad hoc* adjustments to a principled, end-to-end statistical integration of electoral data. 

1.  **Unified Framework:** It shows that survey data, aggregate margins, and population counts can be treated as a single cohesive system rather than separate datasets to be merged later.
2.  **Principled Bias Correction:** It offers a way to correct for non-ignorable non-response without needing rare "instrumental variables," simply by using the aggregate totals we already have from elections.
3.  **Scalable Granularity:** Because the model uses a hierarchical structure, it can provide reliable estimates for small geographic areas or small demographic subgroups where standard surveys would have too few respondents to be useful.

For practitioners, this means more accurate maps of voter participation and a better understanding of which groups are being left out of the democratic process. For researchers, it provides a robust toolkit for handling the pervasive problems of survey bias and the ecological fallacy in political science.
