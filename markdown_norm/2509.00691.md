## The Challenge of Sparse Autoencoder Evaluation

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities, yet their internal decision-making processes remain largely opaque. To address this, researchers have turned to Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs), which are designed to decompose the dense, complex activations of an LLM into a large set of sparse, potentially interpretable "features." Ideally, each feature (or neuron in the SAE's latent space) should correspond to a single, human-understandable concept.

However, evaluating whether these SAEs actually produce interpretable features is a significant challenge. Current state-of-the-art benchmarks often rely on "LLM-judges"—automated systems that use another powerful model (like GPT-4) to look at activation patterns and generate or score natural language explanations. This approach introduces several issues: it is expensive, computationally heavy, non-deterministic (responses can change with every run), and potentially biased by the internal preferences of the judge model.

![ce-bench-framework](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2509.00691v2/CE_Bench.png)
*{fig:ce-bench-framework}: The CE-Bench framework extracts neuron activations from an SAE for contrastive story pairs, calculating contrastive and independence scores to determine a final interpretability metric.*

CE-Bench is introduced as a solution to these problems. It is a fully "LLM-free" evaluation benchmark that measures the interpretability of SAEs using a contrastive framework. Instead of asking a model to explain what a feature does, CE-Bench observes how features respond to semantically opposing contexts. By focusing on intrinsic activation patterns, CE-Bench provides a stable, reproducible, and efficient way to quantify general interpretability.

## Motivation: Moving Beyond LLM Judges

The move toward an LLM-free benchmark is motivated by the practical limitations of current interpretability pipelines. When researchers develop new SAE architectures or training methods, they need a fast way to check if they are making progress. Relying on an external API or a massive local model for evaluation creates a bottleneck.

Furthermore, the "explanation-then-simulation" loop used by many benchmarks—where one LLM explains a feature and another tries to predict activations based on that explanation—is prone to noise. CE-Bench aims to capture the same underlying property (disentanglement of concepts) by looking at how well neurons distinguish between distinct meanings of the same subject. This approach is deterministic; given the same input and the same SAE, the score will always be the same, facilitating scientific rigor and benchmarking.

## The Contrastive Methodology

The core of CE-Bench lies in its dataset of 5,000 contrastive story pairs. The researchers began by filtering over 117 million entities from WikiData to find 1,000 well-known subjects. For each subject, they generated five pairs of stories that are semantically distinct. For example, if the subject is "Apple," one story might focus on the fruit's nutritional value, while the contrastive story might focus on the technology company's latest product launch.

When evaluating an SAE, both stories in a pair are passed through the LLM and the SAE. The benchmark then calculates three primary components:

### Contrastive Score
The contrastive score measures how distinctly an SAE's neurons respond to the different aspects of a subject. If an SAE has successfully learned meaningful features, certain neurons should fire for the "fruit" context and others for the "technology" context. The neuron-wise contrastive vector $C$ is calculated as the absolute difference between the mean activation vectors of the two stories:

$$
C = |V_1 - V_2|
$$

where $V_1$ and $V_2$ represent the average neuron activations for the first and second stories, respectively.

### Independence Score
An interpretable neuron should not only be contrastive but also specific. It should respond uniquely to a particular subject rather than firing generally across all inputs. The independence score evaluates this disentanglement. The researchers first find the combined activation for a subject, $I_1 = V_1 + V_2$, and compare it to a global baseline $I_{avg}$, which represents the average activation across all subjects in the dataset:

$$
D = |I_1 - I_{avg}|
$$

This highlights neurons that are specialized for the specific subject at hand.

### Sparsity-Aware Interpretability Score
In SAE research, there is often a trade-off between how well an autoencoder reconstructs the original signal and how sparse its activations are. To align CE-Bench results with existing human-verified benchmarks, the authors found that incorporating a penalty for active features (sparsity) improved the reliability of the score. The final sparsity-aware interpretability score $S$ is defined as:

$$
S = \text{Contrastive Score} + \text{Independence Score} - (\alpha \times \text{Sparsity})
$$

Using a hyperparameter $\alpha = 0.25$, the benchmark achieves a high correlation with traditional LLM-judged metrics while remaining entirely autonomous.

## Analyzing SAE Interpretability Trends

By applying CE-Bench to a wide variety of pretrained SAEs, the authors uncovered several consistent trends regarding what makes an SAE more or less interpretable.

### Impact of Architecture
The researchers tested several popular SAE variants, including Standard, Top-k, JumpReLU, and Gated SAEs. Their analysis found that **Top-k** and **p-anneal** architectures generally yielded the highest interpretability scores. These architectures explicitly encourage sparsity during training, which seems to help the model disentangle complex features more effectively.

![architecture-analysis](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2509.00691v2/sae_analysis.png)
*{fig:architecture-analysis}: Analysis across different SAE architectures shows that Top-k and p-anneal variants consistently perform well on CE-Bench metrics.*

### The Role of Width and Depth
One of the most significant findings involves the "width" of the SAE's latent space—the number of features it is allowed to learn. The results showed that wider latent spaces (e.g., 65k features vs. 4k features) lead to much higher interpretability scores. This supports the idea that LLM activations are "overcomplete," and a larger latent space is necessary to separate distinct concepts that might otherwise be "smashed" together into a single polysemantic neuron.

![width-analysis](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2509.00691v2/width_analysis.png)
*{fig:width-analysis}: Increasing the latent space width of an SAE significantly improves its ability to capture independent and contrastive features.*

When looking at where to probe the LLM, the benchmark suggested that middle layers (such as layers 10-15 in a 26-layer model) tend to contain the most interpretable features. Early layers often focus on low-level token statistics, while very deep layers may be more focused on predicting the next token, making the features in the middle most conducive to human understanding.

![depth-analysis](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2509.00691v2/depth_analysis.png)
*{fig:depth-analysis}: Interpretability scores tend to peak in the middle layers of the LLM, suggesting these layers contain the most semantically rich features.*

## Validating the Score with Visualization

To ensure that the max-pooling aggregation used in the scoring (picking the most responsive neuron) was appropriate, the authors visualized the distribution of scores for specific subjects.

![score-visualization](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2509.00691v2/score_vis.png)
*{fig:score-visualization}: Visualization of neuron activations for the subject "Computer" shows that only a few neurons exhibit high contrastive and independence scores, validating the focus on dominant features.*

The visualization reveals that for any given subject, the vast majority of neurons are inactive or weakly active. Only a small subset of neurons shows high contrastive or independence scores. This sparse distribution confirms that the SAE is successfully isolating specific concepts into individual neurons, and the CE-Bench scoring mechanism accurately picks up on these specialized signals.

## Conclusion and Significance

CE-Bench provides a new path forward for evaluating the internal representations of large language models. By moving away from costly and variable LLM judges, it offers a deterministic and efficient metric that researchers can use to iterate on SAE design.

The benchmark's significance lies in its accessibility. It allows for:
1.  **Rapid Iteration:** Developers can test new training recipes without waiting for expensive LLM evaluations.
2.  **Reproducibility:** Scientific comparisons between different SAEs are more reliable when the evaluation metric is deterministic.
3.  **Deeper Insights:** Systematic studies of architecture, width, and layer depth provide clear guidance on how to build more interpretable models.

While it does not replace natural language explanations entirely, CE-Bench acts as a powerful "filter" and quantitative baseline. It ensures that the features being studied are truly disentangled and subject-specific, providing a foundation for a more transparent and understandable future for artificial intelligence.
