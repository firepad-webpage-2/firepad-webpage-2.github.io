## Introduction to Flexible Forwards and American Options

In the foreign exchange (FX) markets, businesses often face uncertainty regarding the exact timing of their future cash flows. To manage this risk, they utilize Flexible Forward (FF) contracts. Unlike a standard forward contract, which mandates an exchange at a single fixed date, an FF contract allows the holder to choose the delivery date within a pre-specified window, such as $[T_1, T_2]$. Because the holder aims to choose the timing that maximizes the contract's value, the valuation of an FF contract becomes an optimal stopping problem. Mathematically, this makes the FF contract equivalent to an American-style option on the timing of the exchange.

Valuing these instruments accurately requires models that can capture the complexities of the FX market, most notably the "volatility skew" or "smile." This refers to the observation that implied volatilities vary significantly across different strike prices. While simple models like Black-Scholes assume a constant volatility, they cannot account for this skew. More advanced models like the Heston stochastic volatility model are better, but standard versions often use constant parameters that fail to capture how the skew changes over time (the term structure of volatility).

This paper addresses these challenges by developing a comprehensive framework for valuing American options and FF contracts under a time-inhomogeneous Heston model. By allowing parameters like long-term variance, vol-of-vol, and correlation to change over time, the model can be calibrated more precisely to market data. The researchers introduce highly efficient numerical methods based on integral equations and spectral techniques, significantly improving the speed and accuracy of pricing compared to traditional grid-based methods.

## The Time-Inhomogeneous Heston Model

The core of the proposed valuation framework is the Heston stochastic volatility model, adapted to include time-dependent coefficients. The dynamics of the log-price $x_t = \log(S_t/K)$ and the variance $v_t$ are described by the following system of stochastic differential equations:

$$
dx_t = (r_d(t) - r_f(t) - \frac{1}{2}v_t)dt + \sqrt{v_t}dW^S_t
$$
$$
dv_t = \kappa(\theta(t) - v_t)dt + \xi(t)\sqrt{v_t}dW^v_t
$$

where the correlation between the two Wiener processes is given by $dW^S_t dW^v_t = \rho(t)dt$. In this setup, $r_d$ and $r_f$ are the domestic and foreign interest rates, $\kappa$ is the mean-reversion speed, $\theta(t)$ is the time-varying long-term variance level, $\xi(t)$ is the volatility of volatility, and $\rho(t)$ represents the correlation between asset price and volatility.

The model uses a hybrid parameterization: $\theta(t)$ is modeled as a continuous function (often an exponential decay to reflect market expectations), while $\xi(t)$ and $\rho(t)$ are treated as piecewise constant over specific time intervals. This structure is strategically chosen to maintain analytical tractability while providing enough flexibility to match the market's term structure of skew. For FF contracts, the optimal exercise strategy is known as "bang-bang," meaning the holder will execute the entire notional amount at a single optimal time $t \in [T_1, T_2]$, rather than spreading the exercise over multiple dates.

## The Integral Equation Approach

To price American-style derivatives, the researchers utilize an Integral Equation (IE) approach. This method decomposes the price of an American option $P(t, x, v)$ into two components: the price of an equivalent European option $P_E(t, x, v)$ and an Early Exercise Premium (EEP). The EEP represents the added value of being able to exercise the option before maturity.

The price decomposition formula is:

$$
P(t, x, v) = P_E(t, x, v) + K \int_t^T D_d(t, u) E_Q \{ [r_d(u) - r_f(u)e^{x_u}] \mathbf{1}_{(x_u, v_u) \in \mathcal{E}} \} du
$$

In this equation, $D_d(t, u)$ is the domestic discount factor, and the expectation is taken under the risk-neutral measure $Q$. The term $\mathbf{1}_{(x_u, v_u) \in \mathcal{E}}$ is an indicator function that is equal to one if the asset price and variance at time $u$ fall within the early exercise region $\mathcal{E}$, and zero otherwise.

The primary difficulty lies in the fact that the early exercise region $\mathcal{E}$ is defined by an unknown boundary surface $x^*(t, v)$. This is a "free-boundary problem," where the boundary must be determined as part of the solution. The boundary is found by solving a nonlinear integral equation derived from the "value matching" condition, which states that at the boundary, the option's value must equal its intrinsic value (the immediate payoff from exercise).

## Challenging the Linear Boundary Assumption

A significant contribution of this research is the empirical demonstration that the early exercise boundary is inherently nonlinear. Much of the existing literature on American options under stochastic volatility assumes that the boundary $S^*(t, v)$ can be approximated as a linear function of the variance $v$.

The authors provide visual evidence and quantitative measures showing that this assumption is often incorrect. For both American Put options and FF contracts, the exercise boundary exhibits substantial curvature that changes over time and across different levels of volatility.

This nonlinearity has practical consequences. Using a linear approximation can lead to pricing errors and sub-optimal exercise strategies. By solving the integral equation without assuming a specific functional form for the boundary, the proposed methodology captures these nonlinearities precisely, leading to more accurate valuations. For FF contracts, the boundary behavior can be even more complex, sometimes increasing with variance depending on the interest rate differentials between the two currencies.

## Numerical Methods: COS and DSINC

Evaluating the expectation in the EEP integral requires knowledge of the joint transition density of the asset price and its variance. Since the Heston model is "affine," its characteristic function is known analytically, even with time-dependent parameters. The researchers propose two spectral methods to compute the integral efficiently:

### The Double Cosine (COS) Expansion
The first method, IECOS, uses a double Fourier-cosine expansion to approximate the joint density. This method is exceptionally fast, with a computational complexity of $O(N_x N_v \log N_v)$, leveraging the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT). While effective for many parameter regimes, the COS method can suffer from "Gibbs oscillations"—spurious fluctuations in the numerical result—when the density is very sharp or "peaky." This typically happens when the Feller condition $2\kappa\theta > \xi^2$ is violated, causing the variance density to cluster near zero.

### The Damped Sinc (DSINC) Method
To overcome the limitations of the COS method, the authors develop a Damped Sinc (DSINC) local-basis scheme. This method uses Sinc functions to represent the log-price density. The key advantage is that the resulting integral over the spectral variable is non-oscillatory and can be evaluated very accurately using standard Gaussian quadrature. The complexity of handling time-dependent parameters is reduced to $O(N)$ using a matrix propagator approach to solve the underlying Riccati equations.

Numerical tests show that the DSINC method is remarkably robust. In a test suite of 432 parameter sets, DSINC achieved a median accuracy twelve times better than IECOS and maintained low error levels even in regimes where the COS expansion failed due to numerical instabilities.

## Results and Practical Impact

The researchers benchmarked their spectral methods against a high-precision finite-difference (FD) solver. The results highlight the efficiency of the new approach:

*   **Speed**: The IECOS and DSINC methods can price an American option or FF contract in approximately 1 to 2 seconds. In contrast, a fine-grid FD solver required 27 seconds to achieve similar accuracy.
*   **Accuracy**: The DSINC method proved to be the most reliable, consistently producing accurate prices across a wide range of market conditions and model parameters.
*   **Premium Valuation**: For FF contracts, the early exercise feature (the "American" part) adds significant value. The study found that this premium can range from 160 to 270 pips. Failing to account for this timing flexibility would lead to a substantial underpricing of the contract.

The significance of this work lies in its combination of model realism and computational efficiency. By moving beyond constant parameters and linear boundary assumptions, and by introducing robust spectral numerical techniques, the authors provide a framework that is both theoretically sound and practically viable for real-time trading and risk management. The use of "Put-Call duality" and the "Delayed Exercise Premium" (DEP) decomposition further ensures the numerical stability of the results, even for options that are deep "in-the-money."

In conclusion, the paper offers a sophisticated toolkit for financial institutions to value complex FX derivatives. The findings emphasize that the early exercise boundary is a dynamic, nonlinear surface, and that modern spectral methods like DSINC provide a powerful alternative to traditional numerical techniques, offering the speed and precision required in today's fast-moving financial markets.
