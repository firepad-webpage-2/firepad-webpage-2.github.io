## Overcoming the Computational Bottleneck in Aneurysm Hemodynamics

Abdominal Aortic Aneurysms (AAAs) represent a significant clinical challenge, as the localized dilation of the aorta carries a risk of life-threatening rupture. Currently, clinical decisions are largely dictated by the maximum diameter of the aneurysm. However, this geometric metric is often an insufficient predictor of rupture risk. Modern cardiovascular research highlights that hemodynamic factors—such as wall shear stress (WSS), pressure distributions, and complex vortex dynamics—play a crucial role in the evolution and eventual failure of the arterial wall.

![aaa-geometry](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13629v1/AAA_model.png)
*{fig:aaa-geometry}: Idealized Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA) geometry used for hemodynamic modeling, showing the inlet, aneurysm sac, and distal extension.*

To obtain these hemodynamic markers, two primary methods are used: non-invasive imaging like 4D flow MRI and high-fidelity Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD). While MRI is non-invasive, it often lacks the spatial and temporal resolution required to accurately measure gradients near the vessel walls. CFD, on the other hand, provides excellent resolution but is computationally prohibitive, often requiring hours or days to simulate a single patient case. This latency prevents its use in routine clinical workflows where rapid diagnostics are required.

The research presented here introduces a new approach using Physics-Informed Deep Operator Networks (PI-DeepONets) to bridge this gap. By learning the underlying operator that maps patient-specific flow conditions to the full 3D fluid velocity and pressure fields, the proposed Modified Multi-Input Multi-Output PI-DeepONet (M³PI-DeepONet) provides a significant speedup over traditional solvers while maintaining high physical consistency.

## The Evolution of Neural Operators

Traditional neural networks are typically used to learn mappings between finite-dimensional vectors. In the context of fluid dynamics, standard Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) excel at solving specific instances of partial differential equations (PDEs) by embedding physical laws into the loss function. However, a major limitation of PINNs is that they must be retrained whenever the boundary conditions or source terms change.

Deep Operator Networks (DeepONets) represent a paradigm shift by learning mappings between infinite-dimensional function spaces. A DeepONet consists of two main components:
1.  **Branch Network:** This encodes the input functions (e.g., inlet flow rate waveforms) into a latent representation.
2.  **Trunk Network:** This encodes the query coordinates (spatial positions and time) into a basis representation.

The final output is generated by combining these two representations, effectively approximating the solution operator $G(f)(y) \approx \sum_{k=1}^q \beta_k(f) \tau_k(y)$, where $\beta$ comes from the branch and $\tau$ from the trunk. While effective, standard multi-input versions of this architecture (known as MIONets) often treat branch inputs in a way that keeps the trunk basis functions static, limiting the model's ability to adapt to complex, heterogeneous input signals.

## The M³PI-DeepONet and Aggregated Injection

The core innovation of this work is the "Aggregated Injection" strategy. In complex hemodynamic problems, the solution depends on multiple inputs, such as the velocity profile at the inlet and the initial state of the fluid. The M³PI-DeepONet architecture allows the trunk network—which handles the spatial and temporal coordinates—to dynamically adapt its basis functions based on a fused representation of all active input branches.

![m3pi-deeponet-architecture](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13629v1/Modified_Arch.png)
*{fig:m3pi-deeponet-architecture}: The M³PI-DeepONet architecture, highlighting the Aggregated Injection mechanism that fuses multiple branch inputs to gate the trunk network layers.*

In the Aggregated Injection strategy, latent representations $U^{(\xi)}$ from $n$ different input branches are combined using a learnable weighted sum:
$$
U_{agg} = \sum_{\xi=1}^n \alpha_{\xi} U^{(\xi)}
$$
where $\alpha_{\xi}$ are trainable weights that determine the relative importance of each input channel. This aggregated context is then "injected" into each hidden layer of the trunk network using a gating mechanism. This enables the coordinate basis to become "input-adaptive," meaning the way the network interprets spatial and temporal positions changes depending on the specific physiological conditions provided at the input branches.

## Embedding Physics for Data-Efficiency

One of the primary advantages of this framework is its reduced reliance on labeled data. In many medical applications, high-resolution CFD data is difficult to obtain in large quantities. By incorporating the 3D Navier-Stokes equations directly into the training process, the model learns the physics of fluid flow as a regularizer.

The total loss function $\mathcal{L}_{M^3PI-DeepONet}(\theta) = \sum_i \lambda_i \mathcal{L}_i(\theta)$ includes terms for:
*   **Data Consistency:** Agreement with a small set of interior supervised points (only 0.3% of the available CFD data).
*   **Boundary Conditions:** Enforcement of the no-slip condition at the arterial walls ($\mathbf{v} = 0$) and prescribed velocity at the inlet.
*   **Physical Laws:** Satisfaction of the dimensionless Navier-Stokes equations:
$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0
$$
$$
\frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla) \mathbf{v} = -\nabla p + \frac{1}{Re} \nabla^2 \mathbf{v}
$$
The Reynolds number $Re = \frac{\rho_f U D}{\mu_f}$ characterizes the flow regime, ensuring the network respects the fundamental principles of mass and momentum conservation. This physics-informed approach allows the model to generalize accurately even in regions where training data is sparse.

## Data Stratification and Generation

To train and validate the model, a comprehensive database of 15 distinct pulsatile flow rate waveforms was generated from clinical data. These waveforms were categorized into three groups (FR1, FR2, FR3) based on their peak amplitudes and temporal profiles, representing a range of physiological conditions.

![data-generation-pipeline](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13629v1/Generation_pipeline.png)
*{fig:data-generation-pipeline}: Workflow for generating ground-truth data, starting from patient flow rate waveforms to high-fidelity CFD simulations.*

The data was meticulously stratified to evaluate the model's performance in different scenarios. A "Temporal and Spatial Split" was implemented, where certain regions of the geometry and specific time steps in the cardiac cycle were held out during training. This resulted in three distinct testing challenges:
1.  **Spatial Test:** Predicting flow at coordinates unseen during training.
2.  **Temporal Test:** Extrapolating to time steps within the cardiac cycle that were not part of the training set.
3.  **Hard Test:** The intersection of both—predicting flow at unseen locations at unseen times.

## Experimental Results and Accuracy

The effectiveness of the M³PI-DeepONet was evaluated through extensive ablation studies. A key finding was the importance of branch selection. The researchers found that combining the inlet velocity ($v_{inlet,2}$) with the initial condition ($v_{ic,2}$) provided the best balance of accuracy and computational cost.

![branch-selection-performance](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13629v1/branch_selection_bar_chart.png)
*{fig:branch-selection-performance}: Comparison of L₂-relative errors for various branch input configurations across spatial, temporal, and hard test scenarios.*

The introduction of Aggregated Injection (AI) led to a 36.21% relative improvement in velocity accuracy compared to standard multi-input DeepONet architectures. When combined with other training techniques like Kaiming initialization and exponential learning rate decay, the final model achieved an average relative error of only 3.37% for the magnitude of velocity and 4.25% for pressure across all 15 waveforms.

Crucially, the model demonstrated an inference speedup of approximately 36× compared to the reference CFD solver. While a full CFD simulation for a cardiac cycle took roughly 12 hours, the M³PI-DeepONet could generate the same 101-snapshot solution in just 20 minutes.

## Qualitative Assessment of Flow Features

Qualitative comparisons between the DeepONet predictions and the CFD ground truth show that the model captures essential hemodynamic features with high fidelity. During peak systole, the model accurately represents the high-velocity jet passing through the aneurysm and the complex recirculation zones that form near the distal neck.

![qualitative-velocity-fields](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13629v1/qualitative_fields_peak.png)
*{fig:qualitative-velocity-fields}: Comparison of velocity and pressure fields predicted by M³PI-DeepONet versus CFD at peak systole for a held-out test waveform.*

Probe-point analysis further confirmed that the temporal evolution of both velocity and pressure remains consistent throughout the cardiac cycle. The model correctly tracks the rapid acceleration during the systolic phase and the slow deceleration and oscillatory behavior during diastole. The highest errors were typically localized in high-gradient regions near the aneurysm boundaries, which is expected given the complexity of the flow in those areas.

## Significance and Future Directions

The M³PI-DeepONet represents a significant step toward real-time clinical diagnostics for vascular diseases. By combining multi-input flexibility with an input-adaptive trunk basis and physics-informed regularization, the framework overcomes the limitations of both traditional CFD and standard machine learning models.

The ability to generate high-fidelity hemodynamic maps in minutes rather than hours could allow clinicians to perform "what-if" analyses, exploring how different physiological states or blood pressure conditions might affect the risk of aneurysm rupture. Furthermore, the model's high data efficiency—requiring only 0.3% of internal labeled data—makes it a practical tool even when extensive simulation databases are not available.

Future research aims to extend this framework to patient-specific geometries, where the arterial shape itself is treated as an input branch. Additionally, incorporating fluid-structure interaction (FSI) to account for the deformation of the arterial wall and modeling non-Newtonian blood properties will further enhance the physiological realism and clinical utility of the system.
