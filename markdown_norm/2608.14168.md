## Addressing Connectivity in the High-Speed Satellite Era

The evolution toward 6G wireless communication envisions a world of truly ubiquitous connectivity, where broadband services reach the most remote corners of the globe, from the middle of the ocean to the highest flight paths. To achieve this, the industry is integrating Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTNs)—which include Low-Earth Orbit (LEO) satellites and high-altitude platforms—into the standard cellular framework. However, communicating with a satellite traveling at several kilometers per second is fundamentally different from communicating with a stationary cell tower.

One of the most significant hurdles in NTNs is the physical random access procedure. Before a device (User Equipment or UE) can send data, it must perform a "handshake" with the network via the Physical Random Access Channel (PRACH). This involves transmitting a specific signal called a preamble. The network uses this preamble to identify the device and, more importantly, to estimate the timing delay caused by the distance between the device and the satellite. 

In LEO satellite systems, two major physics-based problems arise: massive propagation delays (due to the hundreds of kilometers of space) and extreme Doppler shifts (caused by the satellite's high orbital velocity). Standard 5G systems use Zadoff-Chu (ZC) sequences for these preambles. While ZC sequences work well on the ground, they struggle in space. Large frequency shifts cause the correlation peaks at the receiver to shift or split, leading to "timing bias" where the network thinks the device is closer or further than it actually is, or even failing to identify the device entirely.

Researchers have proposed using Hyperbolic Frequency Modulation (HFM) to solve this. HFM signals, traditionally used in sophisticated sonar and radar systems, possess a unique property: they are inherently Doppler-tolerant. This paper explores how HFM can be adapted for the 5G New Radio (NR) framework to ensure that even with massive, uncompensated frequency shifts, a satellite can accurately time and identify multiple users simultaneously.

## The Limitations of Current Preamble Designs

To understand why a new design is needed, we must look at how the receiver "sees" the incoming signal. This is characterized by the Ambiguity Function (AF), which maps how well a signal's correlation peak is preserved across different delays and frequency shifts.

In standard terrestrial 5G, Zadoff-Chu sequences are preferred because they have a constant power level and excellent correlation properties. However, their AF takes the form of a "lattice." When a large Doppler shift occurs, the correlation peak doesn't just degrade; it jumps to a different position in the delay-Doppler plane. This makes it impossible for the satellite to tell if a signal arrived late because of distance or if it just looks late because of the frequency shift.

Linear Frequency Modulation (LFM), another alternative, exhibits a "ridge" in its AF. In LFM, the frequency changes linearly over time. If there is a frequency offset, the receiver sees a correlation peak that is perfectly sharp but shifted in time. This creates a deterministic timing bias. If the satellite doesn't know the exact Doppler shift, it will consistently miscalculate the distance to the device.

While some techniques, like Frequency-Domain Superposed LFM (FDS-LFM), attempt to fix this by breaking the signal into pieces, they often fail when the Doppler shift becomes very high (e.g., above 100 kHz) because the phase alignment between those pieces is destroyed. The HFM approach differs by using a nonlinear phase evolution that naturally resists these shifts without needing complex multi-part processing.

## Designing the HFM-Inspired Preamble

The core contribution of this work is the design of a frequency-domain HFM preamble sequence that fits within the existing 5G NR frame structure. Instead of a linear change in frequency, HFM uses a logarithmic phase evolution. The mathematical form of the proposed frequency-domain sequence $S[k]$ is defined as:

$$
S[k] = \exp\left( -j \frac{2\pi f_c T}{c_m} \ln\left( 1 - \frac{c_m k \Delta f}{f_c} \right) \right) \text{ for } k = 0, 1, \dots, L-1
$$

In this expression, $f_c$ represents the carrier frequency, $T$ is the symbol duration, and $\Delta f = 120 \text{ kHz}$ is the subcarrier spacing used in the simulation. The parameter $c_m$ is a scaling factor that serves as the "identity" for a specific preamble. By changing $c_m$, the network can generate a codebook of different preambles, allowing it to distinguish between multiple users transmitting at the same time.

For a preamble length $L=834$, this HFM sequence replaces the Zadoff-Chu sequence in the standard PRACH transmission. Because it is applied in the frequency domain before the Inverse Fast Fourier Transform (IFFT) at the transmitter, it remains fully compatible with the Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing (OFDM) structure used in modern cellular networks. This means that hardware changes are minimal; only the sequence itself and the logic for the matched filter at the receiver need to be updated.

The effectiveness of HFM is most visible when comparing the 3D ambiguity surfaces of the three waveforms. The goal of a good preamble is to have a single, sharp peak at the center (zero delay, zero Doppler) and as little energy as possible everywhere else.

1.  **Zadoff-Chu (ZC):** As shown in {fig:zc-af}, the ZC surface is covered in a grid of peaks. Under high Doppler, the receiver might pick up any one of these "ghost" peaks, leading to a complete failure in synchronization.
2.  **LFM:** The LFM surface shows a clear diagonal ridge. If the frequency shifts, the peak moves along that diagonal, creating the timing bias mentioned earlier.
3.  **HFM:** The HFM surface maintains a dominant central peak. While the peak's amplitude might decrease slightly under extreme Doppler, its position in time does not shift. This "Doppler-delay decoupling" is the property that makes HFM so robust for satellite links.

## Multi-User Detection and Identification

In a real satellite cell, many devices might try to access the network simultaneously. This creates multi-user interference. The paper addresses this by deriving a "minimum spacing condition" for the scaling factors $c_m$ in the HFM codebook. By carefully choosing these factors, the researchers ensure that the cross-correlation between different preambles stays low.

In simulations of a multi-user scenario with three UEs transmitting at different delays and with a massive uncompensated Doppler shift of 300 kHz, the results were stark. The ZC-based receiver produced a "messy" correlation output where individual users could not be clearly distinguished from the background noise and interference peaks. The LFM receiver showed shifted peaks that overlapped with each other. In contrast, the HFM receiver produced three sharp, distinct peaks at the exact correct timing locations.

This performance is quantified using metrics like the Peak-to-Sidelobe Level Ratio (PSLR). A lower (more negative) PSLR indicates that the main peak is much stronger than any false peaks. HFM consistently achieves a lower PSLR than both ZC and LFM, especially as the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) improves.

## Practical Results and Computational Efficiency

The ultimate test for any communication signal is the Probability of Detection ($P_D$). The researchers simulated a LEO satellite at 600 km altitude using a carrier frequency of 30 GHz (Ka-band). Under these conditions, Doppler shifts can exceed 500 kHz.

The findings showed that:
*   **Doppler Robustness:** At a Doppler shift of 100 kHz, the detection probability of ZC preambles drops to near zero. FDS-LFM also begins to fail. HFM, however, maintains $P_D \approx 0.9$ at $-2$ dB SNR even when the Doppler shift is as high as 580 kHz.
*   **Timing Accuracy:** The Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) of the timing estimation was significantly lower for HFM. Because HFM doesn't suffer from the "ridge" effect of LFM, it provides sub-sample timing accuracy without needing to know the exact Doppler shift beforehand.
*   **Receiver Complexity:** Because the HFM peak doesn't move with Doppler, the satellite receiver can use a much "coarser" search grid. In standard systems, the receiver might need to test hundreds of different frequency hypotheses to find the signal. The paper demonstrates that HFM can achieve high accuracy with a very small hypothesis bank, satisfying $|V_{HFM}| \ll |V_{ZC}|$. This reduces the power consumption and processing requirements for the satellite.

## Implications for 6G and Beyond

This HFM-inspired design represents a move away from traditional "terrestrial-first" waveforms toward designs specifically optimized for the physics of space. By decoupling timing from frequency, HFM allows satellites to serve users who might not have access to precise GPS data for pre-compensation—such as devices in "urban canyons" or low-cost IoT sensors.

Furthermore, the fact that HFM works within the existing 5G NR framework makes it a viable candidate for inclusion in future releases of the 3GPP standard. As we move toward 6G, where the line between terrestrial and satellite networks will blur, waveforms that can handle the extreme dynamics of LEO orbits will be essential for maintaining a reliable, global connection. The success of HFM in these simulations suggests that nonlinear frequency modulation is a key tool for solving the most difficult synchronization problems in next-generation networks.
