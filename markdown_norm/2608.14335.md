## Context and the Role of Multi-Orbit Networks

The evolution of wireless communication toward the sixth generation (6G) necessitates a paradigm shift in how global connectivity is achieved. A central pillar of this vision is the integration of Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTNs), which extend the reach of traditional ground-based infrastructure to remote, maritime, and aerial environments. Within this landscape, satellite constellations are being reorganized from isolated layers into unified, multi-orbit systems. These systems leverage satellites at different altitudes: Low Earth Orbit (LEO), Medium Earth Orbit (MEO), and Geostationary Earth Orbit (GEO).

![system-architecture](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.14335v1/figures/system.png)
*{fig:system-architecture}: An illustration of a multi-layer satellite network including LEO, MEO, and GEO layers, where users can be served by beams from different orbital altitudes depending on availability and network conditions.*

Each orbital layer presents a unique set of trade-offs. LEO satellites, orbiting at altitudes below 2,000 km, offer low propagation delay and high data rates, making them suitable for latency-sensitive applications. However, their high orbital velocity means they quickly pass over a user's location, necessitating frequent handovers to maintain a continuous connection. In contrast, GEO satellites, positioned at approximately 35,786 km, provide persistent coverage over vast geographic areas but suffer from high latency and limited capacity relative to the distance. MEO satellites occupy the space between them, offering a balance of latency and coverage duration.

Managing these heterogeneous resources effectively is a significant challenge. Traditional approaches often treat handover management—the process of switching a user's connection from one satellite or beam to another—and power allocation as separate problems. However, in a multi-orbit setting, these decisions are deeply coupled. A handover to a more distant orbit (e.g., LEO to GEO) might provide a more stable link but requires a different power profile and incurs a higher signaling penalty. Coordinating these layers requires a strategy that can handle the fast dynamics of LEO constellations while strategically offloading traffic to higher orbits when necessary.

## Formulating the Joint Optimization Problem

The core technical challenge addressed in this work is the joint optimization of satellite association (which user connects to which beam) and power allocation (how much power is assigned to each link). The objective is to maximize the aggregate network throughput over a given time horizon while accounting for the costs associated with handovers.

This is framed as a Mixed-Integer Nonlinear Program (MINLP). Let $R_u(t)$ be the data rate achieved by user $u$ at time $t$, and $\beta_u(t)$ be a penalty factor representing the cost of a handover. The optimization goal can be expressed as:

$$\max_{I, P} \sum_{t=1}^T \sum_{u=1}^U R_u(t) (1 - \beta_u(t))$$

where $I$ represents the binary association matrix and $P$ represents the continuous power allocation variables. The rate $R_u(t)$ depends on the Signal-to-Interference-plus-Noise Ratio (SINR), defined as:

$$\gamma_{u,b}(t) = \frac{p_{u,b}(t) G_{u,b}(t)}{\sum_{b' \neq b} p_{u,b'}(t) G_{u,b'}(t) + N_0 W}$$

In this expression, $p_{u,b}(t)$ is the power allocated to user $u$ on beam $b$, $G_{u,b}(t)$ is the channel gain, $W$ is the bandwidth, and $N_0$ is the noise power spectral density. The throughput is then:

$$R_u(t) = W \log_2(1 + \gamma_{u,b}(t))$$

The optimization is subject to several critical constraints:
1.  **Power Budget**: The total power transmitted by all beams of a satellite $s$ cannot exceed its maximum capacity $P_s$:
    $$\sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}_s} p_{u,b}(t) \leq P_s$$
2.  **Quality of Service (QoS)**: Each user must be guaranteed a minimum data rate $R_{\text{min}}$ to maintain service quality:
    $$R_u(t) \geq R_{\text{min}}$$
3.  **Visibility and Capacity**: Users can only connect to satellites that are visible from their location, and each satellite beam has a finite capacity for concurrent users.

The coupling of binary association decisions with continuous power variables, combined with the non-convex nature of the SINR-based rate function, makes this problem computationally intractable for large networks.

## The Hierarchical Decomposition Framework

To solve this intractable problem, the researchers proposed a hierarchical decomposition that separates the discrete association decisions from the continuous power allocation. This framework uses Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) to handle the long-term, combinatorial task of satellite association and convex optimization to handle the instantaneous, high-precision task of power allocation.

The process operates in a closed loop at each time step:
1.  **MARL Decision**: Individual agents (representing users) observe their environment and, through a communication-enabled policy, decide whether to stay with their current beam or perform a handover to a new candidate beam. This fixes the binary association matrix $I$.
2.  **Power Optimization**: With the associations fixed, the problem of finding the best power levels $P$ becomes a convex optimization problem. This subproblem is solved exactly using professional-grade solvers to find the optimal power allocation $P^*$ that maximizes throughput while respecting all constraints.
3.  **Feedback and Reward**: The resulting optimal rates $R_u^*(t)$ are used to calculate a reward for the MARL agents. This reward explicitly penalizes handovers, with higher penalties assigned to transitions between different orbital layers (e.g., LEO to MEO or GEO).

## Learning-Based Association with Multi-Agent Communication

The association policy is trained using Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO). In this decentralized-execution model, each user operates as an agent but shares a common neural network policy with others to promote cooperative behavior.

To enable coordination without a centralized controller, the agents use the Targeted Multi-Agent Communication (TarMAC) mechanism. Each agent $i$ maintains a hidden state $h_i$ and generates a "query" and a "key" to determine which other agents have relevant information. Using an attention mechanism, the agent computes weights $e_{i,j}$ for messages from its neighbors:

$$e_{i,j} = \text{softmax}( \frac{(Q h_i)^T (K h_j)}{\sqrt{d_k}} )$$

where $Q$ and $K$ are weight matrices and $d_k$ is a scaling factor. The agent then aggregates the messages into a context vector $c_i$:

$$c_i = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i} e_{i,j} (V h_j)$$

This context vector allows the agent to make association decisions that consider the potential interference or congestion caused to nearby users. The state vector provided to each agent is "layer-aware," including indices for LEO, MEO, and GEO layers, the time elapsed since the last handover, and the current achieved rate relative to $R_{\text{min}}$.

## Optimal Power Allocation Subproblem

Once the agents have selected their respective satellites and beams, the system solves the power allocation subproblem. Because the interference terms in the SINR denominator depend on the power allocated to other beams, this remains a complex task. However, by fixing the associations, the search space is reduced to a continuous domain where the objective function is concave with respect to power.

The researchers utilize CVXPY and the MOSEK solver to find $P^*$ at every step of the reinforcement learning training process. This ensures that the rewards given to the MARL agents are always based on the "best-case" power scenario for their chosen associations. The reward for user $u$ is defined as:

$$r_u(t) = R_u^*(t) \cdot (1 - \beta_u(t))$$

where $\beta_u(t)$ is the handover penalty. By integrating the exact solver into the training loop, the MARL policy learns to select satellite associations that are not just strong in terms of raw signal strength, but are also "power-efficient" and robust to interference from other coordinated users.

## Performance Analysis and Emergent Behavior

The framework was evaluated using realistic satellite orbit data (TLE files) for a scenario involving 30 users and multiple satellites across LEO, MEO, and GEO layers. The results demonstrated a superior balance between throughput and stability compared to standard heuristics.

### Throughput and Handover Trade-offs
The "Greedy" baseline, which always picks the satellite with the strongest signal, achieved the highest raw throughput but at the cost of excessive handovers—nearly 28 per time slot. In contrast, the proposed MARL approach achieved 92% of the Greedy throughput while reducing handovers by a factor of four.

### Emergent Offloading
Perhaps the most significant finding was the emergent behavior of the multi-layer policy. Even though LEO satellites usually offer the best individual rates, the policy learned to offload approximately 5% of users to MEO and GEO layers.

This offloading behavior suggests that the agents realized that by moving some users to higher, more stable orbits, they could reduce the overall handover frequency and interference in the LEO layer, leading to better total network performance. This strategy emerged naturally from the optimization of the reward function rather than being hard-coded.

## Broad Significance and Future Directions

This work provides a structured method for managing the complex interplay between different orbital layers in modern satellite networks. By combining the strengths of reinforcement learning and convex optimization, the researchers created a system that is both aware of long-term temporal dynamics (handovers) and short-term physical constraints (power and interference).

The significance of this approach lies in its scalability and decentralized nature. As satellite constellations grow to include thousands of satellites, centralized optimization becomes impossible. The use of MARL with localized communication allows the network to scale while maintaining high performance. Furthermore, the explicit inclusion of layer-dependent penalties provides a knob for network operators to tune the balance between latency (LEO) and stability (GEO).

Future extensions of this research could involve more complex antenna models, such as Multi-Input Multi-Output (MIMO) systems, and the consideration of inter-satellite links (ISLs) for backhauling data. The current success in coordinating 30 users across three orbital layers serves as a template for more expansive 6G non-terrestrial network architectures.
