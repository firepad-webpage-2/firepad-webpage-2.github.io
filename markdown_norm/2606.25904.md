## Introduction to Other-Regarding Preferences

In classical economic models, individuals are often depicted as "homo economicus"—agents who maximize their own utility based solely on their personal consumption and experiences. However, real-world behavior frequently deviates from this assumption. People exhibit other-regarding preferences, meaning their well-being is affected by the outcomes of others. These preferences can manifest as altruism, where one derives pleasure from another's success, or paternalism, where an individual has specific views on what is best for someone else, even if that person disagrees.

The inclusion of these preferences in social welfare analysis poses a significant challenge. If a society aims to aggregate the preferences of its citizens into a single social preference, how should it handle the fact that Citizen A has strong opinions about Citizen B's lifestyle? Should Citizen A's preference for Citizen B's behavior be given the same weight as Citizen B's own preference for their own behavior?

John Mori's research addresses these questions by introducing the principle of "individual sovereignty." This principle suggests that when a choice only affects the life, body, or property of a single individual, society should defer to that individual's judgment. This concept is a stronger version of the "liberalism" axiom proposed by Amartya Sen in 1970. Mori explores the implications of this principle when individuals have complex, other-regarding preferences, showing that individual sovereignty naturally leads to a form of social welfare known as "self-regarding utilitarianism."

## The Framework of Social Choice

To analyze these interactions, the paper utilizes a formal mathematical framework based on expected utility theory. We consider a society of $n$ individuals. The set of all possible social outcomes is denoted by $X$, which is the Cartesian product of individual outcome sets:

$$
X = X_1 \times X_2 \times \dots \times X_n
$$

In this structure, a single social outcome $x$ is a vector $(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)$, where each $x_i$ represents the specific outcome for individual $i$. Decisions are made over lotteries (probability distributions) over these outcomes, denoted by $\Delta(X)$.

Each individual $i$ has a preference relation $\succsim_i$ over these lotteries, and society has a social preference relation $\succsim_0$. Both are assumed to follow the von Neumann-Morgenstern (vNM) axioms, allowing them to be represented by utility indices $u_i: X \to \mathbb{R}$ and $u_0: X \to \mathbb{R}$.

A critical assumption in this model is "mutual independence." This assumption implies that an individual's total utility can be decomposed into two parts: their self-regarding utility (how they feel about their own outcome) and their other-regarding utility (how they feel about the outcomes of everyone else). Mathematically, for any social outcome $x$, the utility of individual $i$ is:

$$
u_i(x) = u_{i,i}(x_i) + u_{i,-i}(x_{-i})
$$

Where $u_{i,i} : X_i \to \mathbb{R}$ captures $i$'s preferences over their own outcomes $x_i$, and $u_{i,-i} : X_{-i} \to \mathbb{R}$ captures $i$'s preferences over the outcomes of others $x_{-i} = (x_1, \dots, x_{i-1}, x_{i+1}, \dots, x_n)$.

## The Principle of Individual Sovereignty

The core of the paper revolves around the "Individual Sovereignty" axiom. This axiom states that if two social lotteries differ only in the outcome for a single individual $i$, then society's preference between those lotteries must be identical to individual $i$'s preference.

For example, if society is choosing between two health insurance plans that are identical for everyone except for the specific treatment provided to Citizen $i$, society should let Citizen $i$'s preference dictate the choice. This principle respects the autonomy of the individual in matters that are primarily "self-regarding."

The paper's first major finding (Theorem 1) is a characterization of social welfare under this axiom. If individual preferences satisfy mutual independence and social preferences satisfy individual sovereignty, then the social utility function $u_0$ must be a positive linear combination of the individuals' self-regarding utility functions:

$$
u_0(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i u_{i,i}(x_i) + C
$$

In this equation, $w_i > 0$ are weights assigned to each individual, and $C$ is a constant. This result is significant because it suggests that under individual sovereignty, other-regarding preferences do not appear in the structure of the social utility function. Society acts as a "self-regarding utilitarian," focusing only on the self-centered components of each person's well-being. However, this does not mean other-regarding preferences are irrelevant; rather, they are relegated to the task of determining the weights $w_i$.

## Aggregating Interpersonal Comparisons

While Theorem 1 establishes the form of the social welfare function, it does not specify how the weights $w_i$ should be chosen. To address this, Mori proposes that society should derive these weights from the other-regarding preferences expressed by the individuals themselves.

This involves measuring how much one person values the utility gains of another. Suppose individual $i$ is asked to compare a utility gain for individual $j$ against a utility gain for individual $k$. We can define a "rate of interpersonal substitution" $\mu_i^{j,k}$, which represents the ratio of utility individual $i$ assigns to $j$ relative to $k$.

![figure-2-aggregating-diverse-interpersonal-preferences-into-a-single-social-preference-the-figure-shows-how-individual-weight-ratios-represented-by-the-bars-are-processed-by-the-social-welfare-function-phi-to-produce-a-societal-weight-ratio](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.25904v1/img-1.jpeg)

Mori introduces several axioms to govern how these individual comparisons should be aggregated:
1.  **Representative Consistency:** Aggregating in stages (e.g., within groups first, then across groups) should yield the same result as aggregating all at once.
2.  **Homogeneity:** If every individual doubles their valuation of $j$ relative to $k$, the social valuation should also double.
3.  **Anonymity:** The identity of the "voter" making the comparison should not matter.
4.  **Faithfulness:** If everyone agrees on a comparison, society should adopt it.

Theorem 2 proves that if these axioms are satisfied, the only way to aggregate these comparisons is through a geometric mean. This leads to "geometric self-regarding utilitarianism," where the ratio of social weights for any two individuals $j$ and $k$ is given by:

$$
\frac{w_j}{w_k} = \left( \prod_{i=1}^n \mu_i^{j,k} \right)^{\frac{1}{n}}
$$

This approach allows society to use the altruistic and paternalistic concerns of its citizens to calibrate how much weight each person’s personal well-being should receive in the collective decision-making process.

## Constraints and Feasibility

In many practical scenarios, not all combinations of individual outcomes are possible. For example, in a house allocation problem, two people cannot live in the same single-family home. This means the set of social outcomes is a restricted subset $Y \subset X$.

The paper extends the analysis to these constrained settings. Theorem 3 shows that the general form of self-regarding utilitarianism still holds for finite sets of feasible outcomes. However, the strength of individual sovereignty depends on the "connectedness" of the outcome space. If the possible outcomes are highly disconnected, the individual sovereignty axiom provides less information to society about how to weigh different outcomes. This is because society can only defer to an individual's choice if there are "sovereign" pairs of outcomes available—outcomes where only that person's state changes. If such pairs do not exist due to constraints, the principle of sovereignty becomes silent, giving the social planner more discretion in defining the utility scales.

## Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

The final extension of the paper addresses subjective uncertainty. In the real world, individuals rarely agree on the probabilities of future events. They have different beliefs about the likelihood of a policy's success or the risks of a particular investment.

To handle this, Mori adapts the model to a Savage-style setting where preferences are defined over "acts" (functions from states of the world to outcomes). A major challenge here is that if individuals have very different beliefs, the standard individual sovereignty axiom can lead to logical contradictions (a variation of the "spurious unanimity" problem).

To solve this, the paper proposes "restricted individual sovereignty." This modified axiom states that society only defers to an individual's preference if everyone in the society agrees on the underlying probabilities involved in the choice. Under this restriction, Theorem 4 establishes a dual result:
1.  **Utility Aggregation:** Social utility remains a weighted sum of individual self-regarding utilities.
2.  **Belief Aggregation:** Society's subjective belief $\pi_0$ is an affine combination of the individuals' beliefs:

$$
\pi_0 = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i \pi_i
$$

This result provides a consistent way for a group to make decisions even when they disagree on both their values (other-regarding preferences) and their expectations (subjective beliefs).

## Conclusion and Significance

The research presented by John Mori offers a rigorous normative framework for balancing individual rights with social concern for others. By starting from the intuitive principle of individual sovereignty, the paper derives a specific form of social welfare: self-regarding utilitarianism. 

The primary contribution of this work is the separation of preferences into two distinct roles. Other-regarding preferences—the way we care about our neighbors—should not change the *definition* of what is good for those neighbors (which is their own business). Instead, those preferences should inform the *relative importance* society places on each person's welfare. 

This differs from traditional Pareto-based approaches, which often allow one person's paternalistic meddling to directly alter the social utility assigned to another person's outcome. By characterizing geometric self-regarding utilitarianism and extending the theory to constrained environments and uncertain states, the paper provides a complete toolkit for social planners who wish to respect individual autonomy while still accounting for the interconnectedness of human values.
