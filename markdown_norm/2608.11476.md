## Scaling Intelligent Surfaces for 6G Networks

Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces (RIS) have emerged as a significant technology for future 6G wireless communication systems. By using large panels of low-cost, passive elements, an RIS can "steer" or "focus" electromagnetic waves, effectively turning the wireless environment into a programmable entity. Traditional RIS designs operate on a "diagonal" principle, where each element independently adjusts the phase of the incident signal. However, recent research has shifted toward Beyond-Diagonal RIS (BD-RIS). These architectures allow for inter-element coupling through reconfigurable impedance networks, enabling more sophisticated wave manipulation.

The transition to Extremely Large (XL) surfaces and higher frequencies (such as millimeter-wave and sub-THz) brings a new challenge: near-field communications. When the surface is large compared to the wavelength, users and base stations often operate within the Rayleigh distance, where the wavefronts are spherical rather than planar.

While BD-RIS offers superior performance, the complexity of a "fully-connected" architecture—where every element is coupled to every other element—is immense. For an RIS with $M$ elements, the number of hardware entries scales as $M^2$, and the computational effort to optimize them scales as $O(M^3)$. For an XL surface with hundreds or thousands of elements, this complexity becomes a bottleneck. This paper proposes a geometric modal framework that addresses this scaling problem by exploiting the physics of the near-field environment.

## The Physics of the Near-Field and BD-RIS

In the radiative near field, the traditional far-field assumption of plane waves breaks down. The propagation is instead described by the free-space scalar Green function:

$$G(x, y) = \frac{e^{-j\kappa_0\|x-y\|}}{\|x-y\|}$$

This model accounts for both the spherical curvature of the phase and the decay in amplitude, which vary significantly across a large aperture. This curvature allows for "beam focusing," where the RIS concentrates energy at a specific point in 3D space, rather than just a specific direction.

BD-RIS architectures exploit this by allowing signals to "hop" between elements before being reflected. In a fully-connected BD-RIS, the scattering response is represented by a unitary and symmetric matrix $\mathbf{\Theta} \in \mathbb{C}^{M \times M}$. While this matrix provides the maximum degrees of freedom for wave manipulation, optimizing all $M^2$ entries is prohibitive for XL surfaces. 

Current alternatives, such as "group-connected" BD-RIS, reduce complexity by only allowing coupling within small groups of elements. However, these lack optimality guarantees. Furthermore, standard dimensionality reduction techniques like the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) beamspace—popular in far-field massive MIMO—perform poorly in the near field because a single spherical wavefront spreads across many planar-wave (DFT) beams.

## The Geometric Modal Framework

The core contribution of this work is the realization that the electromagnetic field on an XL surface, despite having many physical elements, actually resides in a low-dimensional "active subspace." This subspace is defined by the geometry of the terminals (the base station and the users).

### Modal Representation
Instead of optimizing $M$ individual elements, the authors project the incident and radiated fields onto a set of $L$ orthonormal basis functions, or "modes" $\{\phi_l\}$. This results in a compact $L \times L$ modal scattering matrix $\Psi$. The physical element-domain matrix $\mathbf{\Theta}$ is then related to this modal matrix through a compression matrix $\mathbf{\Phi} \in \mathbb{C}^{M \times L}$:

$$\mathbf{\Psi} = \mathbf{\Phi}^H \mathbf{\Theta} \mathbf{\Phi}$$

The matrix $\mathbf{\Phi}$ is formed by sampling the continuous modes at the $M$ element positions. To ensure the physical RIS remains passive and lossless (unitary), the authors propose a "lossless completion" strategy:

$$\mathbf{\Theta} = \mathbf{\Phi} \mathbf{\Psi} \mathbf{\Phi}^H + (\mathbf{I}_M - \mathbf{\Phi} \mathbf{\Phi}^H)$$

This construction ensures that $\mathbf{\Theta}$ is unitary for any unitary $\mathbf{\Psi} \in \mathcal{U}(L)$. It effectively means the RIS shapes the signal within the modal subspace while reflecting the "rest" of the energy unchanged.

### Constructing the Geometric Basis
The "geometric" part of the framework comes from how the basis $\mathbf{\Phi}$ is chosen. The authors identify "spherical-wave atoms"—vectors representing the aperture's response to point sources at the base station and user locations. The active subspace $\mathcal{S}$ is the span of these atoms:

$$\mathcal{S} = \text{span}([\mathbf{G}, \mathbf{F}^*])$$

The dimension of this subspace, $\rho$, is limited by the number of terminals ($N+K$), where $N$ is the number of base station antennas and $K$ is the number of users. Crucially, $\rho$ does not depend on the physical size of the RIS panel $M$. The basis $\mathbf{\Phi}$ is then constructed using a Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of these atoms.

## Proving M-Independent Optimality

A significant theoretical result of this paper is the proof that this low-rank approach does not lose any performance compared to a full element-by-element optimization. Specifically, if the basis $\mathbf{\Phi}$ encompasses the active subspace and has a dimension $L=2\rho$, then maximizing the sum rate over the $L \times L$ modal matrix $\mathbf{\Psi}$ achieves the exact same result as maximizing over the full $M \times M$ matrix $\mathbf{\Theta}$.

This "M-free equivalence" means that for an XL surface, we only need to reconfigure $L^2$ parameters, where $L \leq 2(N+K)$. For a typical setup with 4 users and an 8-antenna base station, $L$ might be around 14. For a $24 \times 24$ RIS panel ($M=576$), the modal approach requires only $14^2 = 196$ reconfigurable entries, whereas a full element-domain design would require $576^2 \approx 331,776$ entries. This is a reduction in hardware and control complexity by a factor of over 1,700.

## Low-Complexity Optimization Algorithm

To maximize the sum rate, the authors develop an Alternating Optimization (AO) algorithm based on the Weighted Minimum Mean Squared Error (WMMSE) framework. The algorithm alternates between updating the base station beamformers $\mathbf{W}$ and the modal matrix $\mathbf{\Psi}$.

The update for $\mathbf{\Psi}$ is performed on the unitary manifold $\mathcal{U}(L)$ using Riemannian steepest descent. The update rule follows the geodesic on the manifold:

$$\mathbf{\Psi}^{(i+1)} = \exp(-\eta \mathbf{\Xi}) \mathbf{\Psi}^{(i)}$$

where $\mathbf{\Xi}$ is the Riemannian gradient and $\eta$ is the step size. Because the dimensions of $\mathbf{\Psi}$ are small ($L \times L$), each iteration of the algorithm is extremely fast. The computational complexity per iteration is $O(N^3 + L^3 + I_\eta K^2 L)$, which is completely independent of the number of elements $M$. The only step that depends on $M$ is the initial one-time construction of the basis, which costs $O(M(N+K)^2)$.

## Key Results and Performance Comparisons

The proposed framework was evaluated through extensive simulations, comparing the geometric modal approach against DFT beamspace, group-connected BD-RIS, and traditional diagonal RIS.

### Performance vs. Entry Count
The simulations demonstrate that the modal framework achieves the maximum possible sum rate (the fully-connected optimum) using the predicted $L=2\rho$ dimension.

![sum-rate-vs-entries](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11476v1/sumratevsentries.png)
*{fig:sum-rate-vs-entries}: Sum rate versus the number of reconfigurable entries for different architectures. The blue circles represent the modal framework, which reaches the fully-connected performance (star) with orders of magnitude fewer entries than other methods.*

In contrast, the DFT beamspace approach (orange triangles) requires significantly more entries to reach similar performance because its planar-wave basis is mismatched to the near-field spherical waves. Group-connected RIS (green squares) also performs worse for a given number of entries.

### Range Resolvability
One of the unique advantages of the near field is the ability to resolve users based on their distance (range), not just their angle. The modal framework excels at this, showing high sum rates even when two users are located at the exact same azimuth but different distances. 

![range-resolve](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11476v1/resolve.png)
*{fig:range-resolve}: Sum rate as a function of range separation between two users. The modal framework transitions to higher rates much earlier than diagonal or DFT-based surfaces, aligning with the theoretical depth-of-focus limit $2r_0^2/d_F$.*

### Impact of Near-Field Depth
The study also investigated how the required dimension $L$ changes as the base station moves further away (reducing the "depth" of the near-field). 

![bs-depth](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11476v1/dbs.png)
*{fig:bs-depth}: Analysis of the dimensioning rule $L=2\rho$ and sum rate as the base station (BS) distance varies relative to the Rayleigh distance $d_F$.*

As shown in {fig:bs-depth}, the optimal modal dimension $r_G$ (and thus $L=16$ to $L=10$) automatically adapts to the environment. Furthermore, the performance gap between the fully-connected optimum and simple diagonal RIS grows as we move deeper into the near field. This suggests that the complex wave-shaping enabled by beyond-diagonal coupling is most valuable in the near-field regime.

## Conclusion: A Paradigm Shift for XL-RIS

The research presented provides a scalable path for implementing high-performance BD-RIS on extremely large surfaces. By identifying that near-field aperture fields are inherently low-rank, the authors have shown that the "prohibitive" complexity of fully-connected surfaces is actually an artifact of an inefficient element-wise representation.

By switching to a geometric modal basis:
1.  **Complexity is decoupled from size:** The number of tunable components depends on the number of users, not the number of physical elements on the panel.
2.  **Performance is preserved:** The framework is provably optimal, reaching the same performance as a fully-connected element-domain design.
3.  **Near-field features are exploited:** The basis naturally handles spherical wavefronts, enabling superior beam focusing and range-based user separation.

This framework shifts the focus of RIS design from physical element control to geometric mode control, providing a foundational tool for the deployment of intelligent surfaces in 6G networks.
