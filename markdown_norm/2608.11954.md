## Causal Inference for Group-Contaminated Structured Outcomes

In many scientific experiments, the outcomes of interest are not simple numbers but complex, structured objects. Examples include high-resolution microscopy images of cells, the three-dimensional shapes of proteins, or the connectivity patterns of neural networks. A significant challenge in analyzing such data is that the observed output often includes "nuisance" variability—transformations that occur during the data collection process rather than from the biological or experimental treatment itself. 

For instance, in automated microscopy, a cell culture might be slightly rotated or shifted depending on how the plate was placed in the imaging machine. These geometric transformations (translation, rotation, or reflection) can obscure the actual treatment effect. If researchers analyze the raw pixel coordinates, they might mistakenly attribute a difference in cell position to a biological response. This paper addresses this problem by developing a rigorous mathematical framework for causal inference on "group-contaminated" structured outcomes.

## The Challenge of Group Contamination

The core of the problem lies in the relationship between the intrinsic outcome and the observed data. Let $Y(A)$ represent the intrinsic, "true" structured outcome under treatment $A$. In an ideal world, we would observe $Y(A)$ directly. However, in practice, we observe a transformed version:

$$X = \Gamma \cdot Y(A)$$

In this model, $\Gamma$ represents an unobserved element of a group (such as the group of all possible rotations and translations). The key difficulty is that $\Gamma$ is unit-specific and potentially "contaminated." Unlike simpler models where noise is assumed to be independent of the treatment, here the transformation $\Gamma$ could depend on the treatment $A$, the covariates $C$, or even the intrinsic outcome $Y(A)$ itself. This dependency makes it impossible to simply "average out" the noise without a more sophisticated approach.

Existing methods often rely on "registration"—aligning images to a common template—or extracting specific features like the total number of cells. While useful, these approaches can be ad-hoc or may discard important information. The authors of this paper seek to determine exactly what information can be recovered from the observed data $X$ and when that information is sufficient for making causal conclusions.

## Information Boundaries and Causal Identification

The first major contribution of the paper is characterizing the "information boundary." If the transformation $\Gamma$ is completely unknown, what aspects of the original outcome $Y$ can we actually see? The authors prove that the only information uniformly recoverable from $X$ are the "invariant" properties of $Y$. 

An invariant property is one that does not change when the group action is applied. For example, the total intensity of a fluorescent signal in an image remains the same regardless of whether the image is rotated. Formally, a target function $\tau: Y \to S$ is observable if and only if it is G-invariant, meaning $\tau(g \cdot y) = \tau(y)$ for every group element $g$. 

To capture all possible invariant information, the authors use the concept of a "maximal invariant" $M$. A map $M$ is a maximal invariant if it maps two outcomes to the same value if and only if they are related by a transformation in the group $G$. Mathematically:

$$M(y) = M(z) \iff \exists g \in G: z = g \cdot y$$

By applying this maximal invariant to our observation $X$, we obtain the "quotient" $Q = M(X)$. Because $M$ is invariant to the group action, we have:

$$Q = M(\Gamma \cdot Y(A)) = M(Y(A)) = Q(A)$$

This is a powerful result: it shows that the quotient $Q$ is identical to the invariant properties of the intrinsic outcome $Q(A)$, regardless of the nuisance transformation $\Gamma$. Under standard causal assumptions—such as exchangeability ($Q(a) \perp \!\!\! \perp A | C$) and positivity ($P(A=a | C) > 0$)—the interventional distribution of these invariant features can be identified and analyzed using standard statistical adjustment techniques.

## Statistical Losslessness: The Blackwell Boundary

While it is clear that invariant features are observable, a deeper question remains: does reducing the data to these features result in a loss of statistical power or information? In other words, is the reduction "lossless"?

The paper addresses this using the framework of Blackwell equivalence from statistical decision theory. This involves comparing two "experiments": one where we see the full data $(C, A, X)$ and one where we only see the quotient data $(C, A, M(X))$. The authors establish a "sharp sufficiency boundary." They prove that reducing to the quotient is lossless if and only if there exists a parameter-free way to reconstruct the full data distribution from the quotient distribution. 

One specific case where this losslessness occurs is "conditional-Haar" contamination. If the nuisance transformation $\Gamma$ is effectively "randomly" chosen from the group (according to a specific measure called the Haar measure), then all the useful information about the treatment effect is contained in the invariants. In such cases, the specific orientation or position of the structured outcome provides no additional information about the underlying causal process.

## Multi-Component Structures and Group Actions

Many structured outcomes are composed of multiple parts, such as a well in a laboratory plate containing images from two different "sites." The paper highlights a critical distinction in how these components might be transformed:

1.  **Product Actions**: Each site is transformed independently (e.g., site 1 is rotated $30^\circ$ and site 2 is rotated $90^\circ$).
2.  **Diagonal Actions**: All sites are transformed by the same group element (e.g., the entire well is rotated by $45^\circ$).

If the transformations are independent, one can safely reduce each site to its own invariant representation. However, if the transformations are shared (diagonal), the relative orientation between the sites might contain valuable information. The authors show that a component-wise reduction $M^{\times J}(y_1, \dots, y_J) = (M(y_1), \dots, M(y_J))$ is only maximal under product actions. If a diagonal action is present, this simple reduction might discard the "cross-site" information, potentially weakening the analysis.

## Practical Implementation for Microscopy

To demonstrate the practical utility of this framework, the authors focus on microscopy images defined on a discrete grid (a lattice). They define the group of "lattice rigid motions" $Z^2 \rtimes C_4$, which includes translations along the grid and rotations by 90-degree increments.

They construct a specific "canonicalization map" $c(x)$ to serve as a maximal invariant. This map works by:
1.  Shifting the image so its bounding box starts at the origin (removing translation).
2.  Generating all four possible 90-degree rotations.
3.  Selecting the rotation that is "lexicographically smallest" (comparing pixel values like words in a dictionary).

Once the images are canonicalized, the authors use a "characteristic kernel" $k_{\sigma}$ to compare distributions of images. This allows them to perform a non-parametric statistical test called the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) test. 

To ensure the results are robust for small sample sizes, they implement an exact randomization test. By randomly swapping the treatment labels within paired blocks of experiments and recalculating the MMD statistic, they can calculate a p-value that is valid even if the underlying distribution of the images is unknown. The test rejects the null hypothesis if the observed p-value satisfies $p(Z) \leq \alpha$.

## Empirical Validation and Robustness

The authors validated their approach using both simulations and a real-world dataset called RxRx1, which contains high-resolution images of human cells treated with different genetic interventions (siRNAs).

### Simulation Results
In simulations, the authors compared their "exact quotient" method against a "raw pixel" analysis. They found that if the nuisance transformation $\Gamma$ is informative (e.g., if treated images are always rotated differently than control images), the raw pixel analysis fails catastrophically, showing a significant effect even when none exists. In contrast, the exact quotient method maintained the correct error rate and showed high power to detect real biological changes.

### Real-World Case Study (RxRx1)
Using the RxRx1 dataset, the authors created an "auditable" stress test. They deliberately applied treatment-dependent rotations and translations to the cell images. Despite this severe contamination, the exact quotient method successfully identified the correct biological effects. 

The paper also explores "approximate contamination." In reality, transformations might not be perfect group actions—there might be slight rounding errors or small unintended rotations. The authors provide a stability result showing that the error in the statistical results is bounded by the distance from the ideal group action. Specifically, they show that the error in the MMD statistic is bounded by terms like $L_k L_M (\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_0)$, where $L_k$ and $L_M$ are constants related to the kernel and the invariant map, and $\varepsilon$ represents the degree of deviation from the group action.

## Significance of the Work

This research provides a rigorous foundation for causal inference in the era of high-dimensional, structured data. By moving away from heuristic registration and towards a formal group-theoretic framework, it offers several advantages:

*   **Reliability**: It identifies exactly which inferences are "safe" to make despite unobserved transformations.
*   **Efficiency**: It defines the conditions under which reducing data to invariants is statistically lossless, ensuring researchers don't discard useful signal.
*   **Practicality**: It provides a concrete workflow for imaging data, including canonicalization and exact testing, which is particularly relevant for fields like drug discovery and cell biology.

By clarifying the boundaries of what can be observed and how to test it, the paper enables more robust and reproducible scientific conclusions from complex structured observations.
