Generative modeling seeks to construct a bridge between a known, simple base distribution and a complex, unknown target distribution, typically represented by a dataset. In recent years, continuous-time generative models have become a standard approach to this problem. These models, which include Score-based Generative Models (SGMs) and various Flow Matching (FM) frameworks, define the transformation of data through differential equations.

Diffusion Flow Matching (DFM) is a specific instance of this paradigm that allows for stochasticity—or randomness—during the generation process. While DFM has shown empirical success, providing rigorous mathematical guarantees for its performance has been a challenge. This paper addresses these theoretical gaps by refining the convergence bounds for DFM in two key metrics: Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence and the 2-Wasserstein ($W_2$) distance. By improving the dimensional scaling of these bounds and introducing comprehensive guarantees for $W_2$ distance, the authors provide a more robust theoretical foundation for DFM in high-dimensional settings.

## The Principles of Diffusion Flow Matching

At its core, DFM defines a path between a base distribution $\mu$ and a target distribution $\nu^*$ over a time interval, usually normalized to $[0, 1]$. To do this, it uses a "stochastic interpolant" $X^I_t$. This interpolant is constructed by coupling samples from the base and target distributions and adding a Brownian bridge—a type of stochastic process that starts and ends at specific points—to create a smooth transition.

However, the interpolant $X^I_t$ is generally non-Markovian, meaning its future state depends on its entire history, not just its current position. This makes it difficult to use directly as a generative model. To solve this, DFM uses a "Markovian projection" $X^M_t$. This projection is a diffusion process that possesses the same marginal distributions as the interpolant at every time step $t$ but follows a standard Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE):

$$
dX^M_t = \tilde{\beta}_t(X^M_t)dt + \sqrt{2}dW_t
$$

In this equation, $\tilde{\beta}_t(x)$ is known as the "mimicking drift." It is defined as the conditional expectation of the velocity of the interpolant given its current state:

$$
\tilde{\beta}_t(x) = \mathbb{E}[ \dot{X}^I_t | X^I_t = x]
$$

In practice, this mimicking drift is intractable because we do not have perfect knowledge of the target distribution. Instead, it is approximated using a neural network $s_{\theta^*}(t, x)$, which is trained to minimize the difference between its output and the true drift. Once the network is trained, samples are generated by simulating the learned SDE:

$$
dX^{\theta^*}_t = s_{\theta^*}(t, X^{\theta^*}_t)dt + \sqrt{2}dW_t
$$

Because we cannot solve this SDE exactly in continuous time, we use a numerical discretization method, such as the Euler–Maruyama scheme, which moves in discrete steps of size $h$.

## Refining Kullback–Leibler Convergence Bounds

The Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence is a standard statistical measure used to quantify how much one probability distribution differs from a reference distribution. In generative modeling, we want the KL divergence between the target distribution $\nu^*$ and the generated distribution $\nu^{\theta^*}_1$ to be as small as possible.

Prior research into DFM established that the KL divergence was sensitive to the dimension $d$ of the data, with errors scaling at a rate of $O(d^4)$. This paper improves this scaling to $O(d^3)$ under minimal conditions. The authors achieve this by reconsidering how the error terms involving the mimicking drift are bounded. Previous methods involved expanding terms that required calculating three derivatives of the log-density. By using an integration-by-parts argument, the authors effectively "transfer" one of these derivatives to the coupling, reducing the required order of differentiation. This leads to a tighter bound:

$$
KL(\nu^*|\nu^{\theta^*}_1) \leq C(\epsilon^2 + h(h^{1/8} + 1) ( d^2 + \|\nabla \log \pi\|_{L_8(\pi)}^4 ) d)
$$

Here, $\epsilon^2$ represents the $L_2$ approximation error of the neural network, $h$ is the discretization step size, and $\nabla \log \pi$ is the score function of the coupling between the base and target distributions. This result shows that the discretization error depends linearly on the step size $O(h)$, which is standard for these types of solvers, but the dimensional dependence is significantly reduced.

The paper also addresses the "early stopping" regime. In many practical scenarios, the SDE becomes unstable as it approaches the final time $t=1$. By stopping the process at $1-\delta$ for some small $\delta$, these instabilities can be avoided. The authors demonstrate that even with early stopping, the $O(d^3)$ scaling holds, and they relax the assumptions required on the target distribution, making the theory applicable to a wider range of datasets.

## Establishing Wasserstein-2 Guarantees

While KL divergence is mathematically convenient, the 2-Wasserstein ($W_2$) distance is often considered more representative of "visual" or "structural" quality in generated samples. The $W_2$ distance measures the minimum "cost" to transport one distribution to another.

Providing $W_2$ bounds is technically more demanding because it requires understanding how errors propagate and accumulate over the entire trajectory of the SDE. The authors establish these guarantees under the assumption of "weak log-concavity" of the coupling. This is a generalized convexity condition that allows for more complex, multi-modal distributions than standard strong log-concavity, yet still provides enough structure to control the Lipschitz continuity (the "smoothness") of the mimicking drift.

The derived $W_2$ bound for the generated samples is:

$$
W_2(\nu^*, \nu^{\theta^*}_1) \leq C \left[ \epsilon + \sqrt{h}(h^{1/16} + 1) \sqrt{ ( d^2 + \|\nabla \log \pi\|_{L_8(\pi)}^4 ) d } \right]
$$

This result indicates that the $W_2$ error scales with $O(\sqrt{h})$ in terms of the step size and $O(\sqrt{d^3})$ in terms of the dimension. This is the first comprehensive $W_2$ guarantee for DFM that accounts for both the neural network approximation error and the discretization error without imposing overly restrictive assumptions on the learned drift.

To reach this conclusion, the authors performed a detailed Jacobian analysis of the mimicking drift. They proved that if the underlying coupling is sufficiently regular (i.e., its score $\nabla \log \pi$ and the Jacobian of its score $\nabla^2 \log \pi$ are integrable), then the drift itself remains smooth enough to prevent the generative process from diverging. This connects the geometric properties of the data distribution directly to the convergence of the generative model.

## Discretization and Computational Efficiency

A major contribution of this work is the analysis of how the choice of time-stepping affects the overall complexity of the model. In standard DFM implementations using a constant step size $h$, achieving a low error in the early-stopping regime can be computationally expensive.

The authors propose an adaptive step-size schedule. In this schedule, the step size remains constant at the beginning but becomes proportional to the distance from the boundaries (specifically $\min\{t_k, 1-t_k\}$) as the process progresses. By applying this schedule, the authors show a dramatic improvement in computational complexity.

Specifically, if one wishes to achieve a squared error of $O(\epsilon^2)$ in the Fortet–Mourier metric (a variant related to $W_2$), a constant step-size schedule requires a complexity of $O(\epsilon^{-10})$. In contrast, the proposed adaptive schedule reduces this complexity to $O(\epsilon^{-2})$. This suggests that practitioners can achieve high-fidelity samples much faster by simply adjusting how they discretize the time interval during the generation process.

## Significance of the Findings

The theoretical refinements presented in this paper clarify several aspects of Diffusion Flow Matching. First, the reduction from $O(d^4)$ to $O(d^3)$ in KL bounds suggests that DFM is more robust to high-dimensional data than previously estimated. This is particularly relevant for applications like high-resolution image synthesis or molecular modeling, where the dimension $d$ is very large.

Second, the $W_2$ guarantees provide a rigorous justification for using DFM in tasks where the geometry of the distribution matters. By operating under the assumption of weak log-concavity, the authors ensure that their results apply to a broad class of realistic distributions, moving beyond the Gaussian or highly smooth priors often required in earlier theoretical works.

Finally, the study of discretization errors and adaptive step-sizes bridges the gap between theory and practice. It provides a clear mathematical reason why certain sampling schedules perform better and offers a roadmap for optimizing the inference speed of DFM models without sacrificing accuracy. This work sets a new benchmark for the non-asymptotic analysis of flow-based generative models, ensuring that as these models become more prevalent in industry, their behavior can be predicted and understood with greater precision.
