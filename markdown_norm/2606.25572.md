## Introduction to AFDM and ISAC Systems

Integrated Sensing and Communication (ISAC) has emerged as a fundamental pillar for future wireless networks, particularly for applications like autonomous driving, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), and smart industrial automation. These scenarios require high-speed data transmission and high-precision environmental sensing to coexist within the same frequency spectrum and hardware architecture. A significant challenge in these environments is the presence of high mobility, which results in doubly dispersive channels—radio environments characterized by both time-varying delays (due to multi-path propagation) and Doppler shifts (due to the relative motion between transmitters and receivers).

Traditional waveforms, such as Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing (OFDM), often struggle in these high-mobility scenarios because Doppler shifts destroy the orthogonality between subcarriers, leading to inter-carrier interference. To address this, recent research has explored chirp-based waveforms. Affine Frequency Division Multiplexing (AFDM) is a promising candidate that operates in the discrete affine Fourier transform (DAFT) domain. By using chirp-based subcarriers, AFDM can effectively represent doubly dispersive channels and achieve full diversity, making it robust against both frequency-selective and time-selective fading.

In an AFDM-ISAC system, the goal is to estimate channel parameters like complex gains, delays, and Doppler shifts for both communication (channel estimation) and sensing (target detection). While the Cramér-Rao Bound (CRB) is the standard tool for assessing the theoretical limit of estimation accuracy, standard CRB analyses often rely on idealized assumptions: that all transmitted data is known and that the receiver’s signal model perfectly matches the physical reality. This paper addresses the practical reality where these assumptions fail, providing a rigorous analysis of estimation performance under model misspecification.

## The AFDM Signal Model in ISAC

The AFDM system utilizes the DAFT domain to transform symbols into a chirp-based multi-carrier signal. Let $N$ be the number of subcarriers. The input symbols are mapped via an Inverse DAFT (IDAFT) to generate the time-domain signal. After propagation through a channel with $P$ propagation paths or targets, the received signal is demodulated using a DAFT. The resulting observation signal in the DAFT domain can be represented as:

$$
y = H_{\text{eff}}x + \tilde{w} \in \mathbb{C}^{N \times 1}
$$

where $H_{\text{eff}}$ is the effective channel matrix incorporating delay and Doppler effects, $x$ is the transmitted symbol vector, and $\tilde{w}$ is the additive white Gaussian noise. 

In a practical ISAC system, the transmitted vector $x = x_p + x_d$ consists of a known pilot sequence $x_p$ and an unknown data sequence $x_d$. For sensing or channel estimation, the parameters of interest for the $i$-th target are grouped into a vector $\theta_i = [\Re(h_i), \Im(h_i), k_i, l_i]^T$, where $h_i$ is the complex gain, $k_i$ is the normalized Doppler shift, and $l_i$ is the normalized integer delay. The set of all parameters for all targets is denoted as $\bar{\theta}$.

## Addressing the Impact of Unknown Data

A common simplification in performance analysis is to assume that the entire signal $x$ is known at the receiver. However, since the data symbols $x_d$ are random and unknown during the sensing phase, they act as an additional source of interference. This paper refines the estimation benchmark by treating $x_d$ as a zero-mean random variable. 

Under this more realistic model, the true probability density function (PDF) of the observation $y$ remains complex Gaussian, $p_Y(y; \bar{\theta}) = \mathcal{CN}(\mu(\bar{\theta}), C(\bar{\theta}))$. However, the key difference lies in the mean and covariance:
- The mean $\mu(\bar{\theta})$ depends only on the pilot $x_p$.
- The covariance $C(\bar{\theta})$ now includes both the noise power and a parameter-dependent data-induced term $H_{\text{eff}} \text{Cov}[x_d] H_{\text{eff}}^H$.

This parameter-dependent covariance makes the Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) more complex, as it must account for how changes in parameters (like Doppler or delay) affect the interference structure of the unknown data. The paper derives a generalized CRB that accounts for this data-induced uncertainty, showing that even with infinite signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), estimation performance is ultimately capped by the interference from unknown data symbols.

## The Misspecified Cramér-Rao Bound (MCRB)

In many practical scenarios, the estimator used at the receiver does not perfectly match the true physical model of the signal. This is known as model misspecification. For example, an estimator might assume the noise is simple white noise, ignoring the structured interference from data, or it might estimate targets one-by-one rather than jointly.

When the assumed PDF $f_Y(y|\theta) = \mathcal{CN}(\mu_a(\theta), C_a(\theta))$ differs from the true PDF $p_Y(y; \bar{\theta})$, the standard CRB is no longer a valid lower bound. Instead, the estimator converges to a "pseudotrue" parameter $\tilde{\theta}$, which is the value that minimizes the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the true and assumed distributions:

$$
\tilde{\theta} = \arg \min_{\theta} D_{\text{KL}}(p_Y(y; \bar{\theta}) || f_Y(y|\theta))
$$

The performance of such an estimator is characterized by the Misspecified CRB (MCRB). The MCRB provides the lower bound on the variance of any unbiased misspecified estimator and is defined as:

$$
\text{MCRB}(\tilde{\theta}) = J_{\tilde{\theta}}^{-1} K_{\tilde{\theta}} J_{\tilde{\theta}}^{-1}
$$

where $J_{\tilde{\theta}}$ and $K_{\tilde{\theta}}$ are matrices related to the curvature and gradient of the assumed log-likelihood function, respectively:

$$
J_{\tilde{\theta}} = -E_{p} \left[ \nabla_{\theta}^2 \ln f_Y(y|\theta) |_{\theta=\tilde{\theta}} \right]
$$

$$
K_{\tilde{\theta}} = E_{p} \left[ \nabla_{\theta} \ln f_Y(y|\theta) \nabla_{\theta} \ln f_Y(y|\theta)^H |_{\theta=\tilde{\theta}} \right]
$$

## Analysis of Practical Misspecification Cases

The authors identify two critical cases of misspecification in AFDM-ISAC systems.

### Case 1: Covariance Mismatch due to Data Leakage
This occurs when the guard intervals between pilots and data are insufficient, causing the pilot symbols to be contaminated by surrounding data. A typical receiver might use a matched filter that assumes a simple identity covariance matrix (white noise), ignoring the structured interference from the data. The paper proves that while this mismatch does not introduce a bias (the pseudotrue parameter $\tilde{\theta}$ remains equal to the true parameter $\bar{\theta}$), it significantly increases the variance of the estimate compared to a perfectly matched receiver.

### Case 2: Sequential Single-Target Estimation
In environments with multiple targets, a joint estimation of all parameters is computationally expensive. Instead, systems often use sequential estimation, where targets are identified and "cancelled" one-by-one. This leads to both mean and covariance mismatch because the signal model for the $i$-th target ignores the presence of yet-to-be-estimated targets. 

Unlike Case 1, this mismatch introduces a fundamental estimation bias. The pseudotrue parameter $\tilde{\theta}$ shifts away from the true value $\bar{\theta}$ due to the interference from other targets. To capture the total error, the paper utilizes a Mean Squared Error (MSE) lower bound (LB) that combines the variance (MCRB) and the squared bias:

$$
\text{LB}(\bar{\theta}) = \text{MCRB}(\tilde{\theta}) + (\bar{\theta} - \tilde{\theta})(\bar{\theta} - \tilde{\theta})^T
$$

## Key Findings and Design Insights

The research provides several insights through comprehensive simulations and analytical derivations.

### The Floor Effect of Unknown Data
As shown in {fig:matched-model-validation}, the CRB for unknown data symbols (blue line) and the corresponding MSE (yellow dashed line) reach a plateau at high SNRs. This demonstrates that unknown data acts as a persistent source of interference that limits sensing precision, regardless of how much transmit power is used.

### Bias vs. Variance in Sequential Estimation
A critical finding is that in sequential estimation (Case 2), the total error is often dominated by bias rather than variance. ### Pilot Configuration and "Smooth" Performance Regions
The authors observed that the MSE lower bound for sequential estimation exhibits sharp peaks at certain pilot lengths. These peaks correspond to pilot-dependent sidelobes in the signal's autocorrelation function. When a sidelobe is strong, the estimator might "lock onto" a peak that is slightly offset from the true parameter, causing a spike in bias. This suggests that system designers should select pilot lengths within the "smooth" regions of the LB curve to ensure stable sensing performance.

### The Influence of Weak Paths
The study found that weak propagation paths (those with low power relative to the main path) have a disproportionate impact on Doppler and delay estimation. Removing these paths from the analysis significantly lowered the estimation bounds. This implies that in complex multipath environments, the interference from minor reflections is a major contributor to the overall sensing uncertainty and bias.

## Conclusion

This work provides a rigorous analytical framework for understanding the limits of parameter estimation in AFDM-ISAC systems. By moving beyond the idealized assumptions of perfectly known signals and matched models, the authors highlight the crucial role of data-induced interference and estimation bias. The derivation of the MCRB and the total MSE lower bound offers a more realistic benchmark for system performance, especially in practical sequential estimation scenarios. These insights are vital for the robust design of next-generation wireless systems that must sense and communicate simultaneously in challenging, high-mobility environments.
