## Understanding Inference with Incomplete Information

Modern empirical research often involves combining data from multiple sources to estimate structural parameters. For example, a researcher might use price data from one survey and consumption data from another to calibrate a macroeconomic model. While this "data combination" approach is powerful, it introduces a significant challenge for statistical inference: how do we calculate the standard errors of our estimates when the correlations between the different datasets are unknown?

Standard errors are the primary metric for quantifying the precision of an estimate. If we estimate a parameter using moments (statistical summaries) from two independent samples, the covariance between those moments is zero. However, in many practical settings, samples are not perfectly independent—they might overlap in time, geography, or subject matter. If this cross-sample covariance is unknown and cannot be estimated, traditional formulas for standard errors fail.

The research presented in "Bounds for Standard Errors in Combined Data" addresses this problem by providing a systematic way to bound standard errors. Instead of providing a single, potentially misleading number based on an assumption of independence, the authors develop methods to identify the "best-case" and "worst-case" standard errors consistent with the available data. This approach allows researchers to determine whether their findings are robust to any possible correlation structure between their data sources.

## The Theoretical Framework for Combined Data

The paper considers a scenario where a vector of structural parameters $\theta$ is estimated by matching a set of empirical moments. These moments, denoted as $\hat{\mu}$, are estimates of true population reduced-form parameters $\mu$. The link between the structural parameters and the moments is defined by a known function $\mu = h(\theta)$.

In most estimation procedures, such as the Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) or indirect inference, the uncertainty in the estimated structural parameters $\hat{\theta}$ depends on the uncertainty in the moments $\hat{\mu}$. Under standard regularity conditions, the sampling error in the estimate can be approximated linearly:

$$\varphi(\hat{\theta}) - \varphi(\theta_0) \approx \ell'(\hat{\mu} - \mu_0) + o_p(n^{-1/2})$$

where $\varphi(\theta)$ is the specific parameter of interest and $\ell$ is a vector of "loadings" that describe how each moment influences that parameter. If the moments follow an asymptotic normal distribution:

$$\sqrt{n}(\hat{\mu} - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, \Sigma) \text{ as } n \to \infty$$

then the asymptotic variance of the parameter estimate is given by:

$$\sigma^2 = \ell'\Sigma\ell = \sum_{j=1}^p \ell_j^2 s_j^2 + 2 \sum_{i < j} \ell_i \ell_j \Sigma_{ij}$$

Here, $s_j^2$ represents the marginal variance of the $j$-th moment (which we typically know), and $\Sigma_{ij}$ represents the covariance between moments $i$ and $j$ (which we often do not know if the moments come from different samples).

## Defining Sharp Lower and Upper Bounds

When the off-diagonal elements of the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ are unknown, the standard error $\sigma$ can take on a range of values. The paper derives "sharp" bounds for this range—sharp meaning that the bounds are the tightest possible limits given the information available.

If the researcher only knows the marginal standard deviations $s_j$ and the loadings $\ell_j$, the maximum possible value for the standard error (the "worst-case") is:

$$\sigma_{UB} = \sum_{j=1}^p |\ell_j| s_j$$

This upper bound occurs when the sampling errors across all moments are perfectly positively correlated in a way that maximizes their total variance. This result was previously established in the literature. The primary contribution of this paper is the derivation of the corresponding sharp lower bound (the "best-case"):

$$\sigma_{LB} = \max \left\{ \max_{m \in \{1, \dots, p\}} \left( |\ell_m|s_m - \sum_{j \neq m} |\ell_j|s_j \right), 0 \right\}$$

This lower bound has a geometric interpretation. Imagine the values $|\ell_j|s_j$ as the lengths of sticks. If the longest stick is shorter than the sum of all other sticks, you can arrange them to form a closed polygon, meaning the total error can "cancel out" to zero. If one stick is much longer than the others, the error cannot be fully canceled, and the best-case standard error will be strictly positive.

## Refining Bounds with Partial Information

While the "diagonal-only" bounds (using only $s_j$) provide a starting point, researchers often have additional information about the relationship between data sources. The paper demonstrates how this information can be used to tighten the bounds:

1.  **Block Independence:** If a researcher knows that moments 1 and 2 come from a dataset that is entirely independent of the dataset used for moments 3 and 4, the covariance between these two groups must be zero. This significantly narrows the range between $\sigma_{LB}$ and $\sigma_{UB}$.
2.  **Sign Restrictions:** In many economic contexts, the direction of a correlation is known even if its magnitude is not. For example, if two moments are both driven by the same underlying economic shock, they are likely to be positively correlated. Imposing a requirement that $\Sigma_{ij} \geq 0$ will typically raise the lower bound, as it limits the possibility of error cancellation.
3.  **Magnitude Bounds:** If datasets partially overlap (e.g., one covers 1990-2010 and another covers 2000-2020), a researcher can calculate a maximum possible correlation based on the extent of the overlap.

To analyze these effects, the authors express the variance using "sign-adjusted" correlations $T_{ij}$:

$$\sigma^2 = z' T z = \sum_{j=1}^p z_j^2 + 2 \sum_{i < j} T_{ij} z_i z_j$$

where $z_j = |\ell_j|s_j$ and $T_{ij} = \text{sign}(\ell_i \ell_j) \rho_{ij}$. If $T_{ij} > 0$, the moments contribute to "alignment" (increasing variance); if $T_{ij} < 0$, they contribute to "cancellation" (decreasing variance).

## Computation via Semidefinite Programming

When the constraints on the covariance matrix $\Sigma$ become complex, analytical formulas may no longer exist. To solve this, the paper frames the search for bounds as a numerical optimization problem. Finding the best-case standard error is equivalent to finding a correlation matrix $\Omega$ that minimizes the total variance while remaining "valid."

A valid correlation matrix must be symmetric, have ones on its diagonal, and—most importantly—be positive semidefinite (PSD). The PSD requirement ensures that the variance of any linear combination of variables is non-negative, which is a fundamental property of all real-world covariance structures. The problem is formulated as a Semidefinite Program (SDP):

$$\min_{\Omega} \hat{\ell}' \hat{D} \Omega \hat{D} \hat{\ell} \quad \text{subject to} \quad \Omega \succeq 0, \Omega_{jj} = 1, \Omega_{jk} = \hat{\rho}_{jk} \text{ for } (j, k) \in I$$

In this equation, $\hat{D}$ is a diagonal matrix of estimated marginal standard deviations, and $I$ represents the set of pairs for which we have correlation information. Because SDPs are convex optimization problems, global solutions can be found efficiently using standard numerical solvers. The paper also provides Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions to verify that the numerical output is indeed the sharp global bound.

## Empirical Applications

The authors apply these bounding methods to three distinct areas of economic research to show how the choice of covariance assumption changes the interpretation of results.

### Macroeconomic Menu Cost Models
In models of price-setting, "menu costs" represent the cost firms face when changing prices. Researchers often estimate these models by matching moments like the frequency and size of price changes. Applying the SDP approach to a model by Alvarez and Lippi (2014), the authors find that the "best-case" standard errors are much smaller than the "worst-case." However, adding just a single known correlation between the moments of price changes significantly shifts the lower bound, showing that the "best-case" scenario in the absence of information was overly optimistic.

### Heterogeneous-Agent New Keynesian (HANK) Models
HANK models are used to study how monetary policy affects households with different income levels. These models are often calibrated using impulse response functions (IRFs) from different academic papers. Because these papers use different methodologies and time periods, the correlation between their reported IRFs is unknown. The authors show that for several structural parameters in these models, the range between the lower and upper bounds for standard errors is very wide. This suggests that the standard errors reported in the literature, which often implicitly assume zero correlation or independence, may be understating the true uncertainty.

### Two-Sample Instrumental Variables (TSIV)
In microeconomics, TSIV is used when the relationship between an instrument and a treatment is measured in one sample, and the relationship between the treatment and an outcome is measured in another. Researchers traditionally assume these samples are independent. By applying their method to a study on public housing by Currie and Yelowitz (2000), the authors demonstrate that while "diagonal-only" bounds are too wide to be useful, imposing a "block-diagonal" structure (acknowledging that observations within the same study are correlated but those across studies are not) produces tight, informative bounds that validate the original study's conclusions.

## Significance of the Work

This paper provides a rigorous foundation for inference in the increasingly common scenario of combined data. Rather than forcing researchers to choose between a potentially false assumption of independence or a "worst-case" scenario that might be too pessimistic, it provides the full menu of possible standard errors. 

By characterizing the entire interval $[\sigma_{LB}, \sigma_{UB}]$, the method serves as a powerful diagnostic. If the interval is narrow, the researcher can be confident that their inference is robust to unknown correlations. If the interval is wide, it highlights a specific gap in the data—the missing covariance information—and provides a clear incentive for future research to estimate or bound those specific correlations. This shift from "point estimation" of uncertainty to "range estimation" enhances the transparency and credibility of empirical findings in economics.
