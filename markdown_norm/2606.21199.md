## System Identification and the Grey-Box Paradigm

In the study of dynamical systems, researchers often aim to uncover the mathematical rules that govern how a system changes over time. This process, known as system identification, frequently involves analyzing systems described by differential equations. Traditionally, this was approached in one of two ways. "White-box" modeling relies purely on established physical laws, such as Newton's laws of motion, to derive equations. While these models are highly interpretable, they often fail to capture complex, real-world nuances. Conversely, "black-box" modeling uses flexible machine learning techniques, such as neural networks or Gaussian Processes, to fit data without any prior physical knowledge. These are often highly accurate but offer little insight into the underlying mechanisms.

To bridge this gap, "grey-box" or physics-informed modeling has emerged. These methods combine the interpretability of physical models with the flexibility of data-driven approaches. A common challenge in this field is the "model discrepancy problem," where the known physics is incomplete. When a flexible machine learner is added to a physics-based model to account for these unknowns, it often exhibits "greedy" behavior—it attempts to explain dynamics that the physical model could have captured, thereby obscuring the physical parameters and reducing the model's overall interpretability.

## The Challenge of Partial Physics and Sparse Identification

A prominent method for system identification is Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamical Systems (SINDy). SINDy operates on the principle of sparsity, assuming that the governing equations of most physical systems consist of only a few terms. It uses a dictionary of candidate functions—such as polynomials or trigonometric functions—and performs sparse regression to select the most relevant terms.

Mathematically, if a first-order system is described by $\dot{x} = f(x)$, SINDy seeks an approximation:

$$\dot{x} = \Phi(x)^\top \theta$$

where $\Phi(x) = [\phi_1(x), \ldots, \phi_K(x)]^\top$ is the dictionary of $K$ functions and $\theta$ is a vector of sparse coefficients. However, SINDy is fundamentally limited by the completeness of its dictionary. If the underlying physics is "partial"—meaning the dictionary is missing a crucial term—SINDy may produce biased results or fail to model the system accurately.

To address this, researchers have explored additive grey-box models that incorporate a discrepancy function $\tilde{f}(x)$:

$$\dot{x} = \Phi(x)^\top \theta + \tilde{f}(x)$$

In this framework, the parametric part ($\Phi(x)^\top \theta$) handles the known or candidate physics, while the nonparametric part ($\tilde{f}(x)$), often modeled by a Gaussian Process (GP), captures the unmodeled dynamics. The risk here is that the GP might "overfit" by capturing signal that should be attributed to the dictionary terms, leading to a loss of physical meaning in the identified $\theta$ values.

## Orthogonal Discrepancy Kernels

The research introduces a mechanism to enforce a clear division of labor between the dictionary and the discrepancy model. The core principle is to constrain the discrepancy function $\tilde{f}(x)$ to be orthogonal to the functions in the dictionary $\Phi(x)$. This ensures that $\tilde{f}(x)$ only learns dynamics that cannot be explained by any combination of the dictionary terms.

The orthogonality constraint is defined as:

$$\int_X \Phi(x) \tilde{f}(x) dx = 0$$

To implement this constraint within a Gaussian Process framework, the authors introduce a specialized covariance function known as an orthogonal discrepancy kernel, denoted as $k_\perp$. If we assume a GP prior for the discrepancy $\tilde{f} \sim \text{GP}(0, k_\perp)$, the kernel is constructed by modifying a standard kernel $k(x, x')$ (such as the squared-exponential kernel):

$$k_\perp(x, x') = k(x, x') - \Phi(x)^\top H^{-1} \Phi(x')$$

In this expression, $H$ is the Gram matrix of the basis functions, defined as $H = \int_X \Phi(x)\Phi(x)^\top dx$. By subtracting the projection of the data onto the dictionary space, the kernel effectively "blinds" the Gaussian Process to any patterns already representable by the physics-informed dictionary. This prevents the "greediness" typically associated with black-box components in hybrid models.

## Experimental Evaluation: The Cubic Oscillator

To demonstrate the efficacy of this approach, the researchers applied it to an unforced oscillator with cubic nonlinearity. The system is governed by the second-order differential equation:

$$\ddot{y} + c\dot{y}^3 + ky^3 = 0$$

This provides a clear testbed because it includes specific nonlinear terms (cubic damping and a cubic spring) that can be intentionally omitted from the dictionary to simulate "partial physics." The experiments compared the proposed orthogonal kernel GP against a standard non-orthogonal GP in two primary scenarios.

### Scenario 1: Complete Physics
In this case, the dictionary $\Phi(x) = [y, \dot{y}, y^2, \dot{y}^2, y^3, \dot{y}^3]^\top$ includes all the terms necessary to describe the system. The goal is to see if the model correctly identifies the physics without the GP interfering.

![orthogonal-complete](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.21199v1/ortho.png)
*{fig:orthogonal-complete}: Results for the orthogonal kernel GP with a complete dictionary. The white-box correctly identifies both relevant cubic terms ($\theta$), and the GP discrepancy model shows a near-zero response, demonstrating that the orthogonality constraint prevents the black-box from interfering with identifiable physics.*

### Scenario 2: Partial Physics
In this scenario, the cubic spring term $y^3$ is removed from the dictionary, creating a model discrepancy: $\Phi(x) = [y, \dot{y}, y^2, \dot{y}^2, \dot{y}^3]^\top$. The model must now rely on the GP to capture the missing physics.

*{fig:orthogonal-partial}: Orthogonal kernel GP with a partial dictionary (missing $y^3$). The GP accurately captures the missing cubic dynamics. ## Results and Interpretability Analysis

The numerical results highlight two critical improvements offered by the orthogonal discrepancy kernel: predictive accuracy and model interpretability.

**Predictive Performance:** In terms of Normalized Mean Squared Error (NMSE), the orthogonal kernel GP outperformed the non-orthogonal variant in both the complete and partial dictionary scenarios. For the complete dictionary, the orthogonal method achieved an NMSE of 0.000039, significantly better than the 0.001001 achieved by the standard GP. This suggests that by focusing the GP's capacity on truly unmodeled residuals, the overall model becomes more precise.

**Interpretability:** The most striking difference is visible in the comparison between {fig:non-orthogonal-complete} and {fig:orthogonal-complete}. With a complete dictionary, the standard grey-box approach fails to correctly identify the cubic spring term $ky^3$ in the sparse regression. The flexible GP captures this term instead, resulting in a physically incomplete "white-box." The orthogonal kernel, however, forces the GP to yield to the dictionary. As a result, the sparse coefficients $\theta$ correctly reflect the underlying physics, and the GP remains inactive.

In the partial physics case, the orthogonal kernel model demonstrated a "preferential bias" toward the dictionary. When the correct $y^3$ term was missing, the model activated a linear $y$ term from the dictionary to explain part of the dynamics. This behavior confirms that the framework prioritizes explanations derived from the candidate physics over black-box corrections, which is highly desirable for scientific discovery.

## Significance and Implications

The introduction of orthogonal discrepancy kernels addresses a fundamental tension in physics-informed machine learning: the trade-off between the flexibility of data-driven models and the transparency of physical ones. By mathematically enforcing orthogonality, this framework ensures that machine learning components act as a true "residual" rather than a competitor to physical laws.

The implications for research are significant:
1. **Scientific Discovery:** This method allows researchers to identify which parts of a system's behavior are truly "new" or "unexplained." If the orthogonal GP learns a significant signal, it indicates a genuine gap in current physical understanding rather than just an artifact of the learning process.
2. **Robustness to Incomplete Data:** The framework provides a reliable way to use sparse identification (SINDy) in real-world scenarios where the governing equations are not perfectly known in advance.
3. **Generality:** While demonstrated on ODEs, the methodology is mathematically compatible with PDEs and more complex distributed systems. This opens doors for applications in fluid dynamics, structural health monitoring, and climate science, where models are frequently "partial."

In conclusion, this approach provides a principled way to build hybrid models that are both predictive and explanatory. It moves toward a future where machine learning assists physical modeling by highlighting the unknown, without obscuring what is already known.
