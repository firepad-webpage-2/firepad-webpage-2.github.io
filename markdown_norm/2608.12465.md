## Resolving the Doped Hubbard Model Ground State

The two-dimensional Hubbard model is a central pillar in the study of strongly correlated electrons, widely believed to capture the essential physics of high-temperature superconductivity found in copper-oxide materials (cuprates). Despite its mathematical simplicity—describing electrons hopping on a lattice and interacting only when they occupy the same site—its phase diagram at finite doping remains one of the most contested topics in condensed matter physics. The difficulty arises because multiple distinct electronic orders, such as antiferromagnetism, charge and spin modulations known as "stripes," and $d$-wave superconductivity, are energetically very close to one another.

Numerical simulations of the $t-t'$ Hubbard model (which includes next-nearest-neighbor hopping $t'$) have historically provided conflicting results. Different methods, or even the same method with different initialization parameters, often favor one type of order over another. This inconsistency is largely attributed to "variational bias." Many computational approaches, including standard Neural-Network Quantum States (NQS), tend to favor states that explicitly break the symmetries of the underlying physical system, such as translational symmetry. When these symmetries are broken, the system may appear to be in a "stripe" phase even if the true ground state is superconducting. 

The research by Rende, Viteritti, and Georges addresses this challenge by introducing a new variational wave function that preserves translational symmetry by design. By enforcing these symmetries, the researchers were able to navigate the complex energy landscape of the Hubbard model more reliably, revealing robust evidence for $d$-wave superconductivity in the $1/8$-doped regime.

## The Challenge of Broken Symmetries in Variational Methods

In the search for the ground state of a quantum system, researchers use variational methods to find a wave function that minimizes the system's energy. However, the energy landscape of the Hubbard model is remarkably "rugged," filled with many local minima. Many advanced variational ansätze, such as those utilizing backflow determinants in NQS, are highly flexible. While this flexibility allows them to reach low energies, it also makes them susceptible to getting trapped in states that break translational symmetry.

In the context of the Hubbard model, these broken-symmetry states typically manifest as stripes—periodic modulations of the electron density and spin. Once an optimization process falls into a "stripe" minimum, it is extremely difficult for it to escape and find the symmetric ground state. This often leads to an underestimation of superconducting correlations, as the stripe order competes with and suppresses the pairing necessary for superconductivity.

Existing solutions, like restoring symmetry after the optimization is complete (a process called projection), are computationally expensive and often fail if the initial optimization was too strongly biased toward a specific broken-symmetry state. The core innovation of this work is the development of a Symmetry-Preserving Backflow Pairing (SBP) ansatz that ensures the wave function remains translationally invariant throughout the entire optimization process.

## Symmetry-Preserving Backflow Pairing (SBP)

The SBP ansatz is built on the foundation of a pair-product wave function. In this framework, the state of the system is described by a collection of electron pairs. The wave function $\Psi_\theta(n)$ for a configuration $n$ is defined as:

$$\Psi_\theta(n) = \langle n| \left( \sum_{i,j=1}^N f_{ij}(n) c^\dagger_{i\uparrow} c^\dagger_{j\downarrow} \right)^{N_e/2} |0\rangle$$

where $N_e$ is the number of electrons and $f_{ij}(n)$ is a pairing matrix that determines how electrons at sites $i$ and $j$ pair up. The "backflow" aspect means that this pairing is not fixed but depends on the entire configuration $n$ of the system, allowing the model to capture complex, many-body correlations.

To ensure translational symmetry, the researchers structured the pairing matrix $f_{ij}(n)$ as follows:

$$f_{ij}(n) = \frac{e^{-\lambda d(i,j)}}{\sum_{i',j'} e^{-\lambda d(i',j')}} \sum_{\alpha=1}^d W_{i-j, \alpha} y^\uparrow_{i\alpha}(n) y^\downarrow_{j\alpha}(n)$$

In this equation:
- $W_{i-j, \alpha}$ is a weight matrix that depends only on the relative displacement between sites $i$ and $j$. This is the mathematical key to translational invariance.
- $y^\sigma_{i\alpha}(n)$ represents "backflow orbitals" generated by a transformer-based neural network. The neural network is designed to be translationally equivariant, meaning if the input configuration is shifted, the output orbitals shift accordingly without changing their form.
- $e^{-\lambda d(i,j)}$ is a decay factor based on the distance $d(i,j)$ between sites. This incorporates the physical intuition that electrons are more likely to pair with their neighbors than with distant particles, which helps stabilize the optimization on large lattices.

By using this structure, the researchers restricted the search space to only those states that respect the lattice's symmetries. This effectively smooths the energy landscape, removing the "traps" created by unphysical broken-symmetry states.

## Evidence for d-wave Superconductivity

Using the SBP ansatz, the researchers studied the Hubbard model at parameters relevant to cuprates: $t'/t = -0.2$, $U/t = 8.0$, and $1/8$ doping. They scaled their simulations up to a $24 \times 24$ lattice (504 electrons), which is a significant size for high-accuracy variational methods.

The primary finding was a clear signature of $d_{x^2-y^2}$-wave superconductivity. The researchers calculated the pairing correlation function $C_p(r)$, which measures the likelihood of finding electron pairs at a distance $r$. In a non-superconducting state, this correlation would decay to zero as the distance increases. However, the SBP results showed that $C_p(r)$ saturates to a constant, non-zero value at large distances.

By extrapolating these results to the thermodynamic limit (where the lattice size becomes infinite, $1/L \to 0$), they calculated a superconducting order parameter $\Delta_{SC} = 0.022(1)$. This value is consistent with previous high-precision calculations using other methods like Auxiliary-Field Quantum Monte Carlo (AFQMC), but obtained here without the need for artificial pinning fields or specific boundary conditions that can bias the results.

## Interplay Between Stripes and Superconductivity

A common question in Hubbard model research is whether superconductivity and stripes are mutually exclusive or if they coexist. The SBP ansatz provides a unique perspective on this. Because the SBP wave function is translationally invariant, observables like the local charge density or local spin magnetization $\langle \hat{S}^z_i \rangle$ are uniform across the lattice. In contrast, the non-symmetry-preserved version (the BP ansatz) showed clear spatial oscillations characteristic of pure stripe states.

However, even though the SBP state is uniform, it still contains stripe-like correlations. This was visible in the spin-spin correlation function $C_s(r)$, which showed an antiferromagnetic pattern modulated by a stripe-like envelope (see {fig:stripe-correlations-in-sbp}). This suggest that the ground state is not a simple uniform superconductor, but a state where superconducting order and stripe-like fluctuations are intertwined. The researchers found that a negative $t'$ (specifically $t' = -0.2$) weakens these magnetic stripe correlations compared to the $t'=0$ case, which in turn helps stabilize the superconducting order.

## Methodological Significance and Future Reach

The success of the SBP ansatz demonstrates that neural-network-based methods can reach the state-of-the-art accuracy required to resolve long-standing debates in quantum materials science. On a $16 \times 16$ lattice, the SBP achieved a variational energy of -0.74411(1) per site, outperforming several other leading numerical techniques.

The broader significance of this work lies in the architectural strategy of enforcing physical symmetries. By building translational invariance into the neural network's structure, the authors transformed a difficult, non-convex optimization problem into a much more manageable one. This approach allowed for the use of transfer learning—taking an optimized wave function from a $20 \times 20$ lattice and using it as a starting point for a $24 \times 24$ lattice—which significantly reduced the computational time required for large-scale simulations.

Looking forward, this symmetry-preserving framework can be extended to even more complex systems. The Hubbard model used here is a single-band model, but real materials often involve multiple atomic orbitals. The SBP approach could potentially be adapted to multi-band models and other lattice geometries, providing a flexible and robust tool for discovering the ground states of a wide variety of strongly correlated fermionic systems. By removing the bias toward broken-symmetry states, researchers can gain a more objective view of the competing and intertwined orders that define modern condensed matter physics.
