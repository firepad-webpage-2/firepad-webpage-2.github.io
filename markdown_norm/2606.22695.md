## Mapping Effective Connectivity Across Fragmented Data

A central goal in systems neuroscience is to understand effective connectivity, which describes the directed flow of information between different brain regions. While structural connectivity maps the physical "cables" (axons) of the brain, effective connectivity captures the dynamic interaction where activity in one region exerts a causal influence over another. Modern large-scale recording technologies, such as Neuropixels probes and two-photon calcium imaging, allow researchers to sample neural activity at high resolution. However, these techniques face significant physical constraints: they can typically only observe a small fraction of the brain's billions of neurons or hundreds of regions at any given time.

Consequently, large-scale datasets are often fragmented. Data are collected across multiple recording sessions, different animals, or even different laboratories, resulting in partially overlapping regional coverage. Critically, these different recording blocks usually lack a shared temporal reference—they are asynchronous. Traditional methods for inferring directed connectivity, such as Granger causality or Partial Directed Coherence (PDC), generally require that all variables of interest be recorded simultaneously with a common clock. The SPIDER (Stitched Power-spectra for Inferring Directed information flow from incomplete and asynchronous Experimental Recordings) framework is designed to bridge this gap, allowing researchers to reconstruct a unified directed information-flow network from these disconnected pieces.

## The Challenge of Asynchrony and Partial Observability

The primary difficulty in integrating fragmented neural data lies in the temporal misalignment between sessions. If Region A and Region B are recorded in Session 1, and Region B and Region C are recorded in Session 2, there is no direct way to correlate the activity of A and C because their time series were never captured at the same moment. Traditional time-domain models, which rely on predicting the future state of one variable from the past states of others, fail in this context.

Furthermore, partial observability introduces the problem of latent confounders. If a researcher only observes a subset of regions, an unobserved region might be driving two observed regions simultaneously. Without accounting for this hidden common driver, standard connectivity measures might report a direct, directed link between the two observed regions that does not actually exist. 

SPIDER overcomes these hurdles by shifting the analysis from the time domain to the frequency domain. By focusing on the power spectral density (PSD) and cross-spectra of neural signals, the framework leverages the fact that second-order statistics (variances and covariances across frequencies) are stable for stationary processes, even if the specific time points are different. This allows the integration of information across sessions as long as every pair of regions is co-observed at least once in some recording block.

## The SPIDER Pipeline: Stitching and Factorization

The SPIDER framework follows a multi-step process to transform local, asynchronous observations into a global directed map. The core idea is to "stitch" together local spectral estimates into a comprehensive global spectral matrix.

### 1. Spectral Aggregation (Stitching)
For each recording block $u$, which observes a subset of variables $\Omega_u$, the framework computes a local power spectral density matrix $\hat{S}_{\Omega_u}(\omega)$. The global cross-spectrum $\hat{S}_{ij}(\omega)$ for any pair of regions $i$ and $j$ is then estimated by averaging the local estimates from all blocks where both regions were co-observed. Because the cross-spectrum at frequency $\omega$ summarizes the relationship between variables across the entire recording duration, this step does not require the blocks to be temporally aligned.

### 2. Structured Matrix Completion
In cases where some pairs of regions are never co-observed—leaving "holes" in the global spectral matrix—SPIDER employs nuclear-norm minimization. This technique assumes that the underlying spectral matrix has a low-rank structure, meaning the high-dimensional neural activity is driven by a smaller number of latent sources. By filling in the missing entries through this optimization, the framework can infer interactions between regions that were never in the same recording session.

### 3. Regularization and Factorization
To handle high-dimensional systems where the number of regions $K$ is large relative to the data length $T_u$, the framework applies a complex Hermitian graphical lasso (GLASSO). This regularizes the inverse of the spectral matrix, promoting sparsity and ensuring the matrix is positive definite—a requirement for the next step.

The final directed flow is recovered via canonical spectral factorization. Using Wilson’s algorithm, the global spectral matrix $S(\omega)$ is decomposed into:
$$
S(\omega) = H(\omega)\Sigma H(\omega)^H
$$
where $H(\omega)$ is the transfer function and $\Sigma$ is the innovation covariance. From this, the framework calculates the Partial Directed Coherence (PDC):
$$
\pi_{k \leftarrow \ell}(\omega) = \frac{|\bar{A}_{k\ell}(\omega)|}{\sqrt{\sum_{m=1}^K |\bar{A}_{m\ell}(\omega)|^2}}
$$
where $\bar{A}_{k\ell}(\omega) = \Sigma_{kk}^{-1/2} A_{k\ell}(\omega)$ and $A(\omega) = H(\omega)^{-1}$ is the autoregressive transfer function. The PDC $\pi_{k \leftarrow \ell}(\omega)$ quantifies the direct influence of region $\ell$ on region $k$ at frequency $\omega$, after accounting for the influence of all other regions in the network.

## Validation via Simulations

The researchers validated SPIDER using both linear and nonlinear simulations. In a simple three-node system where node 3 drives nodes 1 and 2, local pairwise analysis (which ignores the unobserved node) incorrectly inferred a connection between 1 and 2. SPIDER, by integrating the observations into a unified model, correctly identified node 3 as the source and eliminated the spurious link.

## Application to Brain-Wide Neural Data

The framework was applied to several major neuroscience datasets to demonstrate its utility in real-world experimental settings.

### Neuropixels and the Mouse Brain-Wide Map
Using data from the International Brain Laboratory (IBL), the authors integrated recordings from 43 sessions, covering 50 brain areas and over 4,000 neurons. Because individual neurons are not consistent across animals, SPIDER employed an "area-level" approach. The activity in each brain area was summarized by its first spectral principal component—a canonical population mode representing the dominant rhythm of that region. The results showed that the inferred directed interactions correlated well with known anatomical projections from the Allen Mouse Brain Connectivity Atlas.

### Human Intracranial EEG
The framework was also applied to human intracranial EEG (iEEG) data from 43 patients. While each patient had electrodes in different locations, SPIDER "stitched" these disparate coverages into a single map of 38 left-hemisphere cortical regions. This allowed for the construction of a whole-cortex directed network that would be impossible to observe in any single individual.

## Discovery of the Theta-Band Feedforward Hierarchy

One of the most significant findings facilitated by SPIDER is the identification of a directed hierarchy in spontaneous brain activity. While overall brain activity is highly recurrent (information flows back and forth), the researchers discovered that interactions in the theta frequency band (4–8 Hz) organize into a clear feedforward hierarchy.

In the mouse brain, the hippocampal formation (including the subiculum and CA1) and the ventral striatum acted as the primary sources of theta-band information, which then flowed toward sinks in the thalamus and prefrontal cortex. Remarkably, a similar pattern was found in the human cortex: ventromedial and paralimbic regions served as sources, while lateral prefrontal and sensory areas served as sinks. This consistency across species and recording modalities suggests a fundamental organizational principle of the mammalian brain's spontaneous oscillations.

## Significance and Future Impact

The SPIDER framework provides a robust mathematical foundation for integrating the increasingly fragmented data landscape of modern neuroscience. By moving to a non-parametric, frequency-domain approach, it removes the necessity for simultaneous recordings and common temporal clocks, which have long been bottlenecks for whole-brain effective connectivity analysis.

The potential impact of this work extends beyond just "stitching" data. It enables the discovery of frequency-specific organizational rules, such as the theta-band hierarchy, that are obscured when looking at time-averaged or undirected connectivity. As neuroscience continues toward collaborative, large-scale data collection, frameworks like SPIDER will be essential for transforming diverse, partial observations into a unified understanding of how the brain coordinates information flow across its vast networks.
