## The Challenge of Multi-Dialect Speech Recognition

Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) has achieved high accuracy in standard languages like Mandarin Chinese. However, the linguistic landscape of China is incredibly diverse, encompassing a wide array of regional dialects such as Cantonese, Sichuanese, and Wu. These dialects often differ significantly from standard Mandarin in phonetics, vocabulary, and grammar. For most modern ASR models, recognizing these dialects remains a significant obstacle.

The typical approach to improving a model's performance on a specific dialect is supervised fine-tuning (SFT)—training the model further on a dedicated dialectal dataset. While this often lowers the Character Error Rate (CER) for the target dialect, it frequently leads to a phenomenon known as catastrophic forgetting. As the model adapts to the new linguistic patterns of a dialect, its proficiency in Mandarin begins to degrade. This "Mandarin-dialect trade-off" forces developers to choose between a model that is good at Mandarin or a model that is good at dialects. 

To address this, researchers have proposed a three-stage adaptation framework designed to master dialects while retaining Mandarin proficiency. The core of this framework is a method called On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD), which refines the model by aligning its behavior during real-world inference with a more stable "teacher" version of itself.

## Bridging the Gap: The Three-Stage Adaptation Framework

The researchers utilized Qwen3-ASR-1.7B as their base model and implemented a sequential training pipeline. Each stage is designed to progressively shift the model's focus toward dialects without losing the foundational knowledge of Mandarin.

### 1. Continual Pre-training (CPT)
The first stage involves exposing the base model to a massive, combined corpus of Mandarin and dialect data, totaling approximately 100,000 hours. This dataset, denoted as $D_{zh}$, provides a broad foundation. Instead of starting from scratch or only focusing on one dialect, CPT allows the model to learn the general relationships between Mandarin and various regional speech patterns. The goal here is to establish a more robust ASR baseline before more specialized training begins.

### 2. Dialect Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)
After pre-training, the model undergoes Dialect Supervised Fine-tuning. In this stage, the training data is adjusted to favor dialectal speech significantly. Specifically, the researchers used a dataset $D_{dial}^{zh} = D_{all}^{dial} \cup D_{small}^{mand}$, which combines all available dialect data ($D_{all}^{dial}$) with a small, representative sample of Mandarin data ($D_{small}^{mand}$). This stage forces the model to specialize in the nuances of regional dialects. While this successfully lowers dialect CER, it is the stage where the Mandarin-dialect trade-off is most visible, as the model starts to shift away from standard Mandarin distributions.

### 3. On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD)
The final stage is the primary contribution of the research. OPSD acts as a refinement step to "clean up" the performance of the SFT checkpoint. It specifically addresses two issues: the train-test mismatch in autoregressive models and the preservation of Mandarin knowledge through "soft" targets.

## Understanding On-Policy Self-Distillation

In standard ASR training, models use a method called "teacher forcing." During training, the model is given the ground-truth prefix of a sentence and asked to predict the next character. However, during inference (real-world use), the ground truth is not available. The model must predict the next character based on its own previous, potentially incorrect, predictions. This discrepancy is known as the train-test mismatch. If the model makes a small error early in a dialectal sentence, that error can propagate, leading to a total failure in recognition.

OPSD mitigates this by training the model in an "on-policy" manner. Here is how the process works:

1.  **Teacher and Student:** The system uses two versions of the same model. The "teacher" ($\bar{\theta}$) is a frozen copy of the SFT checkpoint. The "student" ($\theta$) is the version being updated.
2.  **On-Policy Sampling:** For a given audio input $x$, the student model generates its own hypothesis $\hat{y}_{\le T}$ by sampling characters one by one. This mimics how the model actually behaves during inference.
3.  **Privileged Context:** While the student only sees the audio and its own previous guesses, the teacher is given a "privileged context" ($c(y^\star)$)—the actual reference transcript. This allows the teacher to remain grounded in the correct text even if the student's guesses start to wander.
4.  **Soft Targets and KL Divergence:** Instead of using a "hard" loss (where the model is told exactly which character is right or wrong), the student tries to match the teacher's probability distribution $q_t$. By minimizing the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the student's predictions and the teacher's soft targets, the student learns the nuances of the teacher's decision-making process.

The loss function for this stage is defined as:

$$L_{OPSD} = \sum_t D_{KL}(q_t || p_\theta(\cdot | x, \hat{y}_{<t}))$$

where $p_{\theta}(\cdot | x, \hat{y}_{<t})$ is the student's probability distribution given the audio $x$ and its own generated prefix $\hat{y}_{<t}$. This approach allows the model to become more robust to its own mistakes (on-policy) while using the teacher's soft targets to prevent the drastic parameter shifts that cause catastrophic forgetting.

## Empirical Results and Performance

The researchers evaluated their framework using 31 different test sets, including 8 public Mandarin sets, 5 public dialect sets, and 18 internal dialect sets. The results demonstrated that the three-stage process effectively navigated the Mandarin-dialect trade-off.

Starting from the base Qwen3-ASR model, the final OPSD-refined model achieved improvements across the board:
*   **Mandarin Average CER:** Dropped from 3.46% to 3.27%.
*   **Public Dialect Average CER:** Dropped from 15.37% to 12.79%.
*   **Internal Dialect Average CER:** Dropped from 21.01% to 12.42%.
*   **Overall Average CER:** Improved from 15.57% to 10.12%.

### The Role of Each Stage
Ablation studies revealed that every stage was necessary. Without the initial CPT stage, the SFT process caused Mandarin CER to spike significantly (from 3.46% to over 5%). CPT provided the stability needed for further adaptation. 

The comparison between OPSD and "Continued SFT" (simply training longer on the same data with standard cross-entropy) was particularly telling. While continued SFT slightly improved dialects, it caused Mandarin performance to worsen. Only OPSD managed to improve both dialect and Mandarin performance simultaneously. This suggests that the "soft" guidance of distillation is superior to "hard" supervised updates for maintaining existing knowledge.

### Dialect-Specific Observations
The model's performance varied across different dialects based on their linguistic distance from Mandarin.
*   **Easy Dialects:** Regional varieties like Dongbei, Sichuanese, and Henan achieved CERs below 6%. These are linguistically closer to standard Mandarin.
*   **Hard Dialects:** Coastal and southern dialects like Kejia (Hakka), Chaoshan, and Suzhou remained the most challenging, though OPSD provided substantial gains. For instance, Kejia CER dropped from 32.01% after SFT to 28.60% after OPSD refinement.

## Significance and Future Impact

This research demonstrates a viable path forward for creating truly versatile ASR systems in linguistically complex environments. By moving beyond simple fine-tuning and adopting on-policy distillation, it is possible to build models that respect regional diversity without sacrificing standard language accuracy.

The implications extend beyond Chinese dialects. The principles of OPSD—using a privileged-context teacher to guide a student through on-policy states—could be applied to other ASR challenges, such as adapting models to specialized technical domains or improving performance in high-noise environments. By releasing the model weights and evaluation scripts, the researchers have provided the community with a robust foundation for further exploration into multi-dialectal speech processing. 

In summary, the transition from hard supervised learning to soft, on-policy self-distillation represents a significant step in making ASR systems more inclusive and stable across diverse linguistic distributions.
