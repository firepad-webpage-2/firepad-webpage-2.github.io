## Introduction to Dynamic Option Surfaces

In quantitative finance, the modeling of the implied volatility surface is essential for managing the risk associated with option portfolios, particularly Vega risk. An implied volatility surface represents how the market's expectation of future volatility varies across different option strike prices and times to expiration. However, creating a model that can dynamically evolve this surface over time while ensuring it remains physically realistic and strictly free of "static arbitrage" (internal price inconsistencies that would allow for riskless profit) has been a long-standing challenge.

Most traditional models either focus on a single day's snapshot (static calibration) or use discrete grids that do not provide smooth prices for actual tradable contracts with fixed strikes and calendar dates. When these discrete models are interpolated to find prices between grid points, they often produce "too expensive" results or unrealistic probability densities. Furthermore, data-driven generative models often rely on "soft constraints" to prevent arbitrage, which are frequently insufficient to stop machine learning agents from finding and exploiting artificial profit opportunities during training.

DYSANOS (Generative Dynamic Smooth Arbitrage-free Non-parametric Option Surfaces) addresses these issues by providing a continuous, smooth, and strictly arbitrage-free market model. It generates entire paths of daily spot and option prices, allowing for the calculation of daily returns for any specific contract. This capability is vital for advanced financial applications like Deep Hedging, where an algorithm must learn to manage risk by trading specific instruments over time.

## The SANOS Foundation and Arbitrage-Freeness

The core of the DYSANOS framework is SANOS (Smooth Arbitrage-free Non-parametric Option Surfaces). To understand DYSANOS, one must first understand how SANOS ensures that an option surface $C(T, K)$ (the price of a call option with expiry $T$ and strike $K$) is free of static arbitrage.

Static arbitrage-freeness requires that call prices satisfy several criteria: they must be non-negative, non-increasing in strike, and convex in strike (to prevent "butterfly arbitrage"). Additionally, they must be non-decreasing in maturity for a fixed strike (to prevent "calendar arbitrage"). SANOS enforces these rules by parameterizing the surface using "martingale densities" $q$ and increasing total volatilities $W_j$ across a predefined grid of model expiries $\hat{T}_j$ and strikes $\hat{K}_j$.

By formulating the calibration as a linear programming problem, SANOS finds the best fit to market bid and ask prices while strictly satisfying the linear inequalities that define an arbitrage-free surface. The result is a smooth surface achieved through a specialized interpolation that preserves these no-arbitrage properties, avoiding the "jagged" probability densities found in simpler grid-based models.

## The ML-SANOS Decoder: Bridging Math and Machine Learning

While the original SANOS model is powerful for fitting a single day's data, its constrained optimization parameters are difficult to use in a generative machine learning context. Standard neural network training via gradient descent works best with unconstrained, real-valued inputs.

DYSANOS introduces the "ML-SANOS" decoder to solve this. It takes a high-dimensional vector of raw parameters $x$ and transforms it into the constrained parameters needed for SANOS through a series of differentiable operations:

1.  **Volatility Bounds:** Elements of $x$ are mapped through sigmoid functions to ensure forward volatilities $\sigma_j$ stay within a realistic range $[\sigma_{\min}, \sigma_{\max}]$.
2.  **Density Transitions:** To prevent calendar arbitrage, the model uses "discrete local volatilities" $\Sigma_{ij}$ (also bounded within $[\Sigma_{\min}, \Sigma_{\max}]$) to construct a transition operator $\Xi_j$. This operator evolves the probability density from one expiry $q_{j|j-1}$ to the next $q_j$ while maintaining the required "convex order," ensuring prices never drop as the time to expiry increases.
3.  **Strike Normalization:** The relative strikes $\hat{K}_{ij}$ are dynamically adjusted based on the current volatility level and normalized grid points $z_i$.

To make the model even more efficient, the authors train a small neural network to map a low-dimensional "hidden state" $h$ (with only $n_h = 20$ dimensions) to the larger $x$ vector. This 20-dimensional state $h$ acts as a compressed representation of the entire option surface. Researchers found that this compression only introduces a small volatility error (approx. 0.3%) compared to the full model, making it a highly effective way to represent complex market data.

## Generative Dynamics of Spot and Surface

With a way to represent the surface as a simple vector $h_t$, DYSANOS can then model how this state and the underlying asset price $s_t$ (log-spot) evolve over time. The paper uses a continuous-time auto-regressive (AR(1)) process as a baseline for these dynamics.

The evolution of the surface state is described by the stochastic differential equation:
$$
dh_t = \kappa(m - h_t)dt + \Sigma_h dW_{h,t}
$$
In this equation, $m$ represents the long-term average state of the market, $\kappa$ is the speed at which the market returns to that average (mean-reversion), and $\Sigma_h dW_{h,t}$ represents the random innovations or "shocks" that hit the market each day.

The log-spot price $s_t$ is modeled simultaneously:
$$
s_t = \mu dt + \beta^\prime dW_{h,t} + \varsigma dW_{s,t}
$$
This structure allows the spot price to be correlated with changes in the option surface—for example, capturing the "leverage effect" where a drop in the stock price often leads to an increase in implied volatility. The model uses Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to identify the most significant factors driving these surface changes, finding that just 5 factors can explain over 99% of the daily variation in the surface.

## Handling Extreme Events with Importance Sampling

One difficulty in training financial models is that the most dangerous events (like market crashes) are rare. If a simulator only generates "normal" days, a hedging agent will never learn how to handle a crisis.

DYSANOS employs a technique called "Conditional Spot Importance Sampling." This allows the simulator to generate paths where the spot price moves much further than usual—reaching the "tails" of the distribution—without changing the underlying physical laws of the surface dynamics. By adjusting the "tail multiplier" $r$, researchers can force the simulation to explore extreme price levels. To maintain statistical validity, the results are reweighted using a likelihood ratio, ensuring that probabilities and risk metrics remain accurate reflections of the real world.

## Evaluating Arbitrage in Synthetic Markets

A major contribution of this work is the rigorous testing for arbitrage in the generated data. The authors distinguish between two types of trading strategies:

1.  **Returns to Expiry:** This involves buying an option and holding it until it expires. Because DYSANOS ensures every single generated surface is statically arbitrage-free, the model produced **zero** arbitrage opportunities for this strategy class. In contrast, a standard PCA-based model that does not enforce these constraints produced many "free lunch" opportunities.
2.  **Period Returns:** This involves trading options between time steps (e.g., buying today and selling tomorrow). Interestingly, the tests revealed that DYSANOS *does* admit some dynamic arbitrage for these strategies. Even though each "snapshot" of the market is consistent, the *way* the market moves from one day to the next can occasionally create profitable opportunities.

This finding is significant because it shows that static arbitrage-freeness is a necessary but not sufficient condition for a perfect market simulator. It identifies a clear target for future research: models must not only create consistent surfaces but also ensure the *transitions* between those surfaces are economically consistent.

## Performance and Significance

The researchers evaluated DYSANOS using S&P 500 Index data. The model successfully captured the primary "modes" of market movement, such as the overall level of volatility, the slope of the "volatility skew" (how much more expensive downside protection is compared to upside potential), and the term structure (how volatility expectations change for longer-dated options).

While the baseline Gaussian model tended to understate the "fatness" of market tails and the clustering of volatility (where high volatility days tend to follow each other), it provided a strong and stable foundation. The use of the ML-SANOS decoder makes the system computationally efficient; since the pricing logic is differentiable and compatible with GPUs, it can be used to train complex "Deep Hedging" agents that require millions of simulated scenarios.

In conclusion, DYSANOS provides a path forward for realistic financial simulation. By strictly enforcing static no-arbitrage rules and offering a continuous, smooth surface, it creates a more "honest" environment for financial AI and risk management systems. The discovery of remaining dynamic arbitrage points to the next frontier in the field: the integration of transition-based no-arbitrage constraints into generative deep learning architectures.
