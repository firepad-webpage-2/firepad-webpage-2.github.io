## Axiomatic Foundations of Return Risk Measures

The quantification of risk in financial markets has traditionally relied on Monetary Risk Measures (MRMs). These tools evaluate the risk associated with uncertain monetary payoffs or losses, often assuming a "buy-and-hold" investment strategy. However, modern financial theory frequently operates on logarithmic scales, particularly when considering returns or continuously rebalanced portfolios. To address this, the concept of Return Risk Measures (RRMs) was introduced to provide an axiomatic foundation for measuring the risk of returns.

While existing literature has explored RRMs on specific domains like the space of bounded random variables $L^\infty(\Omega; \mathbb{R})$ or the strictly positive real line $(0, \infty)$, this research provides a more general framework. By utilizing the mathematical structure of AM-algebras, the work unifies disparate approaches and extends the theory to multivariate and systemic risk settings. AM-algebras serve as a bridge between finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces and infinite-dimensional functional spaces, allowing for a consistent treatment of risk across various economic models.

## The AM-Algebra Framework

The choice of AM-algebras as the underlying mathematical domain is central to this work. An AM-algebra is a specific type of Banach lattice algebra that possesses an order unit, which also functions as the algebraic identity. This structure is particularly powerful due to the Krein-Kakutani Theorem, which states that any AM-algebra with a unit is isometric to the space of continuous functions on a compact Hausdorff space.

This isomorphism provides several critical advantages for risk measurement:
1.  **Well-defined Operations:** It ensures that operations like multiplication, exponentiation, and logarithms are well-defined for the elements within the space. For any element $x \in V^+$ and a non-negative scalar $t \ge 0$, the power $x^t \in V^+$ is well-defined and behaves according to standard exponentiation rules.
2.  **Unification:** It allows the same theoretical results to apply to both finite-dimensional vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and infinite-dimensional random variables in $L^\infty(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^n)$.
3.  **Structural Clarity:** By moving beyond specific probability spaces, the research highlights the structural properties of RRMs that are independent of the underlying stochastic model.

## Geometrically Convex Risk Measures

A primary focus of the paper is the concept of geometric-geometric convexity, or GG-convexity. In the context of standard MRMs, convexity is a desirable property because it rewards diversification: the risk of a diversified portfolio is no greater than the weighted average risk of its components. For RRMs, which deal with returns, GG-convexity serves a similar purpose but specifically for continuously rebalancing trading strategies.

A risk measure is considered GG-convex if it satisfies a specific inequality when dealing with products and powers of assets. To analyze these properties, the research introduces the "geometric epigraph," defined as:

$$\text{epi}_G(f) = \{(x, \alpha) \in E \times (0, \infty)^m \mid f(x) \leq \alpha\}$$

The paper establishes that a risk measure is GG-convex if and only if its geometric epigraph is a GG-convex set. This geometric approach provides a visual and intuitive way to understand how risk scales and aggregates within this framework.

## Bridging RRMs and Monetary Risk Measures

One of the most significant theoretical contributions of this work is the explicit one-to-one correspondence between RRMs and MRMs. The research demonstrates that any RRM $e\rho: \text{int}_{\|\cdot\|_\infty}(V^+) \to (0, \infty)^m$ can be transformed into a monetary risk measure $\rho$ using a $\log$-$\exp$ mapping:

$$\rho = \log \circ e\rho \circ \exp$$

Conversely, an RRM can be recovered from an MRM via:

$$e\rho(x) = \exp(\rho(\log(x)))$$

This correspondence is not merely a mathematical curiosity; it allows researchers to port a vast library of existing knowledge from the MRM literature into the domain of RRMs. Properties such as lower semicontinuity, properness, and convexity in the monetary world translate directly to GG-convexity and specific continuity properties in the return world. 

For instance, the research uses this link to prove that proper RRMs on AM-algebras are locally Lipschitz-continuous. This means that small changes in the underlying assets lead to bounded changes in the risk assessment, a crucial property for stable financial modeling. However, the work also provides examples showing that RRMs are generally not globally Lipschitz-continuous, highlighting a fundamental difference in how they respond to large scale changes compared to their monetary counterparts.

## Dual Representations and the C-Property

Dual representation is a cornerstone of risk theory, expressing a risk measure as the worst-case expected value over a set of probability measures or dual elements. This paper derives dual representations for GG-convex RRMs on AM-algebras, identifying the specific structure of the dual space.

The representation involves both "order continuous" and "singular" functionals. Order continuous functionals are typically associated with standard probability densities, while singular functionals represent risk assessments that focus on "tail events" or rare, extreme scenarios. 

A significant challenge in this area is determining when singular functionals can be excluded. The paper investigates the "C-property" of the weak topology. If the topology satisfies this property, the risk measure can be represented using only the more manageable order continuous functionals. However, the author provides a counterexample using the space of continuous functions on an interval, demonstrating that the C-property does not hold universally. This finding warns practitioners that excluding singular functionals in certain infinite-dimensional settings might lead to an incomplete assessment of risk.

## Systemic Return Risk Measures

As financial systems become more interconnected, the need to measure systemic risk—the risk of collapse of an entire system rather than an individual entity—has grown. This research extends RRMs to systemic settings, where the input is a vector of returns from different institutions.

The paper defines systemic RRMs and establishes a decomposition theorem. Under an assumption called "preference consistency," a systemic RRM can be broken down into two simpler components:

$$e\rho = e\rho_0 \circ \tilde{\Lambda}$$

In this structure, $\tilde{\Lambda}$ is a "return aggregation function" that combines the multi-dimensional returns into a single system-wide return, and $e\rho_0$ is a scalar RRM that evaluates the risk of that aggregate. This decomposition is vital for regulatory purposes, as it separates the problem of how risks interact (aggregation) from the problem of how risk is quantified (evaluation).

The research also explores "risk GG-convexity" for these systemic measures. While standard GG-convexity and positive homogeneity usually imply risk GG-convexity, the author provides a counterexample showing that this is not always true without the assumption of positive homogeneity. This nuance is important for designing risk measures that accurately reflect the benefits or dangers of systemic diversification.

## Separability in Vector-Valued Measures

In some scenarios, a risk manager might use a vector-valued RRM to provide individual risk scores for different sub-portfolios or subsidiaries simultaneously. A key question is whether these components are "separable"—meaning the risk score for the $i$-th subsidiary depends only on that subsidiary's returns.

The paper identifies sufficient conditions for this separability:
- **Pointwise Positive Homogeneity:** If scaling a single asset's return by a factor scales the corresponding risk component by the same factor.
- **Marginal Domination:** A condition ensuring that the evaluation of one component is not unduly influenced by the presence of others.

The research proves that for RRMs defined on $L^\infty(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^n)$, pointwise positive homogeneity is a strong enough condition to ensure that the RRM can be represented as:

$$e\rho(X) = (\tilde{r}_1(X_1), \dots, \tilde{r}_n(X_n))^\top$$

This result simplifies the implementation of vector-valued RRMs, as it allows for the independent calculation of risks while maintaining a unified axiomatic framework.

## Practical Examples: Entropic Risk and Expected Shortfall

To ground the theoretical results, the paper examines how common risk measures fit into the RRM framework. 

1.  **Entropic Risk Measures:** These are based on exponential utility functions. The paper shows how they can be adapted to the return setting and characterizes the specific subsets of the domain where they remain well-defined.
2.  **Expected Shortfall:** A widely used measure in banking regulation (Basel III/IV). The research demonstrates how Expected Shortfall can be viewed as an RRM and how its dual representation can be constructed within the AM-algebra setting.

These examples illustrate that the abstract theory of GG-convex RRMs on AM-algebras is directly applicable to the tools currently used by risk managers and regulators, providing them with a more rigorous mathematical basis for handling returns and systemic interactions.
