## Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces and the Hybrid Architecture

The evolution of wireless communications toward 6G requires technologies capable of managing the propagation environment. Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces (RIS) have emerged as a primary candidate for this role. An RIS is a planar surface composed of a large number of nearly passive elements that can independently shift the phase of incident electromagnetic waves. By carefully adjusting these phase shifts, the surface can steer reflected beams toward a target receiver, effectively "programming" the wireless channel to overcome obstacles or suppress interference.

However, the effectiveness of an RIS depends entirely on the accuracy of Channel State Information (CSI). In a traditional passive RIS setup, the surface lacks active radio frequency (RF) chains. This means it cannot transmit or receive signals itself; it only reflects. Consequently, the Base Station (BS) can only observe the "cascaded channel"—the combination of the link from the User Equipment (UE) to the RIS and the link from the RIS back to the BS. As the number of RIS elements $N$ grows to provide higher beamforming gain, the overhead required to estimate this cascaded channel becomes prohibitive.

To address this, researchers have proposed Hybrid RIS (HRIS) architectures. Unlike fully passive surfaces, an HRIS integrates a small number of active sensing elements. These elements can simultaneously reflect a portion of the signal and process the remainder locally using dedicated RF chains. This local sensing capability provides a direct observation of the UE-RIS link, significantly simplifying the channel estimation process. The challenge lies in designing an HRIS that maximizes sensing accuracy while minimizing the hardware complexity and power consumption associated with these active components.

## L-Shaped Coprime Sparse Sensing Geometry

A key contribution of this work is the application of coprime array theory to the design of the active sensing elements within the HRIS. In a standard Uniform Rectangular Array (URA) with $N = N_y \times N_z$ elements, making a large portion of the elements active would be costly and power-intensive. Instead, this research proposes a sparse "L-shaped" sensing geometry.

This geometry utilizes the properties of coprime arrays to achieve a large virtual aperture with a very small number of physical sensors. A coprime array is defined by two integers, $P_c$ and $Q_c$, which share no common factors other than 1. By placing sensors at specific multiples of these integers, the array can generate a "difference coarray" that behaves like a much larger filled array.

In this paper, the active sensors are arranged along the first row and first column of the HRIS grid in an L-shape. The number of active elements $N_A$ is kept much smaller than the total elements $N$. For example, in a surface with 121 elements, only 11 might be active. This sparse arrangement allows the system to extract high-resolution angular information—such as the Angle of Arrival (AoA) and Angle of Departure (AoD)—without requiring a dense grid of RF chains.

## A Three-Phase Parameter Estimation Framework

The proposed method estimates the channel parameters through a structured three-phase approach that combines local sensing at the HRIS with global reflection processing at the BS.

### Phase 1: Local Sensing at the HRIS
The active elements at the HRIS capture a fraction of the incident signal from the UE. Let $\eta_S$ be the sensing factor representing the fraction of power diverted to the RF chains. The effective sensing channel $\mathbf{G}_S = \mathbf{J}_S \mathbf{G} \eta_S$ is estimated using matched filtering, where $\mathbf{J}_S$ is a selection matrix identifying the active elements.

Because the system assumes a dominant Line-of-Sight (LoS) environment, this sensing channel is approximately rank-one. The researchers use a Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to perform a least-squares Kronecker factorization, extracting the dominant spatial signatures:
$$\hat{\mathbf{G}}_S \approx \hat{\mathbf{p}}_S \hat{\mathbf{q}}_S^T$$
Here, $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_S$ represents the arrival response at the HRIS, and $\hat{\mathbf{q}}_S$ represents the transmit response at the UE. Because of the L-shape, $\hat{\mathbf{p}}_S$ can be decoupled into horizontal ($\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{S,y}$) and vertical ($\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{S,z}$) 1D spatial signatures. To handle the "gaps" in the sparse coprime array, the method applies coarray-domain spatial smoothing to restore the rank of the covariance matrix, followed by the Root-MUSIC algorithm to find the exact angles.

### Phase 2: Reflection Processing at the Base Station
While the HRIS senses the signal, it also reflects the majority of the power (scaled by a factor $\rho$) toward the BS. The signal received at the BS across $K$ time blocks is vectorized to reveal a Khatri-Rao structure. A key property used here is:
$\text{vec}(\mathbf{A}\text{diag}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{B}) = (\mathbf{B}^T \diamond \mathbf{A})\mathbf{x}$

By stacking observations, the BS can estimate the cascaded channel matrix:
$$\mathbf{U} = (\mathbf{G}^T \diamond \mathbf{H})\boldsymbol{\Omega} + \mathbf{V}'$$
where $\hat{\mathbf{E}} = \mathbf{G}^T \diamond \mathbf{H}$ is the Khatri-Rao product of the UE-HRIS channel ($\mathbf{G}$) and the HRIS-BS channel ($\mathbf{H}$). Using Least-Squares Khatri-Rao Factorization (LSKRF), the BS decouples $\hat{\mathbf{H}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{G}}$. This allows the BS to estimate parameters like the AoA at the BS ($\theta_{BS}$) and the AoD from the HRIS.

### Phase 3: Data Fusion
The final step is the fusion of data. Certain parameters, like the UE's AoD ($\phi_{UE}$), are estimated by both the HRIS (via local sensing) and the BS (via reflection). The framework averages these estimates to improve overall accuracy. The local sensing branch typically provides better accuracy for the UE-HRIS link because it avoids the "double path loss" of the full cascaded link.

## Performance and Accuracy

The research evaluates the system using the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of the angular estimates and the Normalized Mean Square Error (NMSE) of the channel reconstruction. The simulations compare the proposed coprime HRIS against a fully passive RIS.

The results demonstrate that the HRIS architecture provides a significant gain in estimation accuracy for parameters related to the UE-HRIS link. As shown in {fig:rmse-of-hris-azimuth-aoa}, the local sensing capability allows the HRIS to track the user's angle much more precisely than a passive system could. ## Trade-offs: Power Splitting and Sensor Count

A critical aspect of HRIS design is the power-splitting parameter $\rho$. This value determines how much of the incident energy is reflected to the BS (for communication) versus how much is absorbed by the active elements (for sensing).

![impact-of-power-splitting-parameter](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13436v1/img-5.jpeg)
*{fig:impact-of-power-splitting-parameter}: The RMSE of different parameters as a function of the reflection power splitting parameter $\rho$. Increasing reflection power helps the BS-side estimation but degrades the sensing-side estimation.*

As illustrated in {fig:impact-of-power-splitting-parameter}, there is a clear trade-off. When $\rho$ is high, the reflected signal is strong, helping the BS estimate parameters like the BS AoA. However, this leaves less power for the active sensors, causing their local estimates to degrade. The proposed data fusion approach helps stabilize this, as the two branches (sensing and reflection) complement each other across different power regimes.

The number of active elements $N_A$ also plays a role. The simulations show that even a small number of sensors arranged in a coprime geometry can yield high accuracy. Increasing $N_A$ improves sensing because the virtual aperture grows, providing more spatial samples for the Root-MUSIC algorithm. Crucially, because $N_A$ is small relative to the total number of elements, the loss in reflection gain (caused by diverting energy to sensors) is negligible.

## Conclusion and Future Directions

The integration of sparse coprime arrays into a hybrid RIS architecture offers a pathway toward low-overhead, high-accuracy channel estimation for 6G MIMO systems. By combining local sensing with structured cascaded channel processing, the proposed framework achieves superior performance compared to traditional passive RIS methods while maintaining low hardware complexity.

This work focuses on a dominant Line-of-Sight scenario, which is common in high-frequency (mmWave or THz) communications where RIS is most effective. Future research may extend these principles to rich-scattering environments (Non-Line-of-Sight) or dynamic scenarios involving high user mobility. The use of advanced tensor decompositions and optimized power-splitting strategies remains an open area for further refinement of the HRIS paradigm.
