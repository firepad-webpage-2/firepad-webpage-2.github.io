## The Challenge of Global Edge Intelligence

The rapid growth of the Internet-of-Things (IoT) has generated massive amounts of data at the network edge. To process this information efficiently while maintaining data privacy, federated learning (FL) has become a prominent framework. In FL, devices train local models on their own data and only share model updates—rather than raw data—with a central server for aggregation. However, implementing FL on a global scale faces a significant infrastructure gap. Large portions of the Earth, including oceans, remote deserts, and rural regions, lack the terrestrial cellular coverage required for standard FL operations.

![overview-of-the-dual-layer-over-the-air-federated-learning-architecture-in-leo-satellite-networks](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13885v1/model3-mag.png)

Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellite networks offer a solution to this problem by providing ubiquitous global connectivity. Unlike geostationary satellites, LEO satellites orbit closer to Earth, resulting in lower propagation delays and making them suitable for time-sensitive distributed learning tasks. Despite this potential, integrating FL with LEO networks is difficult due to the satellites' high mobility, limited onboard energy, and constrained communication bandwidth. Conventional digital communication methods, where each device is assigned a separate frequency or time slot, suffer from an "uplink bottleneck." As the number of ground devices increases, the time required to collect all updates grows linearly, often exceeding the short window of time a satellite is visible to a specific ground area.

This research proposes a framework that combines dual-layer over-the-air (OTA) computation with adaptive beam hopping (BH) to address these scalability and resource constraints. By moving away from traditional interference-avoidance techniques and instead utilizing the physical properties of the wireless channel, the system aims to decouple communication latency from the number of participating devices while intelligently managing satellite resources.

## Over-the-Air Computation: A Physical Layer Shift

A core contribution of this work is the use of Over-the-Air (OTA) computation. In standard digital systems, the goal is to prevent signals from different users from interfering with one another. In contrast, OTA computation exploits the superposition property of wireless channels to perform data aggregation directly in the air. When multiple devices transmit their analog signals simultaneously over the same frequency, the signals naturally add up at the receiver.

In the context of federated learning, the central server needs to calculate the average of local model updates. If $K$ devices transmit their updates $w_k$ simultaneously, the received signal at a satellite $s$ can be represented as:

$$y_s = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}_s} h_{k,s} w_k + n_s$$

where $h_{k,s}$ is the channel gain for device $k$ to satellite $s$, and $n_s$ represents receiver noise. By carefully controlling the transmit power of each device—a process known as channel inversion—the effects of different channel gains can be neutralized, allowing the satellite to receive a weighted sum of the updates. This process effectively performs the "aggregation" step of federated learning at the physical layer, requiring only a single resource block regardless of how many devices are transmitting.

The proposed architecture extends this to a dual-layer system. In the first layer, ground devices transmit their updates to a LEO satellite. In the second layer, the satellite acts as a transparent relay, amplifying and forwarding the aggregated analog signal to a ground-based Data Processing Center (DPC). This approach minimizes the computational burden on the satellite itself and allows the DPC, which has access to more powerful hardware and energy resources, to perform the final model updates.

## Adaptive Beam Hopping for Resource Efficiency

LEO satellites typically use multi-beam antennas to cover large geographic areas. However, keeping all beams active at all times is energy-inefficient, especially if some areas contain stale data or have poor channel conditions. The research incorporates "beam hopping" (BH), a technology that allows the satellite to dynamically steer its radio resources to specific cells in a time-slotted manner.

![the-operational-workflow-of-the-dual-layer-ota-fl-system](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13885v1/workflow.png)

The scheduling of these beams is critical. If the system only prioritizes devices with the best channel conditions, it might ignore areas with vital new information. Conversely, if it only prioritizes new data, it might suffer from high aggregation errors due to poor signal quality. To balance these needs, the framework uses a data freshness metric called Age of Information (AoI). The AoI for a device $i$ at time $t$ is defined as $\Delta_i(t) = t - u_i(t)$, where $u_i(t)$ is the timestamp of the last successfully aggregated update from that device. A higher $\Delta_i(t)$ indicates that the data is becoming "stale," signaling the scheduler to prioritize that region in future rounds.

## Intelligent Orchestration via Deep Reinforcement Learning

The challenge of deciding which beams to activate and how much power each device should use is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). Given the high mobility of satellites and the time-varying nature of data generation, traditional optimization methods are often too slow or require perfect knowledge of the future. The paper utilizes Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL), specifically the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, to manage this complexity.

The DRL agent, located at the DPC, observes the state of the network, which includes:
1.  **Pending Data Queues:** Reflecting the freshness (AoI) of the data at different ground locations.
2.  **Channel Conditions:** The real-time quality of the links between ground devices, satellites, and the DPC.

Based on these observations, the agent outputs a scheduling policy that determines which cells to illuminate and the optimal transmit power for the devices. The reward function for the agent is designed to maximize the volume of "fresh" data collected while keeping the aggregation distortion, measured by Mean Squared Error ($MSE$), below a specific threshold. The $MSE$ is defined as $MSE = \mathbb{E}[\|\hat{g} - g\|^2]$, where $g$ is the true average of the updates and $\hat{g}$ is the estimate recovered from the noisy analog signal. By penalizing high $MSE$, the agent ensures that the aggregated model remains accurate even as it pushes for higher throughput.

## Empirical Validation: Wildfire Detection Case Study

To test the effectiveness of the proposed framework, the authors conducted a case study focused on a smart forestry application: wildfire detection. In this scenario, thousands of remote IoT sensors monitor environmental conditions. These sensors use a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to detect anomalies associated with fires. Using the FLAME (Fire Luminosity Airborne-based Machine learning Evaluation) dataset, the researchers simulated a LEO constellation at 550 km altitude serving these sensors.

The results demonstrated that the PPO-based scheduling policy outperformed other standard DRL algorithms and greedy heuristics. Specifically:
*   **Learning Convergence:** The system achieved higher global model accuracy in fewer communication rounds compared to digital aggregation methods. This is because the OTA approach allows for more frequent updates within the same time window.
*   **Resource Utilization:** Even with a limited number of active beams (e.g., only 3 beams active out of many potential cells), the intelligent scheduler was able to maintain high data freshness and model performance.
*   **Scalability:** The communication latency remained constant as the number of devices per cell increased, confirming the advantage of OTA computation over traditional digital access.

## Broader Impact and Applications

The integration of dual-layer OTA and adaptive beam hopping has implications beyond forestry. By transforming LEO satellites into intelligent aggregation nodes, this framework enables a new class of global intelligent services.

![potential-application-scenarios-for-satellite-based-federated-learning](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13885v1/application2.png)

In maritime logistics, ships in the middle of the ocean can collaboratively train models for route optimization or mechanical failure prediction without needing expensive, high-bandwidth satellite links to upload raw sensor data. In emergency disaster response, where terrestrial towers might be destroyed, a LEO-based FL system can aggregate data from survivor devices and drones to create real-time disaster heatmaps.

Furthermore, the OTA approach provides a layer of "physical privacy." Because the signals are blended in the air, an eavesdropper intercepting the satellite's transmission would only see a noisy, aggregated waveform rather than individual user data. This makes it significantly harder to perform model-inversion attacks that aim to reconstruct a specific user's private dataset.

## Conclusion

The proposed dual-layer OTA-FL framework addresses the fundamental bottlenecks of deploying edge intelligence in remote areas. By binding physical-layer signal superposition with intelligent spatio-temporal resource scheduling, the system achieves a level of scalability and efficiency that traditional digital satellite networks cannot match. This research provides a path toward truly ubiquitous machine learning, where intelligence can be gathered and refined from every corner of the planet, regardless of local infrastructure. Future work in this area will likely focus on multi-satellite cooperation and handling the heterogeneity of different device types and data distributions across the globe.
