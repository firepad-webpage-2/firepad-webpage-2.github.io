## Introduction to Gallai-Ramsey Theory and Book Graphs

Ramsey theory is often described by the "friendship theorem": in any group of six people, there are either three who all know each other or three who are all complete strangers. In mathematical terms, this is a statement about coloring the edges of a complete graph $K_p$. If we color every edge either red or blue, we are looking for a monochromatic triangle. The Ramsey number $r(G)$ represents the minimum number of vertices needed to guarantee such a structure.

Gallai-Ramsey theory introduces a specific restriction on how we color the edges. A "Gallai coloring" is an edge-coloring of a complete graph that contains no "rainbow" triangles—triangles where all three edges have different colors. This constraint is named after Tibor Gallai, who proved fundamental structural properties of such colorings. Within these Gallai-colored graphs, researchers look for the smallest graph order $gr_t(G)$ that guarantees a monochromatic subgraph $G$ when using $t$ colors.

The research by Budden and Gregory focuses on a specific family of graphs known as "books." A book graph, denoted $B_n := K_2 + nK_1$, consists of a "spine" (an edge connecting two vertices) and $n$ "pages" (additional vertices that are each connected to both vertices of the spine). This structure is a fundamental object in graph theory, and understanding its Ramsey properties helps mathematicians understand how local connectivity propagates through larger networks.

## Defining Weakened Gallai-Ramsey Numbers

Traditional Gallai-Ramsey numbers search for monochromatic subgraphs, meaning the edges of the subgraph must all be of a single color ($s=1$). However, a more flexible version of this problem asks for the minimum order needed to guarantee a subgraph whose edges use at most $s$ colors. This is known as the "weakened" Gallai-Ramsey number, denoted $gr_t^s(G)$.

For $1 \le s < t$, the number $gr_t^s(G)$ is the minimum integer $p$ such that every Gallai $t$-coloring of $K_p$ contains a subgraph $G$ whose edges use at most $s$ colors. The case $s=2$ is the focus of this work, which aims to find the minimum size of a Gallai-colored graph that forces a book $B_n$ to appear using no more than two distinct colors. This variant is often more complex to solve than the monochromatic case because it allows for mixed-color subgraphs, requiring more intricate construction and analysis.

## Methodology: The Gallai Partition Approach

The primary tool for analyzing Gallai colorings is the Gallai Partition Theorem. This theorem states that every Gallai-colored complete graph with at least two vertices has a "Gallai partition." This means the vertices can be partitioned into non-empty sets (blocks) such that:
1. The edges between any two distinct blocks are monochromatic.
2. The edges between different pairs of blocks, when viewed as a single "base graph," form a 2-colored complete graph.

This decomposition allows researchers to simplify a complex $t$-color problem into a series of 2-color problems. If we want to prove an upper bound for $gr_t^s(B_n)$, we assume a graph of order $p$ exists without the desired subgraph and then use the partition to show a contradiction. The authors developed several lemmas to restrict the possible configurations of these partitions:

*   **Lemma 2.2**: A minimal Gallai partition cannot have a base graph of order 3.
*   **Lemma 2.3**: Provides a bound on the order of the base graph $k$ relative to the total vertices $m$ and the number of pages $n$. If $k$ is too large, a $B_n$ using at most two colors is guaranteed.
*   **Lemma 2.4 and 2.5**: Specific technical bounds for cases where the base graph has order 4 or where a vertex is incident to edges of only one color.

By using these lemmas, the authors can systematically check all possible ways a Gallai coloring could be constructed and prove that, eventually, a two-colored book $B_n$ must appear.

## Exact Values for Small Books and Arbitrary Colors

One of the significant contributions of this paper is extending results for the smallest book graphs, $B_3$ and $B_4$, to any number of colors $t$. Previous research had only determined these values for $t=3$. The authors established the following results for any $t \ge 3$:

*   For $B_3$, the weakened Gallai-Ramsey number is constant: $gr_t^2(B_3) = 6$.
*   For $B_4$, the value depends on the number of colors: $gr_3^2(B_4) = 7$, but for $t \ge 4$, the value increases to $gr_t^2(B_4) = 8$.

These results show that as we increase the number of available colors, the "space" required to avoid a two-colored book sometimes increases, but only up to a certain point. The stability for $B_3$ suggests that the structural constraints of rainbow-free colorings are quite restrictive for very small subgraphs.

## Expanding the Catalogue: Larger Books

The core of the paper involves calculating exact values for $gr_3^2(B_n)$ for books with $5$ to $15$ pages. This range represents a significant step forward from previous work that stopped at $n=4$. For each $n$, the authors provide a proof for the upper bound (using the partition methodology) and a construction for the lower bound (showing that a graph one vertex smaller can avoid the target subgraph).

The determined values for $t=3$ and $s=2$ include:
*   $gr_3^2(B_5) = 9$
*   $gr_3^2(B_6) = 11$
*   $gr_3^2(B_9) = 15$
*   $gr_3^2(B_{12}) = 20$
*   $gr_3^2(B_{15}) = 25$

The jump in values often corresponds to the underlying 2-color Ramsey numbers of books. For instance, the authors utilize known values like $r_2(B_3)=14$ and $r(B_2, B_3)=11$ to guide their constructions. The results for $5 \le n \le 15$ provide a dense set of data points that reveal a nearly linear growth in the weakened Gallai-Ramsey number relative to the number of pages $n$.

## General Lower Bound Theorems

To establish lower bounds for any $n$, the authors proved general theorems based on the remainder of $n$ when divided by 3. These bounds are derived from specific constructions that avoid two-colored books by using base graphs of size 4 or 5.

For $t \ge 3$ and $n \ge 3$, the authors established:
$$gr_t^2(B_n) \ge \lceil \frac{4n+6}{3} \rceil \text{ if } n \equiv 0 \pmod 3$$
$$gr_t^2(B_n) \ge \lceil \frac{5n+1}{3} \rceil \text{ if } n \equiv 1 \pmod 3$$
$$gr_t^2(B_n) \ge \lceil \frac{4n+7}{3} \rceil \text{ if } n \equiv 2 \pmod 3$$

These formulas provide a baseline for what to expect for even larger books. The constructions involve placing monochromatic blocks into a 2-colored base graph. For example, a construction might use a $K_4$ base graph where vertices are replaced by smaller complete graphs of a third color.

Additionally, Theorem 2.7 provides a way to generate lower bounds for $B_{n+k}$ based on the existence of specific 2-colorings of $K_k$. Specifically, if there is a 2-coloring of $K_k$ that avoids a monochromatic $B_n$ and a monochromatic star $K_{1,\lfloor \frac{n+k-1}{2} \rfloor+1}$, then $gr_t^2(B_{n+k}) \ge 2k+1$. This connects the weakened Gallai-Ramsey problem directly to classical extremal graph theory problems involving stars and books.

## Significance and Potential Impact

The work of Budden and Gregory is significant because it moves beyond the simplest cases of Gallai-Ramsey theory. By focusing on the "weakened" version ($s=2$), they address a more general and flexible coloring scenario that reflects the complexity of real-world networks where connections might not be strictly monochromatic but are still restricted in variety.

The determination of exact values for $n$ up to 15 allows other researchers to look for asymptotic patterns. The methodology demonstrated—combining structural theorems with specific lemmas to prune the case analysis—is a robust framework that can be applied to other graph families, such as cycles or complete bipartite graphs.

One interesting implication noted by the authors is the relationship between different types of subgraphs. Since $K_{2,n}$ is a subgraph of $B_n$, the results provide an automatic upper bound:
$$gr_t^s(K_{2,n}) \le gr_t^s(B_n)$$
This suggests that studying book graphs is a highly efficient way to bound the Ramsey numbers of multiple related graph structures simultaneously.

Future research in this area may look to extend these results for $s > 2$, exploring how the "weakening" of the color constraint continues to shift the Ramsey threshold. The authors also suggest that while their case-by-case analysis is powerful for small $n$, a more generalized argument that does not rely on specific 2-color Ramsey values would be a major step forward for the field.
