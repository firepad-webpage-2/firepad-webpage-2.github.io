## The Challenge of Estimating Heterogeneous Agent Models

In modern macroeconomics, Heterogeneous Agent (HA) models have become a standard tool for understanding how individual differences—such as variations in wealth, income, and employment status—affect the aggregate economy. Unlike traditional Representative Agent (RA) models, which assume all households or firms behave identically, HA models allow for a more realistic depiction of economic inequality and the transmission of shocks across different segments of society.

However, the increased realism of HA models comes at a significant computational and statistical cost. Estimating these models typically requires a "full-information" approach, where the researcher must specify every single component of the economy, including the household sector, firm production, financial markets, and government policy. This requirement creates a substantial hurdle: if any one part of the model is incorrectly specified, the resulting errors can "contaminate" the estimates for the rest of the model. For instance, a researcher interested in studying household consumption behavior would still need to correctly model the intricacies of the banking sector or the central bank's reaction function to obtain reliable results.

This paper addresses this problem by developing a "limited-information" estimation framework. This method allows researchers to isolate and estimate a specific "block" of an HA model—such as the household sector—without needing to specify or solve the entire general equilibrium of the economy. By focusing on a single block, the method provides a more robust way to conduct structural inference that is shielded from misspecification in other unmodeled parts of the macroeconomy.

## Sufficient Statistics and the Information Structure

The core principle behind this method is the observation that in most standard HA models, agents interact with the rest of the economy through a limited set of variables. In the context of a household block, individual agents do not need to know the specific details of a technological shock or a change in competition policy; they only care about how these events affect the prices and aggregates that directly impact their budget constraints and payoffs.

The authors refer to these variables as "sufficient statistics." For a typical household, the sufficient statistics might include the real wage, the interest rate on savings, and aggregate tax levels. The agents' optimal decisions today depend on their current state (e.g., their current wealth) and their expectations about the future path of these sufficient statistics.

Let $y_t$ represent an aggregate outcome of interest from the block, such as total consumption or the variance of savings across households. Let $x_t$ be a vector containing the sufficient statistics. The decision rules in these models can be linearized around a steady state, allowing the relationship between the outcomes and the statistics to be expressed as:

$$y_t \approx y_{ss} + \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} J_k(\theta) (x_{t+k} - x_{ss}) + \xi_{t-1}(\theta)$$

In this expression, $y_{ss}$ and $x_{ss}$ are the steady-state values. The term $\xi_{t-1}(\theta)$ captures the "distributional momentum"—the effect of the pre-existing distribution of wealth or other states inherited from the previous period. The coefficients $J_k(\theta)$ are the Sequence-Space Jacobians (SSJs).

## Sequence-Space Jacobians as Structural Links

Sequence-Space Jacobians are the mathematical heart of this framework. They represent the sensitivity of an aggregate outcome at time $t$ to a change in a sufficient statistic at some future time $t+k$. Crucially, these Jacobians are functions of the structural parameters $\theta$ that define the behavior within that specific block (for example, the discount factor or the elasticity of intertemporal substitution).

Because the SSJs only depend on the internal structure of the block being studied, they remain invariant regardless of how the rest of the economy is structured. Whether interest rates are set by a Taylor rule or are fixed by a foreign power does not change how a household's consumption reacts to a given path of interest rates. This invariance is what enables limited-information estimation: the researcher can calculate the theoretical SSJs for the household block and then match them to empirical data without ever solving for the full general equilibrium.

## Moment Conditions and Identified Shocks

To estimate the parameters $\theta$, the method requires a way to link the theoretical SSJs to observable data. The authors achieve this by using identified macroeconomic shocks, denoted as $z_t$, as instrumental variables. Examples of such shocks include unexpected changes in monetary policy or government spending.

The methodology relies on two key assumptions regarding these shocks:
1.  The shock $z_t$ is independent of the past state of the economy, meaning it is uncorrelated with the distributional momentum term $\xi_{t-1}(\theta)$.
2.  Under the assumption of rational expectations, the shock $z_t$ is part of the agents' information set but cannot predict future forecast errors.

By taking the covariance of both sides of the linearized decision rule with the shock $z_t$, the authors derive the following system of moment conditions:

$$\text{Cov}(y_t, z_t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} J_k(\theta) \text{Cov}(x_{t+k}, z_t)$$

This equation provides a powerful bridge between theory and data. The terms $\text{Cov}(y_t, z_t)$ and $\text{Cov}(x_{t+k}, z_t)$ are closely related to empirical impulse response functions (IRFs). Specifically, the left side represents how the aggregate outcome $y_t$ responds to the shock $z_t$, while the right side represents a weighted sum of how the sufficient statistics $x_t$ respond to the same shock, with the weights being the structural Jacobians.

## Frequency-Domain Estimation

While the moment conditions can be viewed in terms of time-series IRFs, the authors implement the estimator in the frequency domain. This approach is computationally efficient for handling the infinite sums associated with the SSJs and provides a natural way to account for the long-run dynamics of the model.

The researcher defines a spectral version of the Jacobian:
$$J(\omega; \theta) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} J_k(\theta) e^{-i\omega k}$$
which captures the block's response at different frequencies $\omega$. The estimation then proceeds by minimizing the distance between the empirical cross-spectral density of the data and the theoretical prediction:
$$f_{yz}(\omega) = J(\omega; \theta) f_{xz}(\omega)$$
where $f_{yz}(\omega)$ is the cross-spectrum between the outcomes and the shocks, and $f_{xz}(\omega)$ is the cross-spectrum between the sufficient statistics and the shocks.

This frequency-domain minimum distance estimator allows for the simultaneous use of multiple shocks and multiple aggregate outcomes. For example, a researcher might use both monetary and fiscal shocks to estimate parameters by requiring the model to match the households' responses to both types of disturbances.

## Statistical Inference and the Bootstrap

The structural parameters in HA models often enter the SSJs in highly nonlinear ways. This nonlinearity can make standard asymptotic inference (based on large-sample approximations) unreliable when using the finite datasets typically available in macroeconomics (e.g., $T=120$ quarters).

The authors demonstrate through simulations that standard analytical confidence intervals often fail to provide correct coverage. To solve this, they propose a Gaussian multiplier bootstrap procedure. This bootstrap method effectively reshuffles the residuals of the estimation to generate a distribution of the estimator, providing more accurate confidence intervals and more reliable over-identification tests in small samples. The over-identification test is particularly useful: it allows researchers to statistically test whether the internal structure of their chosen block is consistent with the observed data. If the test rejects, it suggests that the household block itself is misspecified, rather than the error coming from the unmodeled parts of the economy.

## Empirical Illustration: The HANK Household Block

To demonstrate the practical utility of the method, the authors apply it to a simulated economy based on a Two-Asset Heterogeneous Agent New Keynesian (HANK) model. In this model, households hold both liquid assets (like cash) and illiquid assets (like housing), and they face costs when moving funds between the two.

The goal is to estimate four key parameters:
1.  The elasticity of intertemporal substitution ($\gamma$), which dictates how much households shift consumption over time in response to interest rate changes.
2.  The discount factor ($\beta$), representing the households' patience.
3.  Two parameters ($\chi_0, \chi_1$) governing the costs of adjusting illiquid assets.

Using only 120 quarters of simulated data—a realistic sample size for quarterly macro data—the authors show that the limited-information estimator successfully recovers the true parameter values. Crucially, the estimation only uses the household block equations. The researchers do not need to know the Taylor rule used by the central bank or the production function of the firms in the simulation. This highlights the "limited-information" nature of the tool: it works even when the researcher is "ignorant" of the broader economic environment, provided the household block is correctly specified.

## Conclusion and Research Significance

The limited-information estimation framework presented in this paper offers a significant advancement in the empirical toolkit for macroeconomics. By decoupling the estimation of individual model blocks from the solution of the full general equilibrium, it provides several benefits:

*   **Robustness:** It prevents errors in modeling one sector (e.g., the government) from biasing estimates in another (e.g., households).
*   **Flexibility:** Researchers can focus their modeling efforts on the specific parts of the economy they care most about.
*   **Computational Efficiency:** It avoids the need to solve complex fixed-point problems for the entire economy during the estimation loop.
*   **Empirical Rigor:** It provides a formal way to test the specification of HA model components using standard macroeconomic shocks.

As HA models continue to grow in complexity, tools like this will be essential for ensuring that these models remain grounded in empirical reality. This method bridges the gap between purely reduced-form evidence and fully structural, but potentially fragile, general equilibrium models.
