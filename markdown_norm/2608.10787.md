## Understanding Optimal Stratification in Survey Design

In official statistics and survey methodology, a primary goal is to produce accurate estimates of population characteristics while minimizing the costs associated with data collection. One of the most effective techniques for achieving this is stratified sampling. In this approach, a population (or a sampling frame) of $L$ atomic units is partitioned into $K$ non-overlapping subgroups, called strata. Samples are then drawn independently from each stratum.

The challenge lies in finding the "optimal" way to group these units. When strata are homogeneous—meaning the units within each stratum are very similar to one another regarding the variables of interest—the variance of the survey estimates decreases. This allow researchers to achieve the desired level of precision with a smaller total sample size $n$. However, finding the absolute best partition is a difficult combinatorial optimization problem. As the number of atomic units $L$ and the desired number of final strata $K$ increase, the number of possible ways to group them grows exponentially, making exhaustive search impossible.

Historically, this problem has been addressed using classical heuristics. The current standard in many statistical offices is the genetic algorithm (GA), such as the one implemented in the R package `SamplingStrata`. This research explores whether emerging computational paradigms—specifically quantum annealing, gate-based quantum computing, and photonic quantum-inspired devices—can offer practical advantages over these established classical methods.

## The Bethel-Chromy Objective and the Formulation Challenge

The "true" objective in optimal stratification is to minimize the total sample size $n$ required to satisfy a set of precision constraints, usually expressed as target coefficients of variation (CV) for multiple variables. The calculation of this sample size is performed using the Bethel-Chromy algorithm. This algorithm determines the optimal allocation of the sample across the chosen strata.

A significant hurdle for quantum approaches is that the Bethel-Chromy objective is not a simple mathematical function. Quantum computers and many quantum-inspired solvers typically require the problem to be formulated as a Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) problem. A QUBO represents the problem as a polynomial of degree two, involving binary variables. Because the Bethel sample size cannot be directly expressed as a low-degree polynomial, researchers must use a "surrogate" objective that is compatible with quantum hardware.

In this study, the authors used a population-weighted pairwise dispersion surrogate. The goal of this surrogate is to minimize the sum of distances between units assigned to the same stratum, weighted by the sizes of the units. By minimizing this dispersion, the solver naturally seeks more homogeneous groupings, which acts as a proxy for minimizing the final sample size.

## Mapping Stratification to Quantum Hardware

To solve the stratification problem on quantum or quantum-inspired devices, the assignment of $L$ atomic strata to $K$ final strata must be encoded into binary variables. Two primary encoding schemes were evaluated:

1.  **One-Hot Encoding:** This is the most common approach for quantum annealers and photonic devices. For every atomic stratum $h$ and every potential final stratum $k$, a binary variable $x_{hk}$ is created. If $x_{hk} = 1$, atomic stratum $h$ is assigned to final stratum $k$. To ensure each unit is assigned to exactly one stratum, a constraint $\sum_{k=1}^K x_{hk} = 1$ is added as a penalty term to the objective function.
2.  **Binary-Label Encoding:** Used primarily for gate-based quantum processors (like those from IBM), this scheme represents the stratum index $k$ using a bitstring. This requires fewer qubits but results in a more complex Hamiltonian (the mathematical representation of the energy of the system) with higher-order interaction terms.

![figure-4-computational-workflows-for-the-different-platforms-the-diagrams-distinguish-between-classical-pre-post-processing-steps-and-the-genuinely-quantum-or-quantum-inspired-optimization-steps](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10787v1/figures/fig4.png)

The full QUBO objective function used in the study is defined as:

$$Q(X) = Q_{disp}(X) + \lambda \sum_h \left( \sum_k x_{hk} - 1 \right)^2$$

Where $Q_{disp}(X)$ represents the weighted dispersion surrogate:

$$Q_{disp}(X) = \sum_k \sum_{h < h'} w_{hh'} x_{hk} x_{h'k}$$

In this formulation, $w_{hh'} = \sqrt{N_h N_{h'}} d_{hh'}$ is the weighted distance between units $h$ and $h'$, where $d_{hh'}$ is the Euclidean distance between their feature vectors $u_h$. The penalty coefficient $\lambda$ is carefully chosen, here set as $\lambda = 3 \times \max(w_{hh'})$, to ensure that the "one-hot" constraints are respected without making the optimization landscape too difficult for the hardware to navigate.

## Comparative Results: Identifying the Performance Gaps

The researchers conducted a case study using a dataset of 589 Swiss municipalities grouped into 20 atomic strata, with the goal of partitioning them into $K=4$ final strata. The results revealed two distinct gaps in performance:

### The Formulation Gap
The classical genetic algorithm (GA) achieved a sample size of $n = 129$. The "best-known" solution for the surrogate QUBO objective was found using a powerful classical GPU-based solver (Fixstars Amplify AE), resulting in a sample size of $n = 160$. 

This 24% difference ($160$ vs $129$) represents the **formulation gap**. It demonstrates that even if a quantum computer finds the perfect mathematical solution to the surrogate objective, the resulting stratification is still less efficient than the one found by the classical GA optimizing the true Bethel objective. This highlights a fundamental limitation: the surrogate objective is only an approximation of the statistical goal.

### The Solver Gap
The quantum hardware itself struggled to match the classical GPU-based control solver even when minimizing the *same* surrogate objective. The gate-based IBM Quantum processor (using the QAOA algorithm) resulted in $n = 262$, and the photonic QCi Dirac-3 resulted in $n = 286$. 

These results indicate a **solver gap**. The underperformance of the quantum devices is attributed to several factors:
*   **Connectivity:** Gate-based systems often have sparse connectivity between qubits. Mapping the "all-to-all" interactions of a clustering problem onto a sparse hardware lattice requires many "SWAP" gates, which increase the circuit depth and accumulate errors (noise).
*   **Budget and Scale:** Free-tier access limits the number of iterations allowed for the variational optimization (like COBYLA in QAOA).
*   **Hardware Maturity:** Current devices are susceptible to decoherence and gate noise, which degrades the signal as the complexity of the problem increases.

## The Role of Hardware Connectivity

A key finding of the paper is the impact of hardware architecture on performance. Problems like stratification and clustering are "dense" problems, meaning every unit potentially interacts with every other unit. 

On an IBM quantum processor with a "heavy-hex" layout, qubits can only interact with their immediate neighbors. To allow distant qubits to interact, the system must move information across the chip using SWAP gates. For the 20-stratum problem, this resulted in a circuit with over 4,000 two-qubit gates. In contrast, architectures with all-to-all connectivity (like trapped-ion systems or certain photonic designs) would require significantly fewer gates, potentially preserving the quantum signal more effectively.

## Conclusion and Future Outlook

The study concludes that for the practical problem of optimal stratification, classical genetic algorithms currently remain the superior choice. This is primarily because they can optimize the true statistical objective (Bethel sample size) directly, whereas quantum and quantum-inspired methods are currently forced to use surrogate objectives that introduce a significant performance penalty.

However, the research provides a clear roadmap for future development:
1.  **Objective Refinement:** To bridge the formulation gap, new mathematical encodings are needed that better approximate the Bethel-Chromy criteria within a quadratic or low-degree polynomial framework.
2.  **Architecture Selection:** For dense combinatorial problems, hardware with high connectivity (such as trapped-ion or photonic systems) appears more promising than current sparse superconducting lattices.
3.  **Hybrid Approaches:** The success of the "quantum-inspired" Amplify AE solver suggests that using the Ising/QUBO formulation on specialized classical hardware (like GPUs) can be a robust intermediate step, offering fast solutions even if they do not yet outperform direct GA optimization.

By providing a rigorous, side-by-side comparison of these diverse technologies, the paper establishes a baseline for future work and helps researchers manage expectations for "quantum advantage" in the field of survey statistics.
