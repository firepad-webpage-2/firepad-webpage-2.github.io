## Correlation Effects in Multiorbital Systems

The physics of strongly correlated electron systems is often defined by the competition between the kinetic energy of electrons, which favors a metallic state, and electron-electron interactions, which favor localized states. In single-band systems, the Hubbard interaction $U$ drives this competition, leading to the well-known Mott metal-insulator transition. A traditional way to describe the metallic side of this transition is the Brinkman-Rice (BR) scenario. In the BR picture, as correlations increase, the quasiparticle renormalization factor $Z$ (or $z_L$) decreases, signifying an increase in the effective mass $m^*$ and a uniform suppression of the spectral weight across the entire conduction band.

However, in multiorbital materials—such as iron-based superconductors, ruthenates, and molybdates—the situation is more complex. These materials are often categorized as "Hund metals." In these systems, Hund's coupling $J$, which favors high-spin configurations, plays a role as significant as, or even more significant than, the Hubbard $U$. Hund's coupling introduces a phenomenon known as low-energy effectiveness (LEE). Unlike the uniform renormalization of the BR scenario, Hund metals exhibit a "Janus-faced" character: they show strong quasiparticle renormalization and heavy-fermion behavior at very low energies near the Fermi level, yet they maintain a broad, incoherent spectral weight at higher energies, remaining far from the Mott insulating state.

This research addresses a long-standing challenge: how to move beyond qualitative descriptions of LEE and provide a universal, quantitative measure of "Hundness." While the quasiparticle renormalization factor $z_L$ is a standard measure of correlation, it cannot distinguish between the uniform effects of $U$ and the selective, low-energy effects of $J$. The authors propose a new framework that introduces a high-energy correlation factor, $z_H$, to complement $z_L$. The discrepancy between these two factors serves as a universal signature of Hund-driven correlations.

## The Divergence from the Brinkman-Rice Scenario

In the standard BR picture for a single-band Hubbard model, the entire conduction band scales uniformly. If the quasiparticle peak at the Fermi level is narrowed by a factor of $z_L$, the intensity of the rest of the band is suppressed by a similar amount. Mathematically, this implies that the low-energy renormalization and the high-energy suppression of charge fluctuations are tightly coupled. In this context, $z_L \approx z_H$.

Multiorbital systems with significant Hund's coupling $J$ break this symmetry. As $J$ increases, the system can enter a regime where $z_L$ is very small (indicating "heavy" electrons and strong low-energy correlations), but $z_H$ remains relatively large (indicating that high-energy charge fluctuations are still active). This is the hallmark of LEE. The physical density of states (DOS) in such a system shows a very narrow peak at the Fermi level, but the broad "wings" of the band are much more prominent than what the BR scenario would predict.

The self-energy $\Sigma(\omega)$ also reflects this dichotomy. In the BR scenario, the real part of the self-energy, $\text{Re}\Sigma(\omega)$, typically shows a linear slope over a wide energy range. In contrast, in a Hund metal, the steep slope of $\text{Re}\Sigma(\omega)$—which determines the mass enhancement—is confined to a very narrow energy window near $\omega = 0$. Outside this window, the self-energy flattens out, allowing high-energy electrons to behave more like they do in a weakly correlated system.

## A Quantitative Framework for Hundness

To quantify these observations, the researchers defined two distinct factors. The low-energy correlation factor, $z_L$, is the standard quasiparticle renormalization factor calculated from the self-energy:

$$z_L = \left( 1 - \frac{\partial \text{Re}\Sigma(\omega)}{\partial \omega} \bigg|_{\omega=0} \right)^{-1}$$

A value of $z_L = 1$ corresponds to a non-interacting system, while $z_L \to 0$ indicates a transition to an insulating or heavy-fermion state.

The high-energy correlation factor, $z_H$, is a new metric designed to measure the suppression of charge fluctuations. It is derived from the probability $P(N_{\text{occ}})$ that a local site has the nominal average number of electrons. In a non-interacting system, the electrons are distributed among the orbitals according to a binomial distribution. The probability of the nominal occupancy in this "free" limit is:

$$P_{\text{free}}(N_{\text{occ}}) = \frac{\binom{2M}{N_{\text{occ}}}}{2^{2M}}$$

where $M$ is the number of orbitals. In the atomic limit, where charge fluctuations are completely suppressed, the site always has exactly $N_{\text{occ}}$ electrons, so $P_{\text{atomic}}(N_{\text{occ}}) = 1$. The researchers defined $z_H$ by interpolating the observed probability between these two limits:

$$P(N_{\text{occ}}) = z_H P_{\text{free}}(N_{\text{occ}}) + (1 - z_H) P_{\text{atomic}}(N_{\text{occ}})$$

Rearranging this gives the quantitative definition for $z_H$:

$$z_H = \frac{1 - P(N_{\text{occ}})}{1 - P_{\text{free}}(N_{\text{occ}})}$$

Under this definition, $z_H = 1$ in a non-interacting system and $z_H = 0$ when charge fluctuations are entirely frozen. By comparing $z_L$ and $z_H$, one can see if the system follows the BR picture ($z_H \approx z_L$) or if it exhibits Hundness ($z_H > z_L$).

## Microscopic Origin: Spin-Degree-of-Freedom Effectiveness

The study provides a microscopic explanation for why $z_H > z_L$ occurs in Hund metals, using the concept of Spin-Degree-of-Freedom Effectiveness (SDFE). In a multiorbital system, the Hubbard $U$ penalizes any deviation from the nominal charge $N_{\text{occ}}$, thus suppressing $\chi_C$ (charge susceptibility) and $z_H$ simultaneously. However, Hund's coupling $J$ acts differently. It promotes a high-spin state within the nominal occupancy sector, which "locks" the spins and significantly reduces the Kondo temperature—the energy scale below which quasiparticles become coherent.

This spin-locking effect strongly suppresses the low-energy scale, leading to a small $z_L$, but it does not exert a strong penalty on charge fluctuations. The researchers demonstrated this by showing a one-to-one correspondence between the correlation factors and susceptibilities: $z_H$ is directly related to the charge susceptibility $\chi_C$, while $z_L$ is determined by the normalized inverse spin susceptibility $\chi_S^{-1} |S_{\text{eff}}|^2$.

In Hund metals, $J$ reduces $\chi_S$ (making it "effective" at low energies) while having a negligible effect on $\chi_C$. This results in a phase diagram where $z_L$ can be pushed toward zero by $J$ while $z_H$ stays high. This decouple of spin and charge dynamics is the fundamental origin of the LEE signature.

## Validating Hundness in Real Materials

The proposed framework was tested on a wide variety of materials using Density Functional Theory combined with Dynamical Mean-Field Theory (DFT+DMFT). The researchers looked at iron-based superconductors (Fe-SCs), transition-metal oxides like SrVO$_3$ and SrCrO$_3$, and the chalcogenide NiS$_2$.

The results confirmed the universality of the $z_H > z_L$ signature:
1.  **Iron-Based Superconductors:** Materials like FeTe and LaFePO consistently fell deep into the "Hund region" of the phase diagram ($z_H \gg z_L$), confirming their classification as Hund metals.
2.  **SrVO$_3$:** As a $d^1$ system, Hund's coupling has no partner orbital to interact with in the ground state. Consequently, SrVO$_3$ follows the BR scenario, with $z_H \approx z_L$.
3.  **NiS$_2$:** By comparing calculations with and without $J$, the authors showed that $J$ is the specific driver for the discrepancy. With $J$, NiS$_2$ shows $z_H > z_L$. When $J$ is set to zero (keeping $U$ and other parameters constant), the system moves back toward the $z_H \approx z_L$ line.
4.  **Spin-Orbit Coupling (SOC):** The framework remains valid for heavy elements like Osmium in BaOsO$_3$, where strong SOC is present. In these cases, $J$ still promotes higher moment states (in the total moment basis), maintaining the $z_H > z_L$ signature.

## Experimental Implications and Conclusion

One of the most significant aspects of this work is the potential for experimental verification. While $z_L$ is readily accessible through measurements of the specific heat or the linear term in resistivity, $z_H$ is more elusive because it describes high-energy charge states rather than low-energy quasiparticles.

The authors suggest that core-level spectroscopies, such as X-ray photoemission spectroscopy (XPS) or X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS), are ideal tools for measuring $z_H$. These techniques probe the local electronic environment on a very fast timescale, essentially taking a "snapshot" of the local occupancy distribution $P(N)$. By extracting $P(N_{\text{occ}})$ from XPS spectra, experimentalists could calculate $z_H$ and directly plot materials on the $z_H$ vs. $z_L$ phase diagram.

In summary, this research provides a precise, quantitative definition of Hundness that resolves the ambiguity of previous qualitative models. By introducing the high-energy correlation factor $z_H$ and linking it to the suppression of charge fluctuations, the work clarifies how Hund's coupling creates a unique metallic state that is distinct from the traditional Mott-Hubbard picture. This framework not only helps in classifying existing materials but also provides a roadmap for designing new materials with specific correlated properties, such as high-temperature superconductivity or heavy-fermion behavior.
