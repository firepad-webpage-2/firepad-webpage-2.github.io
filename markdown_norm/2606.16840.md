## Understanding Extreme Co-movements in Crypto Markets

The inclusion of cryptocurrencies in investment portfolios is often justified by the promise of diversification. Proponents argue that because these digital assets operate on different technological foundations—ranging from payment tokens to smart-contract platforms—their returns should be sufficiently decorrelated from traditional finance and, ideally, from each other. However, the true test of diversification occurs not during periods of steady growth, but during market stress. When markets crash, assets that usually move independently often begin to fall in unison, a phenomenon known as tail dependence.

Recent research into the cryptocurrency ecosystem reveals a complex and evolving structure of extreme co-movements. While individual assets like Bitcoin (BTC) and Ethereum (ETH) are known to exhibit heavy-tailed downside risk, the way these risks propagate through the entire market has been difficult to map. Traditional econometric tools often struggle to isolate direct relationships from those mediated by a common market trend. Furthermore, there has been significant debate over whether cryptocurrencies crash together more intensely than they rally together—an "asymmetry" in risk that has profound implications for portfolio management.

This study addresses these challenges by applying Hüsler-Reiss (HR) graphical models of extremes to the cryptocurrency market. By benchmarking these results against standard Gaussian models, the research demonstrates that the market structure during extreme events is fundamentally different from ordinary conditions. During crashes, the functional categories that typically distinguish one token from another—such as "Currency" or "Infrastructure"—largely dissolve, leaving a highly interconnected web of systemic risk.

## Mapping the Architecture of Extremes

To understand how cryptocurrencies move together, researchers distinguish between "ordinary" co-movement and "extremal" dependence. Ordinary co-movement is typically measured by correlation, which describes the average relationship between two assets. Extremal dependence, however, focuses on the "tails" of the distribution—the rare 1% or 5% of days where prices move drastically.

A key challenge in this field is identifying *direct* links. If Bitcoin falls and both Litecoin and Solana fall as a result, does Solana fall because of a direct link to Bitcoin, or is the relationship mediated through Litecoin? Standard correlation cannot easily answer this. The research employs graphical models, where assets are represented as nodes and direct conditional dependencies are represented as edges. In this framework:
- A **Gaussian Graphical Model (GGM)** represents ordinary conditions. A missing edge between two assets means they are independent, provided we know the state of all other assets in the system.
- A **Hüsler-Reiss Graphical Model (HR-GGM)** represents the joint tail. A missing edge here means that the assets do not directly crash (or rally) together once the influence of the rest of the market is removed.

The strength of these extreme links is quantified by the extremal correlation $\chi_{ij}$, defined as:

$$\chi_{ij} = \lim_{u \to 1} P(U_i > u | U_j > u)$$

where $U_i$ and $U_j$ are the standardized returns of two assets. A value of $1$ indicates perfect co-movement in the tail, while $0$ indicates that the assets are independent at extreme levels. The HR model relates these correlations to a variogram matrix $\Gamma$, where:

$$\Gamma_{ij} = E[(\log Z_i - \log Z_j)^2]$$

Smaller values in the variogram indicate stronger tail co-movement. By estimating these structures across 13 major cryptocurrencies, the study provides a high-resolution map of the market's "DNA" under pressure.

## Crashing Together: The Dissolution of Sectors

One of the most striking findings of the research is the near-total connectivity of the cryptocurrency market during downturns. In the lower-tail (crash) network, the edge density averages roughly 0.87. This means that, on average, every asset is directly linked to almost every other asset in the market when prices fall sharply.

This density has a significant consequence: the dissolution of functional categories. Under normal market conditions, a Gaussian model shows a degree of "modularity"—a clustering where tokens of the same type (e.g., payment tokens like XRP and XLM) move together more closely than tokens of different types. However, during extreme crashes, this modularity drops to near zero.

In a crash, the market behaves as a single, monolithic block. Whether an asset is a "smart-contract platform" or a "meme coin" becomes irrelevant; they all crash together. This suggests that the internal diversification strategies often touted by crypto-focused funds—such as balancing a portfolio across different "sectors" of the crypto economy—are largely ineffective during the very events they are meant to protect against.

## Rallying Apart: The Emergence of Asymmetry

While the market crashes as a unit, it does not rally with the same level of cohesion. The research identifies a clear "downside asymmetry": the tendency for assets to fall together is consistently stronger than their tendency to rise together. 

This asymmetry is not a static property but has evolved over time. Early in the data (around 2021), the upper-tail (rally) network was actually denser than the lower-tail network. However, as the market matured and experienced various cycles—including the collapse of Terra/Luna and FTX—the upper tail began to "thin out." By 2024, the lower-tail density remained high, but the upper-tail density had decreased significantly. 

This "rallying apart" suggests that the crypto market is becoming more discerning during positive periods. Individual project developments, institutional adoption of specific tokens, or utility-driven news may be causing certain assets to decouple during rallies. However, no such decoupling is observed during crashes, where the "fear" appears to be more systemic and less asset-specific. For a pair like Bitcoin and Ethereum, the lower-tail extremal correlation is approximately $\chi_{ij} \approx 0.78$, while the upper-tail correlation is lower, at $\chi_{ij} \approx 0.72$.

## The Core-Periphery Hierarchy

Despite the high connectivity, the market is not a flat landscape. The research identifies a persistent hierarchy dominated by a core composed of Bitcoin (BTC) and Ethereum (ETH). These two assets form the strongest conditional pair in both tails and act as the primary anchors for the rest of the market.

At the other end of the spectrum is Tron (TRX), which consistently occupies the periphery of the network. Tron exhibits the weakest extremal correlations with the BTC-ETH core, with average values around $\bar{\chi} \approx 0.65$ in the lower tail and $\bar{\chi} \approx 0.59$ in the upper tail. While still highly connected, Tron’s relative isolation suggests it is less susceptible to the immediate, direct contagion that binds the rest of the major tokens.

## Why Standard Models Underestimate Risk

Perhaps the most critical contribution of this work is the demonstration of how standard financial models fail to capture systemic risk. Most traditional risk management tools rely on the Gaussian copula, which assumes that as events become more extreme, assets eventually become independent (a property called asymptotic independence).

The empirical data in this study proves the opposite: cryptocurrencies exhibit **asymptotic dependence**. As we move deeper into the tails (from the 90th percentile to the 99th percentile), the correlations do not decay to zero; they remain high and stable.

![joint-exceedance](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.16840v1/joint_exceedance.png)
*{fig:joint-exceedance}: The probability of multiple assets crashing simultaneously. The orange line shows empirical observations, while the dashed blue line shows the predictions of a Gaussian model. The gap between them illustrates the severe understatement of systemic risk by traditional models.*

The researchers calculated the probability of a market-wide crash where $k$ assets simultaneously breach their 5% loss threshold. The understatement by the Gaussian model is stark:
- For $8$ or more assets crashing together, the Gaussian model understates the probability by roughly **8-fold**.
- For all $13$ assets crashing together, the understatement grows even larger.
- At the 1% threshold, the Gaussian model can understate the risk of a total market collapse by as much as **30-fold**.

For an equal-weighted portfolio, the 99% Expected Shortfall (a measure of the average loss in the worst 1% of cases) was $3.19$ empirically, compared to $2.65$ predicted by the Gaussian model. While a $20\%$ difference might seem manageable, in the context of high-volatility assets, this miscalculation can lead to catastrophic liquidations and systemic failures.

The probability of such events is calculated as:

$$P(\text{count} \geq k) = \sum_{j=k}^{d} P(\text{exactly } j \text{ assets exceed threshold})$$

where $d$ is the number of assets. As {fig:joint-exceedance} illustrates, the empirical curve (orange) stays significantly higher than the Gaussian prediction (blue) as $k$ increases, highlighting the "illusion of diversification" that traditional models provide.

## Implications for the Future of Crypto Finance

The findings of this research offer a sobering look at the reality of cryptocurrency risk. The near-complete connectivity during crashes implies that internal diversification within the crypto space is largely a myth during times of crisis. Investors seeking to mitigate downside risk must look outside the crypto ecosystem, as sectoral labels like "Payment" or "Smart Contract" offer no protection when the core of the market—Bitcoin and Ethereum—experiences a tail event.

For regulators, the study identifies the BTC-ETH pair as the critical nexus for monitoring systemic risk. The dynamic nature of the extremal networks also suggests that surveillance tools should account for shifts in market structure over time rather than assuming a fixed correlation. As the market continues to evolve and "rally apart," the decoupling seen in positive periods might eventually extend to downturns, but for now, the rule remains: when the giants of the market crash, they bring everyone else with them.
