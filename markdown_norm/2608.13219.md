## Understanding Earth's Climate Feedback Estimates

Estimating how much the Earth will warm in response to greenhouse gas emissions is a central challenge in climate science. This sensitivity is largely determined by climate feedbacks—processes that either amplify or dampen the initial warming caused by radiative forcing. The climate feedback parameter, denoted as $\lambda$, represents the change in the Earth's net energy balance per degree of global temperature increase. An accurate estimation of $\lambda$ is essential for narrowing the range of Equilibrium Climate Sensitivity (ECS), which the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) currently places between 2.5 K and 4 K.

A common approach to studying these feedbacks involves using Atmospheric General Circulation Models (AGCMs) forced by observed historical sea-surface temperatures (SSTs) and sea-ice concentrations (SIC). These are often called `amip-piForcing` simulations. In these experiments, researchers typically keep atmospheric constituents, like greenhouse gases and aerosols, fixed at pre-industrial levels, assuming that the effect of historical forcing is already "encoded" in the prescribed SST patterns. This methodology has led many to conclude that Earth's feedback has become significantly more negative (more stabilizing) in recent decades due to specific warming patterns in the Pacific Ocean—a phenomenon known as the "pattern effect."

However, new research by Gyuleva et al. suggests that this widely used method may be compromised by two major issues: the omission of direct atmospheric forcing and the introduction of statistical noise inherent in the way feedbacks are calculated. By using a "perfect model" approach—where AGCMs are forced with SSTs generated by a coupled climate model rather than observations—the authors demonstrate that current feedback estimates might be systematically biased.

## The Forcing-Feedback Framework

To understand the study's critique, it is necessary to examine the standard energy balance model used in climate science. The Earth's net energy imbalance $N$ (the difference between incoming solar radiation and outgoing longwave radiation) is modeled as the sum of the external radiative forcing $F$ and the planet's radiative response $R$:

$$
N = F + R
$$

The radiative response $R$ is typically approximated as a linear function of the global mean surface air temperature change $\Delta T$:

$$
R = \lambda \Delta T
$$

In this framework, the feedback parameter $\lambda$ is calculated as the slope of the regression of the radiative response (where $R = N - F$) against temperature:

$$
\lambda(t) = \frac{\partial(N-F)}{\partial T}
}
$$

In `amip-piForcing` simulations, because atmospheric composition is held constant at 1850 levels, researchers assume $F$ is zero. Therefore, any change in $N$ is attributed entirely to the feedback response driven by the prescribed SSTs. Gyuleva et al. tested whether this assumption holds true when compared to the original coupled models where the SSTs originated.

## The Bias of Missing Forcing

The authors found that ignoring historical changes in atmospheric composition—specifically aerosols—introduces a significant bias. When they compared `amip-piForcing` runs to the original coupled simulations (which included historical forcings), they observed a striking divergence in radiative fluxes.

While the SSTs were identical, the top-of-atmosphere (TOA) radiation in the `amip-piForcing` runs did not match the coupled runs. Specifically, the absence of historical greenhouse gas increases meant that the AGCMs were losing more heat to space via longwave radiation than the coupled models, which were "trapping" heat. Even more critical was the role of aerosols. Around 1970, a period of high aerosol loading, the discrepancy in the calculated $\lambda$ exceeded 1 W m$^{-2}$ K$^{-1}$.

When the authors repeated the experiment but included historical atmospheric forcing (a setup known as `amip-histForcing`), the agreement with the coupled models improved significantly. This indicates that SST patterns alone do not capture the full radiative state of the atmosphere. The "pattern effect" observed in `amip-piForcing` studies may therefore be partially an artifact of missing forcing, rather than a purely physical response to SST changes.

## Statistical Artifacts: The Yule-Slutzky Effect

Beyond physical forcing, the study identifies a fundamental statistical problem in how feedback evolution is diagnosed. Researchers typically calculate $\lambda$ using a 30-year moving-window regression. The study demonstrates that this method is prone to the Yule-Slutzky effect.

The Yule-Slutzky effect occurs when a filter (like a moving-average or a moving-window regression) is applied to random noise, creating the appearance of regular, low-frequency cycles or trends where none exist physically. By running Monte-Carlo simulations using simple white noise, the authors showed that 30-year regressions naturally produce multi-decadal variability in $\lambda$.

This finding is profound: it suggests that a significant portion of the "evolution" in climate feedback reported in recent literature could be indistinguishable from statistical noise. The spectra of these feedback time series show that spurious variability peaks at timescales of 60 to 100 years. Consequently, claiming that a 30-year trend in feedback is a physical response to a specific SST pattern is statistically risky, as the noise in the regression can easily mimic such a signal.

## Searching for the SST Pattern Effect

One of the most cited ideas in modern climate science is that certain SST patterns, like a cooling East Pacific or a strengthening tropical gradient, trigger a strong stabilizing feedback. Gyuleva et al. investigated this by looking at a 4000-year-long pre-industrial control simulation. In this "unforced" world, the climate varies naturally without any changes in CO$_{2}$ or aerosols.

If the pattern effect is a robust physical mechanism, periods where $\lambda$ naturally becomes more negative should be associated with consistent SST patterns across the globe. However, the study found no such consistency.

As shown in the composites above, when looking across many different 30-year windows of feedback change, there was no statistically significant SST pattern that occurred repeatedly. The "agreement fraction"—the likelihood that a specific region warms or cools consistently during feedback changes—was low. This suggests that in a coupled system, the relationship between global feedback and regional SST patterns is either too weak to overcome internal noise or is highly dependent on the specific state of the atmosphere at that time.

## Implications for Climate Science

The study by Gyuleva et al. highlights that the community's reliance on `amip-piForcing` simulations may lead to an overestimation of how much Earth's feedback has changed. The key takeaways for the research community are:

1.  **Prescribe Atmospheric Forcing:** Future studies attempting to reconstruct historical feedbacks must include time-varying atmospheric forcing (aerosols and greenhouse gases) in their AGCM runs. Relying solely on SSTs to represent the forced state of the climate is insufficient and leads to biased results.
2.  **Account for Statistical Noise:** The 30-year moving-window method is inherently noisy. Any detected trends in $\lambda$ must be rigorously tested against the possibility that they are mere products of the Yule-Slutzky effect.
3.  **The Pattern Effect is Elusive:** While idealized experiments show that SST patterns *can* influence feedbacks, detecting this effect in the noisy, transient historical record is much more difficult than previously thought. The signal-to-noise ratio in 30-year windows is likely too low to make definitive causal claims about SST-driven feedback evolution.

By identifying these methodological pitfalls, the researchers argue for a more cautious interpretation of observed feedback trends. If the Earth's feedback is actually less stabilizing than `amip-piForcing` models suggest, our current projections might be underestimating the severity of future warming. This work serves as a call for more robust diagnostic tools and a transition toward experimental setups that more faithfully represent the coupled nature of the Earth's climate system.
