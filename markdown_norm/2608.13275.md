The study of submodules in the Hardy space over the unit bidisk, denoted by $H^2(\mathbb{D}^2)$, is a central theme in multivariable operator theory and complex analysis. While the structure of submodules in a single variable is well-understood through Beurling’s Theorem, the multivariable case presents significant challenges due to the lack of a universal factorization into inner functions. Researchers instead rely on various operator-theoretic invariants to classify and distinguish these submodules.

This paper, authored by Yin Liu, Yufeng Lu, and Chao Zu, provides an explicit analysis of a specific family of submodules generated by polynomials of the form $z^k - w^k$. By computing a sequence of numerical invariants introduced by Rongwei Yang and analyzing the spectral properties of the associated core operator, the authors demonstrate how these tools capture distinct aspects of the module's structure.

## Understanding Submodules in the Hardy Space of the Bidisk

The Hardy space $H^2(\mathbb{D}^2)$ consists of holomorphic functions on the bidisk $\mathbb{D}^2$ whose power series coefficients are square-summable. This space is a Hilbert module over the polynomial ring $\mathbb{C}[z, w]$, where the module action is defined by multiplication. The fundamental objects of study are submodules $M \subset H^2(\mathbb{D}^2)$, which are closed subspaces invariant under multiplication by the coordinate variables $z$ and $w$.

In the one-variable Hardy space $H^2(\mathbb{D})$, Beurling's Theorem states that every non-zero submodule $M$ can be written as $M = \theta H^2(\mathbb{D})$, where $\theta$ is an inner function. However, in two variables, even simple submodules like the one generated by the polynomial $z - w$, denoted $[z - w]$, do not follow such a straightforward classification. This complexity necessitates the development of invariants—quantities that remain unchanged under module isomorphism—to categorize submodules.

One approach involves studying the restriction of the shift operators $S_1 f = zf$ and $S_2 f = wf$ to the submodule $M$, denoted as $R_1$ and $R_2$. The interaction between these operators, particularly their failure to commute in certain contexts or their behavior on "wandering spaces," provides deep insights into the geometry of $M$.

## Yang’s Numerical Invariants and the Monotonicity Conjecture

A significant set of invariants for submodules in $H^2(\mathbb{D}^2)$ is the sequence of numerical invariants $\{\Sigma_j(M)\}_{j \geq 0}$. These were introduced by R. Yang to quantify the operator-theoretic differences between submodules.

The first two invariants, $\Sigma_0$ and $\Sigma_1$, are defined using the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of the commutator of the restriction operators. Specifically:
$$\Sigma_0(M) = \|[R_2^*, R_1]\|_{HS}^2$$
For $j \geq 2$, the invariants are defined using the geometry of the "graded" wandering spaces. If we consider the subspaces $M \ominus zM$ and $M \ominus wM$, which represent the parts of the submodule not reachable by a single shift in $z$ or $w$ respectively, we can choose orthonormal bases $\{\varphi_n\}$ and $\{\psi_n\}$. The invariants $\Sigma_j$ are then defined by the sum of the squares of the inner products between shifted versions of these basis elements:
$$\Sigma_j(M) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty |\langle w^j \varphi_n, z^j \psi_n \rangle|^2, \quad j \geq 2$$

A long-standing hypothesis in this field is Yang's Monotonicity Conjecture, which posits that for any submodule $M$, the sequence $\{\Sigma_j(M)\}_{j \geq 0}$ is nonincreasing. While this has been verified for specific cases like $[z - w]$ and $[(z-w)^2]$, providing explicit computations for more general families of submodules remains a difficult task.

## Analyzing the Submodule Family $M_k = [z^k - w^k]$

The authors focus on the family of homogeneous submodules $M_k = [z^k - w^k]$ for integers $k \geq 2$. This family is particularly interesting because the generating polynomial $z^k - w^k$ can be factored into $k$ distinct linear factors:
$$z^k - w^k = \prod_{m=0}^{k-1} (z - e^{i\frac{2\pi m}{k}}w)$$
This structure differs from previously studied cases like $[(z-w)^n]$, where the factors are repeated. The core objective is to determine whether the numerical invariants can "detect" the parameter $k$ and whether they satisfy the monotonicity conjecture.

The computation of these invariants relies on the analysis of a sequence of matrices $A_n$, which are related to the projection onto the wandering spaces. For $M_k$, these matrices are Toeplitz matrices with a specific banded structure. Specifically, the entries of $A_n$ are determined by the inner products of the shifted generators. For the submodule $M_k$, the matrix $A_{n-1}$ has entries of $2$ on the main diagonal and $-1$ on the $k$-th diagonals (the diagonals $k$ steps away from the center), while all other entries are zero.

## The Residue-Class Decomposition of Toeplitz Matrices

The most significant analytical technique introduced in this work is the "residue-class decomposition" of the matrix $A_n$. Because the non-zero off-diagonal entries only appear at a distance $k$, the matrix $A_n$ can be simplified by reordering its indices according to their residue modulo $k$.

Consider the indices $\{0, 1, \dots, n-1\}$. We can group these indices based on their remainder when divided by $k$. For example, if $k=2$, we group even indices and odd indices. This permutation transforms the sparse, banded matrix $A_n$ into a block-diagonal matrix:
$$PA_{n-1}P^* = B_0 \oplus B_1 \oplus \dots \oplus B_{k-1}$$
Each block $B_s$ corresponds to a residue class $s \in \{0, 1, \dots, k-1\}$. The authors show that each of these blocks is essentially a standard tridiagonal matrix $T_m$ of the form:
$$T_m = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & & \\ -1 & 2 & \ddots & \\ & \ddots & \ddots & -1 \\ & & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$
Since the properties of $T_m$ (such as its determinant and inverse) are well-known, this decomposition allows for the explicit calculation of the determinant $D_n$ and the cofactors of $A_n$. For $n = kq + r$ (where $0 \leq r < k$), the determinant is given by:
$$D_n = (q + 2)^r (q + 1)^{k-r}$$

This explicit formula is a crucial step in calculating the higher-order invariants $\Sigma_j$ and the eigenvalues of the core operator.

## Spectral Properties of the Core Operator

The core operator $C = [R_2^*, R_1]$ is a fundamental operator that acts on the wandering space. Its spectrum (the set of eigenvalues) provides a way to characterize the submodule. A key finding of this paper is the determination of the spectrum of $C$ for the submodule $M_k$.

The authors prove that the eigenvalues of the core operator for $M_k$ are:
$$\{0, 1\} \cup \left\{ \pm \frac{1}{m+1} : m \geq 1 \right\}$$
Interestingly, these eigenvalues and their multiplicities are independent of the parameter $k$. This means that if we only look at the spectral data of the core operator, we cannot distinguish between $[z^2 - w^2]$, $[z^3 - w^3]$, or any other $M_k$. This result highlights a limitation of using only spectral data for submodule classification and motivates the use of the numerical invariants $\{\Sigma_j\}$.

## The Block-Repetition Phenomenon and Its Implications

The primary result of the paper is the explicit description of the numerical invariants for $M_k$. The authors uncover a "block-repetition" phenomenon, where the sequence $\{\Sigma_j(M_k)\}$ is constructed by repeating the values of the invariants of the simpler submodule $[z-w]$.

Specifically, for $j \geq 1$, the relationship is given by:
$$\Sigma_j(M_k) = \Sigma_{\lceil j/k \rceil}([z-w])$$
In this formula, $\lceil \cdot \rceil$ denotes the ceiling function. This implies that the sequence of invariants for $M_k$ consists of blocks of length $k$, where each block takes a constant value. For example, if $k=3$, the sequence would look like:
$$\Sigma_0(M_3), \underbrace{\Sigma_1, \Sigma_1, \Sigma_1}_{\text{Block 1}}, \underbrace{\Sigma_2, \Sigma_2, \Sigma_2}_{\text{Block 2}}, \dots$$
where $\Sigma_m$ are the invariants of $[z-w]$.

This result has several profound implications:

1.  **Verification of Yang's Conjecture:** Since the invariants of $[z-w]$ are known to be strictly decreasing, the sequence for $M_k$ is nonincreasing. This confirms Yang's Monotonicity Conjecture for the entire family $\{M_k : k \geq 2\}$.
2.  **Recovery of the Parameter $k$:** Unlike the spectral data, the numerical invariants allow for the unique identification of $k$. One can simply count the length of the first constant block of invariants to find $k$:
    $$k = \max \{j \geq 1 : \Sigma_j(M_k) = \Sigma_1(M_k)\}$$
3.  **Refined Classification:** The block-repetition pattern demonstrates that numerical invariants capture structural information (the degree and algebraic decomposition of the generator) that is invisible to the core operator's spectrum.

The following Python-like pseudocode illustrates the block-repetition logic for calculating the sequence:

## Significance and Future Directions

The work of Liu, Lu, and Zu provides a rigorous verification of a major conjecture for a significant class of submodules. By bridging the gap between algebraic properties (the polynomial $z^k - w^k$) and operator-theoretic invariants, they demonstrate the utility of numerical invariants as a sensitive tool for submodule classification.

The "residue-class decomposition" method provides a roadmap for analyzing other submodules generated by polynomials with similar periodic structures. Furthermore, the discovery that numerical invariants provide more information than spectral data encourages further research into the "invisible" properties of operators in multivariable settings. This study serves as a clear example of how specific, explicitly computable families can provide broader insights into the abstract structure of Hilbert modules.
