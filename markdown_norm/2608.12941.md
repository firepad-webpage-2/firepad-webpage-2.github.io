## Understanding Particle Resuspension and Macroscopic Degeneracy

Particle resuspension—the process by which small solid particles are detached and re-entrained into a fluid flow from a surface—is a fundamental phenomenon in fluid mechanics. It plays a significant role in diverse areas, ranging from the spread of environmental pollutants and respiratory pathogens to the safety protocols of nuclear reactors and the precision requirements of semiconductor manufacturing. Despite its importance, predicting the exact rate of resuspension remains difficult because it involves complex interactions between turbulent fluid forces and microscopic surface adhesion.

Researchers often use Lagrangian-stochastic models (LSMs) to simulate this process. These models treat the movement of a particle as a series of random steps influenced by the average properties of the surrounding turbulence. A common simplified metric used to evaluate these models is the Resuspended Fraction (RF), which represents the total percentage of particles that have left the surface after a certain amount of time.

A central challenge identified in this research is known as "macroscopic degeneracy." This occurs when two different mathematical models, based on different physical assumptions, produce the same macroscopic result (like the RF) after being calibrated. This suggests that the standard way of validating these models—by checking if the total number of detached particles matches observations—might be insufficient. It may mask the true, detailed physical pathways that lead to a particle detaching. This study investigates whether a more complex, "second-order" model can reveal hidden dynamics that a simpler "first-order" model misses, even when their final resuspension tallies are the same.

## Bridging Scales with Second-Order Stochasticity

The traditional first-order approach to modeling particle resuspension often relies on the Langevin equation, where the particle's velocity is treated as a stochastic process. In this study, the motion is modeled as a particle rolling along a surface. The change in the particle's angular velocity $\omega$ is governed by a moment balance:

$$M = I \frac{d\omega}{dt} = bF_D + \frac{a}{2}F_L - r_a F_a - \frac{a}{2}mg$$

In a first-order model, the fluctuating part of this angular velocity $\omega'(t)$ is typically modeled as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process (OUP). This means the velocity changes in response to a "white noise" force that is completely uncorrelated in time. The evolution of the velocity fluctuation $\omega'(t)$ is given by:

$$d\omega'(t) = -\omega'(t) \frac{dt}{T_L} + \sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2}{T_L}} dW(t)$$

where $T_L$ is a relaxation time and $dW(t)$ represents the random noise. While computationally efficient, this assumes that the forces acting on the particle have no "memory"—if a force pushes a particle at one microsecond, it tells you nothing about the force at the next microsecond.

The researchers propose a second-order model to address this. Instead of applying random noise directly to the velocity, they apply it to the tangential acceleration $\alpha'$. This creates a "memory" effect: the acceleration persists for a short time (the Kolmogorov time scale $\tau_\eta$), making the resulting velocity trajectories smoother and more physically realistic at very small time scales. The coupled equations for this second-order system are:

$$d\omega'(t) = -\omega'(t) \frac{dt}{T_L} + \left(\frac{2}{d_p}\right) \alpha'(t)dt$$
$$d\alpha'(t) = -\alpha'(t) \frac{dt}{\tau_\eta} + \sqrt{\frac{A_0 \langle\epsilon\rangle}{\tau_\eta^2}} dW(t)$$

By increasing the order of the model, the state of the particle is now defined by both its velocity and its acceleration. This allows the model to capture finite correlation times in the fluid forces, which is especially important in the region very close to the wall where turbulence is highly structured.

## Trajectory Analysis and Pre-Detachment Transport

The study focuses on the "pre-detachment" phase—the period when a particle is still technically attached to the surface but is moving or rolling due to fluid fluctuations. To determine when a particle is officially "resuspended," the authors use a detachment criterion: if a particle rolls a distance greater than the typical surface roughness (asperity spacing) within a single viscous wall time, it is considered detached.

One of the most striking findings is the existence of an "anomalous-transport window." For particles that have not yet detached (the "survivors"), the researchers calculated the mean-squared displacement (MSD). In simple diffusion, MSD grows linearly with time ($t^1$). However, the model shows that before detaching, particles undergo a period of rapid exploration where the MSD grows much faster—scaling with $t^2$ (ballistic) or even $t^3$ (super-ballistic).

This suggests that particles don't just sit still until a giant gust of wind knocks them off; instead, they undergo a complex "walk" on the surface, picking up speed and correlation before the final detachment event. As the turbulence intensity (measured by the friction Reynolds number $Re_\tau$) increases, this window of anomalous transport shifts and expands. This insight is invisible if one only looks at the final RF, as it describes the *pathway* to resuspension rather than just the outcome.

## Structural Fingerprints: Autocorrelations and Structure Functions

To distinguish between the first-order and second-order models, the researchers looked at the statistical "fingerprints" of the particle trajectories. These are mathematical tools used to quantify how smooth or "bursty" a particle's motion is.

### Differentiability and the Second-Order Structure Function
A key difference between the two models lies in the second-order structure function $S_2(\tau)$, which measures the average square of the change in velocity over a time lag $\tau$:

$$S_2(\tau) = \langle [\omega'(t+\tau) - \omega'(t)]^2 \rangle$$

For the first-order model, $S_2(\tau) \propto |\tau|$ at short times, which is the signature of a "rough," non-differentiable path (like a jagged zigzag). For the second-order model, $S_2(\tau) \propto \tau^2$ for $\tau \ll \tau_\eta$. This quadratic scaling indicates that the velocity path is "smooth" and differentiable. Even though both models describe the same bulk resuspension, the second-order model predicts a fundamentally different physical behavior at the smallest scales of motion.

### Intermittency and Kurtosis
The researchers also examined the third-order structure function $S_3(\tau)$ and the kurtosis $K(\tau)$.

$$S_3(\tau) = \langle [\omega'(t+\tau) - \omega'(t)]^3 \rangle$$
$$K(\tau) = \frac{S_4(\tau)}{[S_2(\tau)]^2}$$

$S_3$ measures the asymmetry of the velocity changes. Both models show a negative asymmetry, meaning that large, sudden decreases in rolling velocity are statistically more significant than increases during the pre-detachment phase. However, the second-order model shows much higher kurtosis ($K > 3$) at short time lags. In statistics, high kurtosis indicates "heavy tails"—meaning that extreme, rare events are more likely to happen than in a standard Gaussian distribution. This suggests that the second-order model captures the "intermittent" nature of turbulence—the sudden, intense bursts of force—more accurately than the first-order model.

## Implications for Future Modeling

The significance of this work lies in its demonstration that macroscopic observables are not enough to validate a physical model of a complex system. By exploring the macroscopic degeneracy between first- and second-order models, the authors have shown that:

1.  **Model Order Matters:** Augmenting a stochastic model with acceleration (second-order) changes the regularity of the particle paths, making them differentiable and introducing a finite memory that more closely mimics real fluid interactions.
2.  **Anomalous Transport is a Key Pathway:** The transition from ballistic to diffusive motion on the surface is a critical part of the resuspension process. Future models should aim to capture this "anomalous-transport window" to accurately predict how particles behave before they leave the surface.
3.  **Beyond the Mean:** Standard metrics like the Resuspended Fraction are "blind" to the multiscale statistics of trajectories. To truly understand and predict resuspension, researchers must look at structure functions, kurtosis, and the probability distributions of velocity increments.

This research provides a roadmap for developing more robust Lagrangian models. It suggests that for systems where the small-scale dynamics are as important as the bulk outcome—such as in high-precision manufacturing or the study of infectious aerosols—higher-order stochastic descriptions are necessary to resolve the true physics of the problem. By "breaking" the macroscopic degeneracy, this work moves the field toward models that are not just calibrated to fit the data, but are dynamically faithful to the underlying turbulent processes.
