In the study of topological spaces and the maps between them, researchers often seek to quantify the complexity of specific operations, such as motion planning or decomposing a space into simpler components. This paper investigates these concepts within the framework of equivariant homotopy theory, where spaces and maps are subject to symmetries represented by the action of a group $G$. Specifically, the authors introduce and explore the equivariant relative sectional category, a versatile invariant that unifies and extends several classical measures of topological complexity to settings involving group actions.

## Foundation: From Motion Planning to Symmetry

The study of topological complexity originated from the practical problem of robot motion planning. Given a space of possible configurations, a motion planning algorithm must produce a continuous path between any two points. In algebraic topology, this is formalized as the sectional category of the free path space fibration. A related concept, the Lusternik-Schnirelmann (LS) category, measures how many contractible open sets are needed to cover a space. Both of these invariants provide numerical values that describe the "intricacy" of a space's structure.

However, many physical and mathematical systems exhibit symmetries. For instance, a robotic arm might have identical segments, or a multi-agent system might operate in a symmetric environment. In these cases, it is more natural to view the system as a $G$-space—a topological space equipped with a continuous action of a group $G$. The invariants must then be adapted to respect these symmetries, leading to the equivariant LS-category and equivariant topological complexity.

The work by Daundkar, Santhanam, and Singh addresses a gap in this field by generalizing the relative sectional category to the equivariant setting. The relative version is particularly powerful because it allows for the analysis of complexity relative to a specific map $f$, rather than the entire space.

## Defining the Equivariant Relative Sectional Category

The central definition of the paper is the equivariant relative sectional category, denoted by $secat_{G,f}(p)$. Consider a $G$-map $p: E \to B$ and another $G$-map $f: K \to B$. The invariant $secat_{G,f}(p)$ is defined as the smallest non-negative integer $n$ such that the domain $K$ can be covered by $n + 1$ $G$-invariant open sets $U_i$.

For each set $U_i$, there must exist a $G$-equivariant local section $s_i: U_i \to E$. A $G$-equivariant map is one that commutes with the group action, meaning $s_i(g \cdot u) = g \cdot s_i(u)$ for all $g \in G$ and $u \in U_i$. The condition for these local sections is that they must satisfy $p \circ s_i \simeq_G f|_{U_i}$, where $\simeq_G$ denotes a $G$-homotopy. This means that, within each patch of the cover, the map $f$ can be "lifted" to the space $E$ through the map $p$ in a way that respects the symmetry $G$.

If no such finite integer $n$ exists, the category is defined to be infinite. This definition generalizes the classical relative sectional category by requiring all sets and maps to be compatible with the group action $G$.

## Homotopy Pullbacks and Theoretical Consistency

One of the significant achievements of this work is proving that this new relative invariant is consistent with the established equivariant sectional category $secat_G(p)$. The authors utilize the construction of a $G$-homotopy pullback $P_{f,p}$ to bridge these concepts.

For any cospan of $G$-maps $K \xrightarrow{f} B \xleftarrow{p} E$, the $G$-homotopy pullback $P_{f,p}$ consists of triples $(k, e, \gamma)$ where $k \in K$, $e \in E$, and $\gamma$ is a path in $B$ connecting $f(k)$ to $p(e)$. The authors demonstrate that the relative complexity of $f$ with respect to $p$ is identical to the absolute equivariant sectional category of the projection map $\tilde{p}: P_{f,p} \to K$. Mathematically, this is expressed as:
$$
secat_{G,f}(p) = secat_G(\tilde{p})
$$
If $p$ is a $G$-fibration, this simplifies further to $secat_{G,f}(p) = secat_G(f^*p)$, where $f^*p$ is the pullback of the fibration $p$ along $f$. This connection is vital because it allows the researchers to apply the entire existing toolbox of equivariant homotopy theory to analyze the new invariant.

## Structural Properties and Comparison Theorems

The paper establishes a suite of properties that confirm $secat_{G,f}(p)$ behaves predictably as a homotopy invariant. Key among these are the comparison and product inequalities.

The authors show that restricting the problem to a specific map $f$ generally simplifies the task: $secat_{G,f}(p) \leq secat_G(p)$. Furthermore, they prove a product inequality for two maps $f_1, f_2$ and two fibrations $p_1, p_2$:
$$
secat_{G, f_1 \times f_2}(p_1 \times p_2) \leq secat_{G, f_1}(p_1) + secat_{G, f_2}(p_2)
$$
This subadditivity is a hallmark of category-style invariants and suggests that the complexity of a combined system is at most the sum of the complexities of its parts.

Another powerful result relates the equivariant invariant to its behavior on fixed-point sets. If $H$ is a subgroup of $G$, the fixed-point sets $K^H$, $E^H$, and $B^H$ consist of elements that are left unchanged by the action of $H$. The authors prove that $secat_{H', f^H}(p^H) \leq secat_{G,f}(p)$ for suitable subgroups $H'$. This "descent" property is crucial for computations, as it often allows researchers to reduce a complex equivariant problem to a simpler non-equivariant one on the fixed-point sets.

## Establishing Bounds via Cohomology and Connectivity

Calculating the exact value of $secat_{G,f}(p)$ is often difficult. To address this, the paper provides both lower and upper bounds.

The lower bounds are derived using equivariant cohomology theories $h^*_G$. The authors show that if there exist cohomology classes $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_k \in \ker p^*$ such that their product, when pulled back to $K$ via $f$, is non-zero, then the complexity is at least $k$. Formally, if $f^*(\alpha_1 \smile \dots \smile \alpha_k) \neq 0$, then $secat_{G,f}(p) \geq k$. This provides a concrete algebraic method to prove that the complexity of a map is high.

For upper bounds, the authors provide a $Ganea$-type characterization. They show that $secat_{G,f}(p)$ is the least $n$ for which a certain map $\sigma: K \to *_{nB} E$ exists, where $*_{nB} E$ is the $(n+1)$-fold fibered join of $E$ over $B$. This characterization leads to a dimension-connectivity bound:
$$
secat_{G,f}(p) < \frac{hdim_G(K) + 1}{s + 1}
$$
Here, $hdim_G(K)$ is the equivariant dimension of the space $K$, and $s$ relates to the connectivity of the fibers of the map $p$. This bound offers a way to estimate the maximum possible complexity based on the geometric and topological dimensions of the spaces involved.

## Equivariant Topological Complexity of Maps

The researchers apply their general theory to define equivariant versions of the topological complexity of a map. This is a specific case where the goal is to understand the complexity of motion planning when the start and end points are constrained by a map $f$.

Two prominent definitions of topological complexity of a map exist in the literature: one by Scott and another by Murillo and Wu. In the equivariant setting, these are:
1.  **Scott's version** ($TC_{Sc}^G(f)$): Defined as the relative sectional category $secat_{G, f \times f}(\pi_Y)$, where $\pi_Y: Y^I \to Y \times Y$ is the standard path space fibration.
2.  **Murillo-Wu's version** ($TC_{MW}^G(f)$): Defined using the equivariant relative sectional category of the diagonal map $\Delta_X: X \to X \times X$.

A major finding of this paper is the proof that these two definitions are equivalent in the equivariant context:
$$
TC_{Sc}^G(f) = TC_{MW}^G(f)
$$
This equivalence unifies two different perspectives on the complexity of maps and ensures that researchers can use whichever definition is more convenient for their specific problem.

## Illustrative Examples and Significance

To demonstrate the utility of their theory, the authors provide concrete examples. One such example is the Hopf fibration $f: S^{2m+1} \to CP^m$ under an $S^1$ action. They calculate that $TC_{Sc}^G(f) = 1$. In a more complex scenario involving a $C_2$ group action (a simple symmetry swapping two components) on the product space $S^2 \times S^2$, they use their cohomological bounds to show that $cat_G(f) = 2$ and $TC_{Sc}^G(f) = 4$.

These results are significant because they provide a rigorous mathematical framework for quantifying the impact of symmetry on the complexity of maps. In fields like robotics, knowing $TC_{Sc}^G(f)$ informs the design of motion planning algorithms: a higher value indicates that no matter how we partition the task, the algorithm will inevitably face a certain number of discontinuities or "hard cases" that respect the system's symmetries.

By unifying the LS-category of maps and topological complexity of maps under the single umbrella of the equivariant relative sectional category, the authors have provided a more cohesive and powerful set of tools for the algebraic topology community. This work paves the way for deeper investigations into how symmetries govern the fundamental limits of computation and motion in structured spaces.
