The discovery of supermassive black holes (SMBHs) with masses exceeding $10^9 M_{\odot}$ existing less than a billion years after the Big Bang presents a significant challenge to modern astrophysics. Explaining how these objects grew so large so quickly requires either extremely rapid accretion throughout their lifetimes or the existence of "heavy seeds"—initial black holes with masses between $10^3 M_{\odot}$ and $10^6 M_{\odot}$.

A leading theory for creating these seeds is the formation of supermassive Population III (Pop III) stars. In the early universe, the first stars formed from metal-free primordial gas. While typical Pop III stars are thought to have masses around $100 M_{\odot}$, certain environmental conditions can lead to "supermassive" stars (SMSs) that reach $10^4-10^5 M_{\odot}$. These stars eventually collapse into direct collapse black holes (DCBHs). This paper systematically explores which environmental factors—specifically the Lyman–Werner (LW) radiation background and the assembly history of dark matter halos—most effectively trigger the formation of these heavy seeds.

## The Environment of Early Star Formation

Early star formation is primarily governed by the cooling properties of primordial gas. In the absence of metals, the main coolant is molecular hydrogen ($H_2$). However, $H_2$ is fragile and can be easily destroyed by Lyman–Werner (LW) radiation ($11.2\text{--}13.6 \text{ eV}$ photons). When a high LW background is present, $H_2$ cooling is suppressed, preventing gas from collapsing in small halos.

Collapse is delayed until the halo reaches the "atomic cooling" threshold, where the virial temperature is approximately $10^4 \text{ K}$. At this point, atomic hydrogen cooling becomes efficient. This delay allows the gas to fall into a much deeper gravitational potential, resulting in higher temperatures and higher gas inflow rates. If the accretion rate onto a central protostar remains high enough (typically $> 0.01\text{--}1 M_{\odot} \text{ yr}^{-1}$), the star stays "bloated" and cool, avoiding the intense ionizing feedback that would otherwise halt its growth.

## Simulating Halo Diversity

To understand the statistical likelihood of SMS formation, the researchers conducted 65 high-resolution cosmological zoom-in simulations using the AMR code **Enzo**. They selected 15 distinct dark matter halos from a larger volume to sample a wide variety of "assembly histories"—some halos grew slowly, while others experienced rapid mergers.

Each halo was simulated under several different intensities of LW radiation, parameterized by $J_{21}$ (where $J_{21} = 1$ corresponds to $10^{-21} \text{ erg s}^{-1} \text{ cm}^{-2} \text{ Hz}^{-1} \text{ sr}^{-1}$). The values ranged from $J_{21} = 0$ up to $J_{21} = 300$.

## Predicting Stellar Masses

Explicitly simulating the growth of a star from a protostellar seed up to $10^5 M_{\odot}$ is computationally prohibitive for a large sample of halos. To overcome this, the authors developed a novel post-processing prescription. They estimated the final mass $M_{\text{PopIII}}$ by analyzing the gas inflow rates at the moment the simulation reached its maximum refinement.

The fiducial enclosed mass inflow rate is defined as:
$$\dot{M}_{\text{infall}}(r) = \frac{M_{\text{gas}}(<r)}{t_{\text{infall}}(r)}$$
where $t_{\text{infall}}(r) = -r / v_r(r)$ is the characteristic time for gas at radius $r$ to reach the center.



The final stellar mass is calculated by integrating this accretion history until the inflow rate drops below a critical threshold $\dot{M}_{\text{crit}}$. This threshold was calibrated to $6.7 \times 10^{-3} M_{\odot} \text{ yr}^{-1}$ using previous simulations that included explicit protostellar particles.

## LW Radiation vs. Halo Assembly

A key question in the field is whether the "direct collapse" of gas is triggered by external radiation (LW) or by internal dynamics (rapid halo mergers). The simulations in this study show a clear winner: the LW radiation background.

The researchers found a statistically significant correlation between the predicted stellar mass and the LW intensity $J_{21}$. In contrast, there was no significant correlation with the halo assembly timescale. This implies that the thermal state of the gas—controlled by the suppression of molecular cooling—is the primary driver for high-mass star formation, rather than how quickly the dark matter halo itself is growing.

## Abundant Heavy Seeds?

The paper further combines these simulation results with a large-scale semi-analytic model (SAM) to estimate the cosmological density of these potential black hole seeds. The SAM tracks the formation of Pop III stars across different volumes while accounting for the spatially varying radiation fields.

Most Pop III stars form in halos with very low LW backgrounds. However, the study found that:
*   Approximately **91.6%** of Pop III-forming halos exist in "moderate" backgrounds ($1 \leq J_{21} < 10$).
*   Only **0.01%** of halos experience the extreme backgrounds ($J_{21} \geq 10$) usually required in "classical" direct collapse models.

If the threshold for forming supermassive stars is as low as $J_{21} \approx 10$ (rather than the $J_{21} \approx 1000$ often cited in older literature), then the number of potential heavy black hole seed sites is roughly $4.1 \times 10^{-3} \text{ cMpc}^{-3}$. 

Even more significantly, if the high accretion rates observed in the simulations at $J_{21} \sim 1$ can also lead to heavy seeds, the number density of these sites could jump to $57.2 \text{ cMpc}^{-3}$. This is nearly four orders of magnitude higher than conservative estimates.

## Significance and Outlook

This work suggests that heavy black hole seeds may be much more abundant than previously thought. By showing that moderate LW radiation ($J_{21} \approx 10$) is sufficient to trigger rapid gas inflow, the authors demonstrate that the conditions for supermassive star formation are not restricted to extremely rare environments.

The study highlights that the thermodynamic state of the halo at the time of collapse is the fundamental factor determining stellar mass. Any process that delays collapse until the halo reaches the atomic cooling threshold—whether it be LW radiation, baryon-dark matter streaming velocities, or dynamical heating—will likely result in a heavy black hole seed. This provides a more robust and flexible framework for explaining the early appearance of supermassive black holes in the universe.

Future work will need to refine these results by using simulations that explicitly model the fragmentation of gas and the radiative feedback from the growing protostar to determine the exact efficiency of this mass accumulation. However, this statistical survey provides a crucial step toward understanding the population-level behavior of the universe's first massive black holes.
