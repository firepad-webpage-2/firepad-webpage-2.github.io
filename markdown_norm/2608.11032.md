Polynomial Identity theory (PI-theory) is a central branch of associative algebra that classifies algebras based on the polynomial relations they satisfy. For a given algebra $A$, a polynomial $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ is called a polynomial identity if it evaluates to zero for every possible substitution of elements from $A$ into the variables. The set of all such identities forms a $T$-ideal, and the "size" of this set is measured quantitatively by the codimension sequence $c_n(A)$. A fundamental invariant derived from this sequence is the PI-exponent, $\exp(A)$, which characterizes the exponential growth rate of the identities.

In recent years, this theory has been extended to algebras equipped with additional structures, such as derivations. A derivation is a linear map $d: A \to A$ that satisfies the Leibniz rule, $d(ab) = d(a)b + ad(b)$. When a Lie algebra $L$ acts on an associative algebra $A$ through derivations, we speak of differential identities or $L$-identities. These identities involve not only the variables $x_i$ but also their "derivatives" $x_i^d$ for $d \in L$. The study of these identities leads to the concept of $L$-varieties, $T_L$-ideals, and the differential codimension sequence $c_n^L(A)$.

The paper "Differential Varieties of Upper Triangular Matrices" by Daniela La Mattina and Carla Rizzo focuses on the algebra of $k \times k$ upper triangular matrices, denoted $UT_k$. In classical PI-theory, $UT_k$ is a cornerstone because it generates minimal varieties of a given exponent. Specifically, $UT_k$ generates a minimal variety of exponent $k$. The authors aim to establish a similar foundational understanding in the differential setting, particularly for the case $k=3$.

## Differential Action and Upper Triangular Matrices

To study the differential identities of $UT_k$, one must first understand the possible actions of a Lie algebra $L$ on it. A derivation $d$ is called "inner" if it can be represented as an adjoint action, $d = \text{ad}(a)$, where $\text{ad}(a)(b) = [a, b] = ab - ba$ for some $a \in UT_k$. A well-known result in matrix theory states that for the algebra of upper triangular matrices, all derivations are inner. This significantly simplifies the problem, as every derivation is induced by some element $a \in UT_k$.

Any such $a$ can be decomposed into two parts: a semisimple part $a_s$ (the diagonal entries) and a nilpotent part $a_n$ (the strictly upper triangular entries). Consequently, any derivation $\text{ad}(a)$ decomposes into a semisimple derivation $\text{ad}(a_s)$ and a nilpotent derivation $\text{ad}(a_n)$. 

The authors build upon a critical result established by Rizzo, which states that the $L$-exponent of an algebra is identical to its ordinary PI-exponent:
$$\exp_L(A) = \exp(A)$$
Since $\exp(UT_3) = 3$, it follows that any $L$-action on $UT_3$ will result in a differential exponent of 3. The research focuses on identifying which specific $L$-actions on $UT_k$ lead to "minimal" varieties of this exponent, which are the smallest varieties that maintain that specific growth rate.

## The Simplification Theorem for Minimal Varieties

One of the primary contributions of this work is a structural reduction theorem that constrains the types of Lie algebra actions that need to be considered for minimal varieties. The authors prove that if an $L$-variety of exponent $k$ generated by $UT_k$ is minimal, then the action of $L$ can be assumed to be semisimple without loss of generality. 

Specifically, they show that for any action $\theta: L \to \text{Der}(UT_k)$, the variety generated by $UT_k$ with the semisimple part of the action is contained within the variety generated by $UT_k$ with the full action. In the context of minimality, this implies that the nilpotent parts of the derivations do not contribute to the essential growth properties of the variety. This reduction allows researchers to focus exclusively on inner derivations induced by diagonal elements of $UT_k$, greatly simplifying the classification process.

## Fundamental Building Blocks of Exponent 3

The paper establishes that $UT_3$ serves as a fundamental building block for all $L$-varieties generated by upper triangular matrices of size $k \geq 3$. The authors demonstrate that for any $k \geq 3$ and any Lie algebra action on $UT_k$, the generated $L$-variety must contain $UT_3$ equipped with one of five specific semisimple actions (or the trivial action). These actions are defined by derivations induced by the following diagonal matrix units:
- $\epsilon_1 = \text{ad}(-e_{11})$
- $\epsilon_2 = \text{ad}(e_{33})$

The five specific semisimple $L$-actions identified are:
1. The trivial action (all derivations evaluate to zero).
2. Action via the one-dimensional Lie algebra $F\epsilon_1$.
3. Action via the one-dimensional Lie algebra $F\epsilon_2$.
4. Action via the one-dimensional Lie algebra $F(\epsilon_1 + \beta \epsilon_2)$ for $\beta \in F \setminus \{0\}$.
5. Action via the two-dimensional Lie algebra $F\epsilon_1 \oplus F\epsilon_2$.

By proving that these $UT_3$ $L$-algebras are embedded within any $UT_k$ $L$-variety, the authors justify a deep dive into the specific identities and codimension sequences of these five cases.

## Quantitative Analysis: Codimension Sequences

To characterize these $L$-algebras, the authors calculate the exact differential codimension sequence $c_n^L(UT_3)$ for each case. The codimension sequence measures the dimension of the space of multilinear differential polynomials of degree $n$ that are not identities. As $n \to \infty$, this sequence grows as $3^n$, but the polynomial coefficients and lower-order terms reveal the fine structure of the variety.

The authors use a rigorous five-step methodology for these calculations:
1. **Adapted Basis Selection**: Choosing a basis for $L$ that reflects the specific derivation action.
2. **Identification of Identities**: Proposing generators for the $T_L$-ideal and verifying them using matrix evaluations and the Leibniz rule.
3. **PBW Reduction**: Using the Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem to represent differential polynomials in terms of the universal enveloping algebra $U(L)$. This allows them to bound the number of non-trivial derivatives and the positions of variables.
4. **Tail Elimination**: Leveraging the existence of a identity element in $UT_3$ to simplify the counting of basis elements.
5. **Linear Independence and Counting**: Proving that the remaining polynomials are linearly independent via specific evaluations on matrix units $e_{ij}$ and counting them to find the formula for $c_n^L(UT_3)$.

## Algebraic Identities for UT3 L-algebras

The explicit results for the $T_L$-ideals and codimensions provide a complete map of the differential identity landscape for $UT_3$. For example, when the action is trivial, the $T_L$-ideal is generated by $x^{d_i} = 0$ for all $d_i \in L$ and the standard PI-identities of $UT_3$. The codimension sequence in this case is:
$$c_n^L(UT_3) = (n^2 - 7n + 9)3^{n-2} + (3n - 6)2^{n-1} + 3$$

When $L$ acts via a single derivation $d_1 = \text{ad}(-e_{11})$, the identities include the relation $x_i^{d_1^2} - x_i^{d_1} = 0$, which reflects the fact that the derivation $\text{ad}(-e_{11})$ has eigenvalues 0 and 1 (or -1, depending on the convention) when acting on the basis of $UT_3$. The codimension for this action is:
$$c_n^L(UT_3) = (n^2 - 4n)3^{n-2} + (3n - 2)2^{n-1} + 2$$

The authors also examine more complex actions, such as $d_1 = \text{ad}(e_{22})$ (which corresponds to $\beta = -1$ in the weighted sum). This derivation has eigenvalues 0, 1, and -1. The resulting identities involve terms like $x_1^{u_{23}} x_2^{u_{12}} = 0$, where $u_{ij}$ are elements in $U(L)$ that "pick out" specific matrix transitions. The codimension for this specific action is:
$$c_n^L(UT_3) = (n^2 - n)3^{n-2} + (3n - 2)2^{n-1} + 2$$

For the case where $L$ is two-dimensional and acts via both $\epsilon_1$ and $\epsilon_2$, the identities reflect the independence of the two diagonal actions. The sequence becomes:
$$c_n^L(UT_3) = (n^2 - n)3^{n-2} + 3n2^{n-1} + 1$$

## Summary and Impact

This research provides a comprehensive study of the differential identities of $UT_3$, a key algebra in the classification of varieties of exponent 3. By proving the reduction to semisimple actions, the authors have significantly narrowed the search for minimal $L$-varieties. The explicit determination of the codimension sequences for $UT_3$ under various diagonal actions offers a precise quantitative tool for distinguishing between different $L$-varieties that share the same exponential growth rate.

The results demonstrate how the structure of the acting Lie algebra $L$ influences the identity structure of an associative algebra. Even though the overall exponent $\exp_L(UT_3)$ remains 3 regardless of the action, the specific identities and the lower-order terms of the codimension sequence vary significantly depending on whether $L$ is one-dimensional, two-dimensional, or how its generators correspond to the diagonal of the matrix algebra. This work lays the necessary groundwork for a complete classification of minimal $L$-varieties of exponent 3, mirroring the historical development of classical PI-theory.
