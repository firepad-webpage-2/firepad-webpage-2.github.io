## The Imperative for Spatially Explicit Urban Forest Assessment

Urban forests serve as essential environmental infrastructure. Beyond their aesthetic value, trees in cities actively modify local climates through radiation regulation and evapotranspiration, intercept rainfall to mitigate stormwater runoff, and filter atmospheric pollutants. As urban centers face increasing challenges from rising temperatures and development pressures, the ability to map and monitor these natural assets becomes a priority for municipal planning.

Traditional methods for assessing urban tree cover often rely on two extremes: intensive field inventories or high-cost remote sensing. While field surveys provide granular details on species and health, they are difficult to scale across entire cities. Conversely, airborne LiDAR provides precise vertical structures but is often too expensive for frequent updates. This research addresses this gap by utilizing high-resolution optical imagery and "GeoAI"—the integration of geographic information systems with artificial intelligence—to create a reproducible framework for canopy assessment. Taking Davis, California, as a study site, the work demonstrates how routinely acquired aerial photography can be transformed into a high-confidence map of individual tree crowns and then integrated with socioeconomic and environmental data to guide urban interventions.

## A Multi-Stage GeoAI Workflow for Canopy Mapping

The core of the methodology is a modular workflow designed to identify individual tree crowns from 0.6-meter resolution National Agriculture Imagery Program (NAIP) imagery. The process relies on three distinct computational stages to ensure that the resulting map is both accurate and spectrally consistent with actual vegetation.

The first stage utilizes **DeepForest**, a deep learning model pre-trained to generate candidate bounding boxes for tree crowns. This model processes the imagery in overlapping patches to detect the visual signatures of trees. However, object detection alone can produce false positives, such as misidentifying dark rooftops or shadows as vegetation. To mitigate this, the second stage applies a filter using the **Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI)**. By calculating the mean NDVI within each proposed bounding box, the system removes candidates that do not exhibit the spectral signature of chlorophyll-rich vegetation.

The final stage employs the **Segment Anything Model (SAM)**. While DeepForest provides a box, SAM refines these proposals into precise, pixel-level masks. This "crown-anchored" approach ensures that the resulting canopy layer is limited to distinct tree objects, preventing the over-expansion of the mask into surrounding lawns or unrelated green spaces. The integration of these tools results in a high-confidence map that identifies 11,741 candidate crowns across the city, covering approximately 9.37% of the municipal area.

## Quantifying Agreement with Structural Reference Data

A critical component of this research is the validation of the optical GeoAI product against a high-quality reference: a LiDAR-assisted canopy product prepared for the USDA Forest Service and CAL FIRE. By aligning both products to an identical municipal boundary, the study quantifies exactly what the optical workflow captures.

The results characterize the optical product as a "selective" but "high-precision" layer. It achieved a pixel precision of 0.878, meaning that when the GeoAI workflow identifies a pixel as canopy, there is an 87.8% chance it matches the LiDAR-assisted reference. Furthermore, 97.4% of the detection centers (the centroids of the identified crowns) fell directly onto the reference canopy. While the optical layer only captured about 34.2% of the total reference canopy area—likely due to missing smaller understory trees or trees with low spectral contrast—its high precision makes it a reliable tool for identifying prominent, established crowns that provide significant shade and environmental benefits.

## Thermal Regulation and Built-Environment Interactions

One of the primary motivations for mapping urban trees is to understand their role in mitigating the urban heat island effect. The study integrated the canopy data with Land-Surface Temperature (LST) derived from Landsat satellite imagery.

![figure-06-thermal-and-built-context](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13856v1/figures/Figure06_Thermal_and_Built_Context.png)
*{fig:figure-06-thermal-and-built-context}: Spatial distribution of Land Surface Temperature (LST) and Dynamic World built-environment probabilities across the study area.*

The analysis revealed a consistent inverse relationship between tree cover and heat. The Spearman rank correlation between canopy percentage and LST was $\rho = -0.293$ with a significance of $p < 0.001$. This relationship remained robust even when controlling for the amount of built infrastructure in a given area, yielding a partial correlation of $\rho = -0.370$. Interestingly, the study found that Dynamic World "built probability" actually had a positive bivariate association with canopy ($\rho = +0.280$), likely reflecting the concentration of mature trees in established residential neighborhoods compared to more open, undeveloped, or industrial peripheral zones.

## Socioeconomic Patterns and Spatial Autocorrelation

Urban forestry is not just a biological concern; it is a matter of environmental justice. The study examined how canopy distribution intersects with socioeconomic indicators like population density, poverty, and income. 

Preliminary tests showed that canopy distribution varied significantly across poverty and income levels. However, the researchers emphasize that simple correlations can be misleading because spatial data is often "autocorrelated"—meaning that values in one location are highly dependent on values in neighboring locations. The study found high Global Moran’s $I$ values for canopy ($0.40$), LST ($0.68$), and poverty ($0.93$), indicating strong geographical clustering.

To account for this, the study utilized a **spatial-lag model**. This model suggests that the canopy cover in a specific area is not just a function of local variables like temperature or poverty, but is also heavily influenced by the canopy cover in the surrounding neighborhood. The spatial autoregressive coefficient was $\rho = 0.548$, confirming a substantial neighborhood effect. In this adjusted model, a $1^\circ\text{C}$ increase in temperature was associated with a 1.077 percentage point decrease in canopy cover, providing a more accurate estimate of the canopy-heat relationship than standard non-spatial models.

## Strategic Planning through Attention Surfaces

To translate these complex spatial relationships into actionable planning tools, the research introduced "attention surfaces." These maps aggregate multiple environmental and social factors to highlight areas that require priority intervention.

The first is the **Shade-Attention Surface**, which focuses exclusively on the intersection of heat and canopy need. It identifies areas that are both "hot" and "low-canopy." The attention value is calculated by combining normalized scores for temperature and tree cover:

$$
A_{\text{shade}} = \frac{1}{2} (Z_{\text{LST}} - Z_{\text{canopy}})
$$

![figure-09-shade-attention](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13856v1/figures/Figure09_Shade_Attention.png)
*{fig:figure-09-shade-attention}: The Shade-Attention map identifies priority areas where low canopy cover coincides with high land-surface temperatures.*

The second tool is a **Multi-Factor Attention Surface**. This surface incorporates nine different variables, including population density, poverty, distance to parks, and bikeway proximity. Rather than assigning arbitrary weights, the model uses the absolute strength of the Spearman association between each variable and the canopy cover to determine its importance. The weighting $w_i$ for each factor $i$ is defined as:

$$
w_i = \frac{|\rho_i|}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} |\rho_j|}$
$$

And the final attention score is a weighted sum:

$$
A_{\text{multi}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \cdot x_i
$$

![figure-10-weighted-attention](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13856v1/figures/Figure10_Weighted_Attention.png)
*{fig:figure-10-weighted-attention}: A multi-criteria attention surface where weights are derived from the statistical associations between canopy cover and socioeconomic factors.*

This approach ensures that the planning map is grounded in the actual spatial logic of the city. For example, in Davis, canopy percentage and residential land value received the highest weights ($16.2\%$ each), followed by temperature ($13.8\%$), reflecting their primary roles in characterizing the city's urban forest distribution.

## Significance for Municipal Management

The integration of GeoAI with spatial modeling provides a framework that is both auditable and scalable. By moving away from "black-box" models and towards transparent weighting systems, the study allows city planners to justify their decisions based on clear, evidence-based metrics. 

The high-precision optical mapping serves as a reliable baseline that can be updated as new aerial imagery becomes available, offering a way to track the growth of young trees or the impact of removals over time. Furthermore, by identifying that nearly half of the detected crowns are within 15 meters of a road, the research highlights specific opportunities for enhancing the "active travel" experience—making walking and cycling more viable by providing natural cooling along transit corridors. Ultimately, this end-to-end framework enables cities to move beyond simple mapping toward a nuanced, spatially aware strategy for urban forest management and climate resilience.
