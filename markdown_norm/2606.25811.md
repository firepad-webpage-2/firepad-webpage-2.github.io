## Modeling the Term Structure of Commodity Futures

The commodity futures market is a complex ecosystem where participants trade contracts for the delivery of raw materials at specific future dates. Unlike equities, where a single ticker represents ownership in a firm, a single commodity (like Crude Oil or Gold) is associated with multiple futures contracts, each with a different expiration or "maturity." This collection of prices across different maturities is known as the term structure.

Traditional quantitative strategies in this space often focus on directional bets—predicting whether the price of a specific contract will go up or down. However, such strategies expose traders to the inherent volatility of the underlying commodity's price. An alternative approach is the Calendar Spread (CS) strategy. A CS involves taking a long position in one maturity and a short position in another maturity of the same commodity. By doing so, the trader bets on the relative price movement between the two contracts (the "spread") rather than the direction of the commodity price itself. This approach typically mitigates broad market risk, focusing instead on the cost-of-carry and supply-demand dynamics specific to different time horizons.

Despite their prevalence in professional trading, CS strategies have received limited attention in machine learning research. Most existing models treat different contracts as independent entities or ignore the specific dependencies created by their maturities. This research introduces a hierarchical graph learning framework designed to capture these maturity-dependent interrelationships, providing a more nuanced prediction mechanism for statistical arbitrage.

## Theoretical Foundations of Calendar Spreads

Before developing a predictive model, it is essential to establish why CS strategies are advantageous. The researchers provide a theoretical framework comparing CS strategies with standard long-only (LO) strategies.

A CS strategy for a commodity $c$ is defined by a weight vector $w_{tc}$ that satisfies two primary conditions:
1.  The strategy is dollar-neutral (commodity-wise): $\mathbf{1}^T w_{tc} = 0$.
2.  The total exposure is normalized: $\|w_{tc}\|_1 = 1$.

The researchers propose three key propositions to justify the use of CS:
*   **Variance Reduction:** Under conditions where contract returns are highly correlated, a CS strategy can significantly reduce return variance compared to an LO strategy.
*   **Information Ratio (IR) Enhancement:** If a learning model can outperform a simple equal-weight benchmark by a sufficient margin, a derived CS strategy can achieve a higher risk-adjusted return (IR) than the corresponding LO strategy.
*   **Risk Exposure (Delta) Mitigation:** CS strategies naturally reduce exposure to the underlying commodity's price movements (delta risk). The paper shows that for a large majority of trading days (over 99%), CS strategies maintain a lower delta than LO strategies.

To bridge the gap between machine learning predictions and execution, the authors use a projection method. If a model predicts a vector of expected returns $\hat{Y}_t$, it is projected into a weight vector $w_t$ that is dollar-neutral and balanced across commodities. This is achieved by centering the predictions (subtracting the commodity-specific mean) and scaling them:
$$\hat{Y}_{tcd} - \hat{Y}_{\bullet tc}$$

## The Hierarchical Graph Learning (HGL) Architecture

The core technical contribution is the Hierarchical Graph Learning (HGL) model $f_k$. Commodity markets are structured naturally as a hierarchy: commodities at the top and their respective contracts at the bottom. The HGL architecture is designed to reflect this structure while accounting for the "Time-to-Maturity" (TTM) of each contract.

### 1. Graph Construction and Preprocessing
The model first converts historical data $H_t$ into a bi-level graph $G_t$.
*   **Nodes:** The upper level contains commodity nodes $\hat{C}_{t+2}$, and the lower level contains physical futures contracts $\hat{U}_{t+2}$.
*   **Edges:** The model defines five types of edges. $E_{CC+}$ and $E_{CC-}$ capture positive and negative correlations between commodities. $E_{CU}$ and $E_{UC}$ connect commodities to their constituent contracts. $E_{UU}$ connects a contract to its immediate neighbors in the term structure (the next shorter and next longer maturities).
*   **Features:** For each contract, features $x_{tcd\tau}$ are extracted from historical log-prices over a window $n_{\text{min\_sam}}$.

### 2. Graph Lifting and Virtual Grid Alignment
A significant challenge in graph learning for futures is that different commodities have different delivery cycles. For example, Corn might have contracts expiring in March, May, and July, while Gold might expire every month. This makes it difficult to compare "the 3-month contract" across different assets directly.

The researchers address this through **Graph Lifting** ($f_{L,k}$), which maps physical contracts onto a shared "virtual" TTM grid $\tau_j$. This creates an augmented graph $G_{t,0}$ where "virtual futures contracts" $\hat{C}_{t+2,0}$ exist for every commodity at standardized maturities. This alignment allows the model to learn cross-commodity relationships specifically for contracts with similar time horizons.

![hgl-bi-level-convolution](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.25811v1/hgnn.png)
*{fig:hgl-bi-level-convolution}: The bi-level convolution process involves four steps: elevating contract information to a virtual grid, propagating information across commodities on that grid, lowering the information back to physical contracts, and finally aggregating information within a commodity's term structure.*

### 3. Bi-Level Convolutional Layers
The model processes information through $l_{\text{con}}$ layers of bi-level convolutions. Each layer consists of four distinct steps:
1.  **Elevating ($g_{UC,kl}$):** Interpolates information from physical contracts $Z_{t,l-1}$ to the virtual TTM nodes $Z_{UC,tl}$.
2.  **Inter-commodity Propagation ($g_{CC,kl}$):** Shares information between different commodities that share the same virtual maturity. This captures how, for instance, a 6-month Brent Crude contract might influence a 6-month Heating Oil contract.
3.  **Lowering ($g_{CU,kl}$):** Maps the processed virtual information back to the physical contracts $Z_{CU,tl}$.
4.  **Intra-commodity Propagation ($g_{UU,kl}$):** Aggregates information along the term structure of a single commodity. It specifically looks at the relative differences between neighboring maturities to capture the "shape" of the curve (contango or backwardation).

The final embeddings $Z_{t,l_{\text{con}}}$ are passed through a prediction head to generate the target $\hat{Y}_{t+2}$, which predicts the demeaned, normalized returns of each contract.

## Empirical Performance and Validation

The HGL model was tested on a massive dataset of 63 commodities and over 350,000 futures contracts spanning 1977 to 2025. The researchers evaluated the model's ability to predict price movements and its performance in a simulated trading environment from 2016 onwards.

### Predictive Accuracy
HGL outperformed standard baselines such as Ridge regression, LightGBM, and Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLP). Critically, it also outperformed a standard Graph Neural Network (GNN) that did not account for TTM dependence. This confirms that the hierarchical structure and the TTM-alignment are vital for capturing the underlying market dynamics.

### Trading Results
When used to drive a Calendar Spread strategy, the HGL model demonstrated superior risk-adjusted returns.
*   **Information Ratio (IR):** HGL achieved an IR of 0.08459, which is more than double the performance of the long-only benchmarks and significantly higher than the non-hierarchical GNN (0.01492).
*   **Risk Mitigation:** The strategy showed a Maximum Drawdown (MDD) of 0.02002, which is significantly lower than the MDD of long-only strategies (which often exceeded 0.30).
*   **Market Correlation:** The HGL CS strategy showed a near-zero correlation (-0.01850) with the S&P 500, indicating its potential as a strong diversifier in a broader portfolio.

### Ablation Studies
To understand which part of the hierarchy was most important, the authors performed ablation tests:
*   Removing **Inter-commodity** relationships (connections between different assets) led to a moderate drop in performance.
*   Removing **Intra-commodity** relationships (connections between different maturities of the same asset) led to a much sharper decline.
This suggests that while cross-asset signals are useful, the most critical information for statistical arbitrage lies in the relative movements within a commodity's own term structure.

## Conclusion

This research demonstrates that accounting for the specific temporal structure of financial contracts is essential for effective modeling. By combining the theoretical risk-reduction benefits of Calendar Spreads with a hierarchical graph architecture that respects maturity dependencies, the authors have developed a robust framework for statistical arbitrage in commodity markets.

The HGL model's ability to "lift" irregular physical contracts into a standardized virtual space allows it to extract signals that are invisible to traditional models. The resulting strategy not only offers higher risk-adjusted returns but also provides significant diversification benefits due to its low correlation with broad equity indices. While practical implementation must still consider factors like transaction costs and margin requirements, the work provides a significant step forward in applying deep learning to the complex world of futures term structures.
