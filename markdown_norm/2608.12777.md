## Introduction to Heterogeneous Beliefs and Asset Pricing

In financial economics, the behavior of asset prices is often studied under the assumption of a "representative agent"—a single hypothetical investor whose beliefs and preferences summarize the entire market. However, real-world markets are composed of diverse participants with differing opinions on future economic growth. This heterogeneity of beliefs plays a significant role in determining interest rates, risk premiums, and the valuation of long-term financial contracts like bonds.

A central theme in this field is the concept of "market selection" or "investor survival." If some investors have more accurate forecasts than others, do they eventually dominate the market? Conventional theory suggests that investors with superior forecasting ability will accumulate wealth at the expense of those with incorrect beliefs. Over time, the consumption share of the less accurate forecasters may dwindle to zero—a phenomenon known as physical extinction.

However, a critical question remains: does an investor's influence on market prices disappear simply because their wealth share vanishes? Previous research has suggested that even "extinct" investors can remain "valuation relevant." This occurs when their consumption is concentrated in specific future states of the world that are rare but carry high marginal utility (i.e., states where aggregate consumption is very low). In such cases, their beliefs still shape the prices of claims that pay off in those states.

"Physical Extinction and Long-Run Pricing under Time-Varying Beliefs" by Sourav Majumdar extends this inquiry by introducing time-varying disagreement. While earlier models often assumed that the gap between investors' forecasts was constant, this paper explores what happens when the disagreement evolves dynamically. The research investigates how the cumulative nature of these shifting beliefs affects the survival of investors and the stability of long-term bond pricing.

## The Economic Model and Time-Varying Disagreement

The paper utilizes a standard Arrow–Debreu economy featuring two investors: a "rational investor" ($R$) and a "dogmatist" ($N$). The economy produces a single perishable good, referred to as the aggregate endowment ($D_t$), which follows a geometric Brownian motion:

$$
dD_t = \mu D_t dt + \sigma D_t dW_t
$$

In this equation, $\mu$ represents the expected growth rate, $\sigma$ is the volatility, and $W_t$ is a standard Brownian motion representing economic shocks. The rational investor correctly perceives the parameters $\mu$ and $\sigma$. In contrast, the dogmatist believes the growth rate is different. Specifically, the dogmatist's forecast of the growth rate at time $t$ is defined as:

$$
\mu + \sigma^2 \eta_t
$$

Here, $\eta_t$ is a deterministic function of time that represents the "belief distortion" or the level of disagreement. The unique contribution of this model is that $\eta_t$ is not a constant; it can fluctuate, grow, or decay over time. This allows the author to model various scenarios, such as fading pessimism, cyclical disagreements, or alternating periods of optimism and pessimism.

Both investors share the same preferences, characterized by Constant Relative Risk Aversion (CRRA) utility with a subjective discount rate $\rho$ and a risk aversion coefficient $\gamma$. The equilibrium in this economy is determined by the relative wealth and consumption shares of the two investors, which in turn depend on the history of economic shocks and the evolution of their beliefs.

## Distinguishing Total Disagreement and Net Disagreement

A fundamental innovation of the paper is the distinction between two types of accumulated disagreement: **total disagreement** and **net disagreement**. These two metrics govern different aspects of the economy's long-run behavior.

The total disagreement, denoted as $A_t$, is defined as the integral of the squared forecast errors:

$$
A_t = \int_0^t \eta_s^2 ds
$$

Because the errors are squared, $A_t$ is always non-decreasing. It tracks the cumulative magnitude of the "mistakes" made by the dogmatist relative to the rational investor.

The net disagreement, denoted as $H_t$, is the integral of the signed forecast errors:

$$
H_t = \int_0^t \eta_s ds
$$

Unlike total disagreement, net disagreement can increase or decrease. If the dogmatist is sometimes overly optimistic ($\eta_t > 0$) and sometimes overly pessimistic ($\eta_t < 0$), these errors can cancel each other out over time in the calculation of $H_t$.

The paper proves that the physical survival of an investor depends entirely on the total disagreement. Specifically, the dogmatist's share of consumption converges to zero ($c_{N,t}/D_t \to 0$) if and only if $A_t$ tends to infinity as time $t$ goes to infinity. This aligns with the intuition that persistent forecasting errors eventually lead to the loss of market share.

However, the pricing of assets—specifically long-term bonds—depends on a different set of dynamics. Even if an investor is physically extinct (because $A_t \to \infty$), they may still influence the "valuation weights" used to price future cash flows. The author shows that these valuation weights are driven by the behavior of net disagreement $H_t$ rather than just total disagreement.

## Long-Run Pricing and the Stability of the Yield Curve

The paper focuses heavily on the behavior of zero-coupon bonds as their maturity $T$ becomes very large. This involves analyzing the "long-forward measure" and the "long bond." The long-forward measure represents the weights assigned to different economic states when valuing a payoff in the infinite future, while the long bond represents the return process of a bond with an infinite maturity.

The central finding regarding long-run pricing is that these objects (the long-forward measure and the long bond) exist and are stable if and only if the net disagreement $H_t$ converges to a limit (which can be finite or $\pm \infty$).

1.  **Dominance of one investor:** If $H_T \to +\infty$, the rational investor's beliefs completely dominate the long-run valuation. If $H_T \to -\infty$, the dogmatist's beliefs dominate the long-run valuation, even if the dogmatist is physically extinct.
2.  **Shared influence:** If $H_T$ converges to a finite value, both investors' beliefs contribute to the long-run valuation, and the market acts as a weighted average of the two.
3.  **Instability:** If $H_T$ oscillates and does not converge (for example, in a scenario of constant-amplitude cyclical disagreement), then a stable long-forward measure and long bond do not exist. In this case, the weights used to price the long end of the yield curve never settle down, regardless of how far into the future one looks.

This result highlights a significant departure from models with constant disagreement. In a constant-disagreement model, $H_T$ always goes to infinity (unless the disagreement is zero), meaning one investor eventually dictates all long-term prices. In a time-varying model, the "path" of disagreement matters: errors that cancel each other out can sustain the influence of both investors indefinitely.

## The Disconnect Between Yields and Valuation Measures

Another major contribution of the paper is demonstrating a disconnect between the convergence of the "long yield" and the existence of long-run pricing objects. The long yield $y_\infty$ is the limiting average discount rate for a bond:

$$
y_\infty = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{-\ln P_0^T}{T}
$$

The author shows that the long yield can converge to a stable value even when the underlying valuation weights are oscillating and the long-forward measure does not exist. For instance, consider a case where the average disagreement vanishes over time ($H_T / T \to 0$), but the net disagreement $H_T$ continues to oscillate. In this scenario, the long yield will converge to the rational investor's benchmark yield:

$$
r_R = \rho + \gamma \mu - \frac{1}{2} \gamma (\gamma + 1) \sigma^2
$$

However, because $H_T$ is oscillating, the relative importance of the dogmatist's beliefs in pricing the bond continues to shift. This means that the "long yield" alone does not provide a complete picture of the market's long-run valuation dynamics. A stable yield does not necessarily imply a stable valuation law.

## Finite-Maturity Approximations and the Log-Utility Case

While much of the paper focuses on asymptotic results ($T \to \infty$), the author also provides practical formulas for bonds with finite maturities. These approximations are particularly powerful in the case of logarithmic utility ($\gamma = 1$), where the formulas become exact.

In the $\gamma = 1$ case, the valuation weight of the dogmatist for a bond maturing at $T$, denoted as $\theta_{t,T}$, can be expressed as:

$$
\theta_{t,T} = \frac{w_t e^{- A_{t,T}/2}}{1 + w_t e^{- A_{t,T}/2}}
$$

In this expression, $A_{t,T} = \int_t^T \eta_s^2 ds$ represents the total disagreement over the remaining life of the bond, and $w_t$ represents the current relative consumption share. This formula shows that as the total disagreement over the bond's horizon increases, the dogmatist's weight in the bond's price declines.

The author uses these exact solutions to illustrate several scenarios:
*   **Fading Pessimism:** If the dogmatist's pessimism vanishes over time such that $H_T \to -\infty$ but $H_T / T \to 0$, the dogmatist's weight $\theta_{t,T}$ actually increases with maturity $T$. The dogmatist becomes "valuation dominant" for very long-dated bonds even though they are physically extinct.
*   **Cyclical Disagreement:** If the disagreement oscillates, the valuation weight $\theta_{t,T}$ will also oscillate as a function of maturity. This creates a yield curve where the influence of different beliefs waxes and wanes depending on the specific maturity of the bond.

## Significance and Concluding Remarks

The research by Majumdar provides a more nuanced understanding of how diverse beliefs shape the financial landscape. By moving beyond the assumption of constant forecast errors, the paper reveals that the "path" of disagreement is just as important as its magnitude.

The key takeaway is that an investor's physical disappearance from the market does not equate to their beliefs disappearing from the price of long-term assets. The distinction between total disagreement (which drives wealth) and net disagreement (which drives pricing weights) allows for a world where "extinct" investors still matter. Furthermore, the discovery that the long yield can stabilize while valuation weights remain volatile suggests that the yield curve may hide underlying instabilities in how the market processes information about the distant future.

This work has implications for the valuation of ultra-long-dated assets, such as pension liabilities or infrastructure bonds, and for understanding how periods of alternating market sentiment (optimism followed by pessimism) leave a lasting imprint on the term structure of interest rates. By providing analytical tools to handle time-varying beliefs, the paper offers a rigorous framework for studying the complex interplay between investor survival and asset pricing.
