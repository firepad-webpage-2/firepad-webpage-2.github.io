In the landscape of private markets—encompassing private equity, venture capital, and private credit—the absence of standardized reporting and public exchange listings creates a fundamental information asymmetry. Unlike public markets where stock prices and quarterly filings are readily available, private company data is often sparse, irregular, and non-transparent. A core challenge for investment professionals is the identification of "peers": companies that are economically comparable. Identifying the right peers is essential for valuation, benchmarking, and risk management.

Traditionally, peer groups are constructed using rule-based filters such as industry classifications (GICS or SIC codes), geographic location, and funding stage. While intuitive, these methods often fail to capture the nuanced business models of modern firms, especially those operating across multiple sectors. This research introduces a framework for **Valuation-Anchored Similarity**, which leverages machine learning to learn what makes companies similar based on their actual market valuations rather than predefined categories.

## The Challenge of Private Market Data

Analyzing private companies involves navigating a data environment characterized by significant gaps. In the dataset used for this research—comprising approximately 270,000 companies—financial metrics such as revenue, EBITDA, and net income are missing for nearly 75–80% of the observations. However, categorical attributes like industry group, product offerings, and geography are more consistently available.

Standard analytical tools often struggle with this "missingness." Traditional regression models usually require complete data or simple imputation techniques that can introduce bias. Furthermore, the relationship between a company's attributes and its valuation is often non-linear. For example, the impact of geographic location on the valuation of a software startup in San Francisco may be vastly different from its impact on a manufacturing firm in the Midwest. 

## A Supervised Approach to Similarity

The central innovation of this work is treating company similarity as a **supervised learning problem**. Most similarity metrics, such as Euclidean distance or cosine similarity, are "unsupervised"—they treat all features as equally important or rely on manual weights. In contrast, supervised similarity learning anchors the definition of proximity to a specific objective: in this case, post-money valuation.

The methodology utilizes **CatBoost**, a high-performance gradient-boosted decision tree (GBDT) algorithm. CatBoost is particularly well-suited for this task because it handles categorical variables natively and manages missing numerical data without requiring explicit imputation. The model is trained to predict the post-money valuation of a company using its latest funding round data and financial characteristics.

However, the primary goal is not just to predict a single valuation number, but to extract the underlying "similarity structure" the model has learned. This is achieved through a technique called **weighted leaf-node co-occurrence**.

### Deriving the Similarity Metric

In a decision tree, companies that share similar characteristics are routed to the same "leaf node." If two companies frequently end up in the same leaves across thousands of different trees in an ensemble, they are likely to be similar in the eyes of the model. 

To formalize this, let $X_1$ and $X_2$ be two companies. The similarity $S(X_1, X_2)$ is calculated by summing the importance weights of the trees where both companies fall into the same terminal node. If we denote $w_t$ as the importance of tree $t$ and $L_t(X)$ as the leaf index for company $X$, the similarity is:





$$S(X_1, X_2) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} w_t \cdot \mathbb{1}[L_t(X_1) = L_t(X_2)]$$

The importance weight $w_t$ is determined by how much that specific tree contributed to reducing the model's error during training. From this similarity score, a dissimilarity distance $D$ can be derived:





$$D(X_1, X_2) = 1 - S(X_1, X_2)$$

This results in a bounded distance $D \in [0, 1]$, where 0 indicates identical neighbors and 1 indicates maximum dissimilarity. This distance metric effectively ignores features that do not impact valuation and prioritizes those that do.

## Interpreting Valuation Drivers

One of the significant advantages of this approach is its interpretability. By using **SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)**, the researchers can decompose which features are driving the similarity and the valuation estimates.

![shap-values-illustrating-the-primary-drivers-of-private-market-valuations](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12594v1/shap.png)
*{fig:shap-values-illustrating-the-primary-drivers-of-private-market-valuations}: Global feature importance based on SHAP values. Deal type and geographic location are the strongest predictors of valuation in the private market dataset.*

The analysis revealed that the **Deal Type** (e.g., Series A, Series B, Angel round) was the most dominant factor in determining valuation. This is intuitive, as funding stages are strong proxies for a company's maturity and risk profile. Other critical drivers included geography (Country, Region, and City) and financial performance (Revenue). Interestingly, the "average time since last financials" also emerged as a significant feature, suggesting that the staleness of data is itself an informative signal for valuation.

## Quantifying Uncertainty with Conformal Prediction

Given the inherent volatility of private markets, a point estimate for valuation is rarely sufficient. Investors need to understand the range of possible values. The paper employs **Conformal Prediction**, a framework for generating distribution-free prediction intervals. 

Unlike traditional confidence intervals that often assume a normal distribution of errors, conformal prediction makes very few assumptions. It uses the model's past errors on a calibration set to determine the range for new predictions. The researchers applied a "scaled" version of this technique to account for the fact that larger deals typically have larger absolute errors. This resulted in intervals that provide a mathematically grounded "margin of error" for each valuation, helping analysts assess the risk associated with a specific peer-based benchmark.

## Evaluation: Does the Metric Work?

The authors evaluated the learned similarity metric by comparing it against standard baselines:
1.  **Euclidean Distance:** Standard distance on scaled features.
2.  **Gower Distance:** A metric designed for mixed categorical and numerical data.
3.  **Cosine Similarity:** Applied to embeddings (MPNet) derived from textual company descriptions.

The evaluation focused on a $k$-nearest-neighbor ($k$-NN) task. For a target company, the system identifies its $k$ closest peers using each metric and then uses those peers to "estimate" the target company's valuation.

### Neighborhood Consistency
In qualitative tests within the Financial Services sector, the learned metric produced more coherent neighborhoods. When looking at the 20 nearest neighbors, the learned metric showed significantly lower mismatch rates for critical categorical features like "Deal Type" and "Country." It also maintained closer alignment in numerical features like revenue and net income compared to text-based embeddings.

### Quantitative Accuracy
The quantitative results demonstrated that the learned metric consistently outperformed the baselines in valuation accuracy. In Financial Services, the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) were lower for the learned metric across all neighborhood sizes (from $k=5$ to $k=20$).

## Significance for Private Market Workflows

The ability to derive an explicit, valuation-anchored similarity matrix has several practical implications for private market participants:

*   **Sourcing and Screening:** Investors can find "hidden" peers—companies that may be in different GICS categories but share identical valuation drivers and growth profiles with top-performing portfolio companies.
*   **Due Diligence:** Instead of relying on a handful of manually selected (and potentially biased) "comps," analysts can use the system to generate a data-driven peer group. This provides a robust baseline for evaluating whether a company's requested valuation is aligned with the broader market.
*   **Risk Management:** By grouping companies based on learned similarity, portfolio managers can identify concentration risks that are invisible to traditional industry-based reporting. For instance, two companies in different sectors might both be highly sensitive to the same regional economic factors or funding-stage dynamics.

## Conclusion

The transition from rule-based to learned similarity represents a shift toward more objective and scalable private market analytics. By anchoring similarity in market valuations and utilizing the strengths of tree-based ensembles, this framework addresses the challenges of data sparsity and non-linearity inherent in private equity and venture capital. The inclusion of SHAP for interpretability and conformal prediction for uncertainty quantification ensures that the resulting insights are not just accurate, but also transparent and actionable for financial professionals.
