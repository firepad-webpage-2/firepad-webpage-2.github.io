## Conceptualizing Diversity through Target-Based Majorization

A central challenge in institutional design is the objective measurement and promotion of diversity. Whether in university admissions, corporate hiring, or committee formation, decision-makers often seek to balance individual merit with a desire for a representative group composition. Traditionally, this has been addressed through affirmative action policies or the use of specific diversity indices, such as Shannon entropy or the Gini-Simpson index. However, these methods can be sensitive to the choice of index, leading to inconsistent rankings of different groups. Furthermore, defining what constitutes "under-representation" often relies on subjective or binary categorizations of individuals into "designated types."

The framework of "Diversity as Majorization" proposes a formal approach to this problem by shifting the focus from specific indices to the underlying principles of distributional comparison. It introduces a target-based perspective where diversity is measured relative to an arbitrary benchmark distribution. This benchmark could reflect the demographic makeup of a local population, a desired balance of skills, or any other institutional objective. By grounding this comparison in majorization theory—a mathematical tool used to study inequality and dispersion—the framework establishes a rigorous method for determining which of two groups is "more diverse" without relying on a single, potentially arbitrary numerical index.

The core intuition behind this approach is the "Robin Hood transfer." In classical economics, a distribution is considered more equal if it can be reached from another by moving wealth from a richer individual to a poorer one. In the context of diversity, a group becomes more diverse if agents are shifted from types that are over-represented relative to a target to those that are under-represented. This generalization allows for a nuanced comparison that accounts for any number of categories and any desired target composition.

## The Mathematical Framework: $r$-Targeting Diversity

The paper formalizes these concepts by considering a population divided into $n$ types. Let $x$ represent a vector of counts for each type, and $\langle x \rangle$ denote the total number of individuals in the group. The institutional target is expressed as a probability measure $r \in \Delta^{n-1}$, where the ideal level for type $i$ is $\langle x \rangle r_i$.

To measure how far a group deviates from this ideal, the authors define the deviation for each type $i$ as:

$$d_{r,i}(x) := x_i - \langle x \rangle r_i$$

A group $y$ is considered "more $r$-diverse" than $x$ (denoted $y \succeq_r x$) if it can be obtained from $x$ through a sequence of $r$-targeting Robin Hood transfers. A single transfer involves moving a small mass $\delta$ from type $i$ to type $j$ such that $d_{r,i}(x) \geq d_{r,j}(x) + \delta$. This process effectively "balances" the deviations, bringing both types closer to their relative targets.

A key contribution of the paper is linking this intuitive notion of diversity to standard majorization theory. Standard majorization typically compares vectors relative to a uniform distribution (where all components are equal). To bridge this gap, the authors introduce a linear transformation $T_r: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ defined by:

$$T_r(x) = x + \langle x \rangle(u - r)$$

In this equation, $u = (1/n, \dots, 1/n) \in \Delta^{n-1} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ represents the uniform distribution. This transformation maps the target distribution $r$ to the uniform distribution $u$, allowing the authors to prove that $y \succeq_r x$ if and only if the vector $T_r(x)$ majorizes $T_r(y)$. This equivalence allows the extensive literature on majorization to be applied directly to the problem of $r$-targeting diversity.

## The $r$-Diversity Frontier and Index Robustness

In practical selection problems, an institution must choose $q$ agents from a larger available population $x$. The set of all possible type distributions for a group of size $q$ that can be formed from the population is denoted $B(x, q) := \{y \in \mathbb{Z}_+^n : y \leq x, \langle y \rangle = q\}$. Within this set, there may be many different combinations of types.

The $r$-diversity frontier, $F_r(x, q) := \{y \in B(x, q) : \exists z \in B(x, q) \text{ s.t. } z \succ_r y\}$, is the set of all type distributions that are maximally $r$-diverse. A distribution is on this frontier if it is not strictly $r$-dominated by any other feasible distribution. 

One of the most significant theoretical results of the paper is the robustness of this frontier. Different diversity indices (e.g., Gini-Simpson, Shannon entropy) are often used to rank groups, but they may disagree. The authors define a class of target-adjusted diversity indices of the form:

$$\Phi_{r,g}(y) := \sum_{i=1}^n g(T_{r,i}(y))$$

where $g$ is any concave function. This class includes target-adjusted versions of most common indices. The paper proves that the $r$-diversity frontier is precisely the set of distributions that simultaneously maximize every index in this broad class. This means that if a selection is on the $r$-diversity frontier, it is considered "optimally diverse" regardless of which specific concave index an institution chooses to measure diversity. This result provides a strong justification for focusing on the majorization-based frontier rather than selecting a specific index.

## Structural Properties: The Unit-Width Box

The paper further explores the structural properties of the $r$-diversity frontier. Because individuals are indivisible, the frontier consists of discrete points in the space of type distributions. The authors demonstrate that these points have a very specific structure. 

First, they show an exchange property: if one distribution on the frontier has more of type $i$ and less of type $j$ than another frontier distribution, then swapping one agent of type $i$ for an agent of type $j$ results in another distribution that is also on the frontier. Furthermore, any two distributions $y, z \in F_r(x, q)$ are equally $r$-diverse ($y \succeq_r z \text{ and } z \succeq_r y$).

This structure leads to the derivation of type-specific bounds. For each type $i$, let $L_{r,i}(x, q)$ be the minimum number of type-$i$ agents present in any distribution on the frontier, and $U_{r,i}(x, q)$ be the maximum. The paper proves a "unit-width box characterization," showing that for every type $i$:

$$U_{r,i}(x, q) - L_{r,i}(x, q) \in \{0, 1\}$$

This means that for any type, the "allowable" number of agents that maintains maximal diversity varies by at most one. The frontier is exactly the set of all feasible distributions $y$ such that $L_r(x, q) \leq y \leq U_r(x, q)$. This characterization is crucial because it transforms an abstract set of maximally diverse distributions into a concrete set of lower and upper bounds for each type. These bounds function as endogenous reserves and quotas.

## Implementation: The Reserve-and-Quota Policy

The identification of $L_r(x, q)$ and $U_r(x, q)$ allows for the design of a practical selection policy that balances diversity with merit. While the frontier identifies which *compositions* are diverse, it does not specify which *individuals* should be selected. Individuals within a type typically have different priority or merit rankings.

The authors propose a "Reserve-and-Quota" (RQ) policy:
1.  **Reserves:** For every type $i$, reserve $L_{r,i}(x, q)$ seats and fill them with the highest-priority individuals of that type.
2.  **Open Competition with Quotas:** Fill the remaining seats (the open slots) using the highest-priority individuals available from the remaining pool, regardless of type. However, a type $i$ individual can only be selected if the total count of type $i$ agents already selected is less than the upper bound $U_{r,i}(x, q)$.

This policy is deterministic and easy to implement once the bounds are calculated. The paper demonstrates that the outcome of this policy, $A_{RQ}$, is optimal in two ways. First, its type distribution $\xi(A_{RQ}) \in F_r(x, q)$ is on the $r$-diversity frontier, meaning it achieves the highest possible level of diversity. Second, the policy is Pareto optimal with respect to diversity and merit. For any other possible selection of $q$ agents, that selection is either less $r$-diverse than the RQ outcome or is priority-dominated by the RQ outcome. 

## Significance and Impact

The "Diversity as Majorization" framework provides a rigorous foundation for diversity-aware market design. By moving away from fixed, exogenous quotas and towards endogenously derived bounds based on a target distribution, the research offers a more flexible and theoretically sound approach to group selection. 

The significance of the work lies in several areas:
- **Target-Based Generalization:** It provides a way to evaluate diversity relative to any benchmark, which is essential for institutions with specific, non-uniform representation goals.
- **Robustness:** The proof that the $r$-diversity frontier is index-independent removes the ambiguity often associated with choosing between different diversity metrics.
- **Policy Derivation:** The methodology provides a clear, step-by-step process for calculating type-specific reserves and quotas directly from an institution's goals and the available population.
- **Balancing Merit and Diversity:** The RQ policy identifies a specific point on the Pareto frontier, ensuring that diversity is achieved without unnecessary sacrifices in the priority or merit of the selected individuals.

In summary, the paper bridges the gap between abstract mathematical theories of dispersion and the practical needs of institutional selection. It offers a principled way to define, compare, and achieve diversity, providing decision-makers with tools that are both mathematically robust and practically applicable.
