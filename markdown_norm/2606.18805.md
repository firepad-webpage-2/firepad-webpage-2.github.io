## Understanding Emotional Spillovers in Traffic

The human emotional state is a powerful driver of decision-making, yet its influence often extends far beyond the situation that triggered the emotion. In behavioral economics, this phenomenon is known as an emotional spillover. Research has documented how the outcomes of high-stakes collective events, such as professional sports, can influence a variety of unrelated societal outcomes, from stock market fluctuations to judicial sentencing. One of the most critical, yet understudied, areas where these spillovers occur is on the road.

Every year, millions of fans attend sporting events, experiencing intense emotional highs and lows based on their team's performance. When the final whistle blows, these fans transition almost immediately from a state of high emotional arousal into the complex environment of modern traffic. This research investigates whether the emotional cues generated by game outcomes—specifically those that deviate from a fan's expectations—manifest as changes in real-world driving behavior. By analyzing minute-level vehicle speeds and crash data following NFL and NBA games, the study provides a detailed look at how collective disappointment or excitement translates into risky behavior behind the wheel.

## The Mechanics of Reference-Dependent Emotions

The study's theoretical framework is rooted in the concept of reference-dependent preferences. According to this theory, individuals do not evaluate outcomes in isolation; instead, they assess them relative to a reference point, which is often shaped by their expectations. In the context of sports, the pre-game betting line (the "point spread") serves as a natural proxy for these expectations.

The research breaks down emotional responses into two primary components: suspense and valence. 
1.  **Suspense:** This refers to the emotional arousal generated when an outcome is uncertain. Games that are predicted to be close create higher levels of suspense than those where one team is heavily favored.
2.  **Valence:** This refers to the "goodness" or "badness" of the outcome (i.e., winning or losing).

When a game is predicted to be close and ends in a loss, the resulting emotion is one of intense disappointment and frustration. Conversely, an "upset win" (winning when a loss was expected) creates a sense of surprise and elation. The authors hypothesize that these specific combinations—what they term "suspense-valence profiles"—are the primary drivers of behavioral changes. A key insight of the study is that the intensity of the emotional spillover is not just about the win or loss itself, but how that result compares to what the fan anticipated before the game began.

## Measuring Behavioral Shifts: Data and Methodology

To capture these subtle changes in behavior, the researchers utilized high-resolution spatiotemporal data from five professional stadiums in Florida. This included home games for three NFL teams (Buccaneers, Dolphins, Jaguars) and two NBA teams (Heat, Magic) between 2015 and 2019.

The primary data source was anonymized GPS-based traffic data from HERE Technologies. This allowed the researchers to observe the average vehicle speed on specific road segments, known as Traffic Message Channels (TMCs), in 10-minute increments. By focusing on roads within a 5-kilometer radius of the stadiums, the study could track fans as they dispersed from the venue.

![stadium-traffic-map](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.18805v1/bucs_map.png)
*{fig:stadium-traffic-map}: Example of road segments (TMCs) within a 5-kilometer radius of Raymond James Stadium in Tampa, Florida.*

The empirical strategy employed a fixed-effects linear regression model to isolate the effect of game outcomes from other factors like weather, time of day, or general game-day congestion. The model is specified as:

$$
Speed_{it} = \alpha_i + f(p_{it}, y_{it}; \lambda) + \theta_{it} + \delta_{it} + \epsilon_{it}
$$

In this equation:
- $Speed_{it}$ is the average vehicle speed on road segment $i$ at time $t$.
- $\alpha_i$ represents TMC-level fixed effects, controlling for the permanent characteristics of each road (like speed limits or lane counts).
- $f(p_{it}, y_{it}; \lambda)$ is the core function of interest, capturing the interaction between pre-game expectations $p_{it}$ and actual outcomes $y_{it}$.
- $\theta_{it}$ is a binary indicator for whether a crash occurred on that segment at that time, which helps ensure that speed changes are behavioral and not just a result of accident-related delays.
- $\delta_{it}$ accounts for time-varying controls such as weather conditions and seasonal trends.
- $\epsilon_{it}$ is the error term.

By comparing game days to matched non-game days (the control group), the researchers could identify deviations in traffic patterns specifically attributable to the emotional context of the game.

## Empirical Findings: The "Close Loss" Effect

The most striking results came from the NFL analysis. The researchers found that driving behavior is significantly affected by high-suspense, negative-valence outcomes. Specifically, following a game that was predicted to be close (within a $\pm 4$ point spread) but resulted in a home-team loss, average vehicle speeds increased by up to 3 mph compared to similar games that ended in a win.

This speed increase is particularly noteworthy because it occurs despite the heavy congestion typically found around stadiums after a game. While general traffic usually slows down after a game due to the high volume of vehicles, the "emotional driving" effect pushes speeds higher than they would otherwise be. This effect was most pronounced within the first hour after the game ended and within a 3-kilometer radius of the stadium. As time passed and drivers moved further away, the emotional arousal appeared to decay, and speeds returned to normal levels.

Interestingly, "upset wins"—where the team won despite being predicted to lose—also showed some evidence of increased speed, likely due to elation-induced arousal. However, this effect was less consistent and smaller in magnitude than the "close loss" effect. "Upset losses" (losing when a win was expected) did not show a systematic increase in speed, possibly because the surprise of the loss led to a different emotional state than the sustained frustration of a closely contested defeat.

## Comparing the Gridiron and the Court

One of the study's unique contributions is the comparison between two different sports: professional football (NFL) and professional basketball (NBA). While the NFL games showed clear evidence of emotional driving, the NBA games did not. Across all suspense-valence profiles for the NBA, vehicle speeds remained largely unchanged relative to the control group.

The authors suggest several reasons for this divergence:
1.  **Event Scarcity:** An NFL season consists of only 17 regular-season games, making each game highly significant. In contrast, an NBA season has 82 games, which may dilute the emotional stakes of any single regular-season outcome.
2.  **Physicality and Arousal:** The high-contact, high-intensity nature of football may generate greater physiological arousal in spectators compared to basketball.
3.  **Timing:** NFL games are almost exclusively held on weekends (mostly Sundays), while NBA games occur throughout the week. The weekend environment may facilitate different social behaviors (like tailgating or post-game gatherings) that interact with emotional states.

This finding suggests that the "emotional driving" phenomenon is not a universal reaction to all sports but is contingent on the intensity and perceived importance of the event.

## Safety Consequences and Policy Interventions

The behavioral changes identified in the speed data have direct implications for public safety. Even a small increase in average speed across a large population of drivers can lead to a significant rise in the probability of fatal crashes. To test this link, the researchers analyzed Florida statewide crash data.

The results confirmed that NFL game days are indeed more dangerous on the road. In the first hour following a game, crashes were approximately 404% more frequent than on non-game days. Crucially, this increase was even higher following losses (505%) than following wins (408%). This alignment between the speed data and the crash data reinforces the conclusion that emotionally induced risky driving is a genuine public health concern.

The predictability of these effects—occurring at specific times, in specific locations, and following specific game outcomes—offers a roadmap for policy interventions. Rather than relying on general safety campaigns, local authorities could implement targeted measures:
- **Responsive Enforcement:** Law enforcement could increase their presence or deploy automated speed cameras specifically in the 0–3 km zone following games predicted to be close, especially if the home team loses.
- **Dynamic Messaging:** Stadiums and local transport departments could use electronic signage to provide "behavioral nudges," reminding fans to stay calm and drive safely, particularly after disappointing results.
- **De-escalation Spaces:** Encouraging fans to remain at the venue for post-game activities or providing enhanced public transport could give them time to "cool off" and allow emotional arousal to subside before they get behind the wheel.

By integrating behavioral insights into traffic management, cities can mitigate the hidden costs of professional sports and ensure that the passion of the fans doesn't lead to tragedy on the road.
