## From Average to Distributional Treatment Effects

In many fields of empirical research, from economics to public health, the gold standard for evaluating the success of an intervention is the Average Treatment Effect (ATE). The ATE provides a single numerical summary—the difference in mean outcomes between a treated group and a control group. While useful, this summary measure can mask significant heterogeneity. A policy might improve the average outcome while simultaneously harming the most vulnerable individuals or benefiting only those already at the top of the distribution.

To address these limitations, researchers have increasingly turned toward Distributional Treatment Effects (DTEs). Rather than asking "What is the average effect?", DTE methods ask "How does the entire distribution of outcomes change?" This approach allows for a more granular view of who wins and who loses as a result of a treatment. A critical, yet often under-explored, phenomenon in this context is the treatment-effect sign reversal. This occurs when an intervention has a positive effect on one part of the distribution and a negative effect on another. Such scenarios are more than just theoretical possibilities; they appear frequently in evaluations of welfare reforms, microcredit, and educational programs.

The paper introduces a structured toolkit to detect, locate, and analyze these sign reversals, which the authors term "treatment-effect discontinuities." By focusing on the points where the marginal distributional effect changes sign, researchers can identify specific thresholds in the outcome variable where the nature of the treatment's impact fundamentally shifts.

## The Treatment Effects Curve (TEC)

The foundation of this toolkit is the Treatment Effects Curve (TEC). Mathematically, if we have two groups—Group A and Group B—with their respective cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) denoted as $F_A(x)$ and $F_B(x)$, the TEC is defined as the difference between them:

$$\Delta(x) = F_A(x) - F_B(x)$$

Here, $x$ represents the outcome variable (e.g., income, test scores, or consumption). A positive value of $\Delta(x)$ at a specific point $x$ indicates that distribution A has a higher cumulative probability than distribution B at that level, which typically suggests a rightward shift of B relative to A. When the distributions cross, $\Delta(x)$ passes through zero.

The TEC is a powerful visual and analytical tool. If the curve remains entirely on one side of the zero line, one distribution "stochastically dominates" the other, meaning the treatment effect has a consistent sign across the entire population. However, when the TEC crosses the zero line, it identifies a point of discontinuity where the marginal distributional effect reverses. These crossing points are the primary focus of the toolkit, as they represent the boundaries between different regimes of treatment impact.

## Horizontal and Vertical Discontinuity Analysis

Once crossing points are identified, the toolkit provides two complementary frameworks for deeper analysis: Horizontal Discontinuity Analysis (HDA) and Vertical Discontinuity Analysis (VDA).

### Horizontal Discontinuity Analysis (HDA)

HDA focuses on the regions of the outcome distribution separated by the crossing points. If a crossing occurs at $x_0$, the populations below and above $x_0$ experience treatment effects of opposite signs. The goal of HDA is to explain *why* these groups respond differently. To do this, the paper suggests using machine learning techniques, specifically causal random forests, to model conditional average treatment effects (CATEs) within each region. By examining how covariates—such as education level, household size, or pre-treatment wealth—influence the effect within these regions, researchers can identify the characteristics that drive the sign reversal.

### Vertical Discontinuity Analysis (VDA)

VDA shifts the focus to the crossing point itself. It examines whether there is an abrupt change or "jump" in the treatment effect as one moves across the threshold $x_0$. This approach is inspired by Regression Discontinuity Design (RDD). If the treatment effect changes smoothly across the distribution, the crossing point might simply be a point of transition. However, if there is a sharp jump at $x_0$, it suggests a structural break in how the treatment affects individuals near that specific outcome level.

## Statistical Inference for Crossing Points

A major contribution of this work is the adaptation of asymptotic theory to provide rigorous statistical tests for the location and nature of these crossing points. Identifying a crossing in a sample estimate does not necessarily mean a crossing exists in the underlying population; it could be the result of sampling noise.

The toolkit addresses this through several inferential steps:

1.  **Locating the Crossing:** The estimator $\hat{x}_0$ is identified using un-smoothed empirical CDFs. The authors show that $\hat{x}_0$ is a $\sqrt{n}$-consistent estimator that follows a Gaussian distribution in large samples.
2.  **Testing the Local Slope:** For a crossing to be well-defined and "non-tangential," the slope of the TEC at the crossing point must be non-zero. The slope is defined as:

$$\Delta'(x_0) = f_A(x_0) - f_B(x_0) \neq 0$$

where $f_A(x_0)$ and $f_B(x_0)$ are the probability density functions at the crossing point. If the slope is near zero, the distributions are nearly parallel, making the exact location of the crossing highly uncertain.
3.  **Bias Correction:** Estimating densities (and thus the slope) often introduces bias, particularly in small samples. The toolkit employs a bias-corrected Wald statistic, $W_{bc}$, to test the null hypothesis that the slope is zero ($H_0: \Delta'(x_0) = 0$). This correction is essential for ensuring that the confidence intervals for the crossing location are accurately calibrated.

The precision of the crossing-point estimator $\hat{x}_0$ is directly linked to the steepness of the slope. A "steep" crossing is easier to identify and locate than a "flat" one. Through Monte Carlo simulations, the authors demonstrate that while detection power is high for steep crossings even in moderate samples (e.g., $n \geq 4,000$), flat crossings require much larger datasets (e.g., $n = 16,000$) for reliable identification.

## Empirical Diagnostics: The PROGRESA Case Study

To demonstrate the practical utility of the toolkit, the paper applies it to data from Mexico’s PROGRESA program, a well-known conditional cash transfer experiment. The analysis focuses on food consumption distributions for households eligible for the program versus those that were ineligible (studying potential "spillover" effects).

![progresa-tec-analysis](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.28017v1/CDFTECs.png)
*{fig:progresa-tec-analysis}: TEC analysis of the PROGRESA program. Panels (a) and (b) show that for eligible households, the program effects were consistently positive (stochastic dominance). Panels (c) and (d) examine ineligible households, where the toolkit diagnoses subtle and statistically insignificant sign reversals in the short term.*

The PROGRESA application highlights the diagnostic value of the toolkit. For the eligible group, the TEC remains positive, confirming that the program improved consumption across the entire distribution. However, for the ineligible group in the short run (November 1998), the TEC hovered near zero and exhibited multiple crossings. 

By applying the non-tangentiality test, the researchers found that these crossings were "flat"—the local slopes were not significantly different from zero, and the confidence intervals for the crossing locations were extremely wide (e.g., a range of $[78; 431]$ pesos for a crossing estimated at 172 pesos). This diagnostic result cautioned against performing HDA or VDA, as the sign reversals were likely artifacts of noise rather than structural shifts. This illustrates a key benefit of the toolkit: it prevents researchers from over-interpreting minor distributional fluctuations as meaningful policy thresholds.

## Significance for Policy and Research

The toolkit for treatment-effect discontinuities fills a specific gap in the econometric landscape. While many methods focus on the *magnitude* of heterogeneity—identifying which quantiles gain the most—this toolkit focuses on the *direction* of the effect.

The significance of this approach is three-fold:

*   **Identifying Vulnerable Subgroups:** By locating exactly where a treatment effect switches from positive to negative, policymakers can identify segments of the population that may be unintentionally harmed by a broad intervention.
*   **Structural Insights:** Distinguishing between sharp jumps (VDA) and smooth transitions at crossing points provides clues about the underlying mechanisms of a treatment. A sharp jump might suggest a "poverty trap" or a specific eligibility threshold, while a smooth transition suggests a gradual shift in behavioral response.
*   **Robustness and Rigor:** By providing bias-corrected inferential tools, the toolkit ensures that findings of "crossing distributions" are statistically significant and not merely the result of sampling error.

This methodology serves as a bridge between high-level distributional summaries and detailed individual-level modeling. It provides a structured workflow—visualize with the TEC, locate and test with crossing-point inference, and then explain with HDA and VDA—that allows for a comprehensive understanding of complex treatment effects in diverse populations.
