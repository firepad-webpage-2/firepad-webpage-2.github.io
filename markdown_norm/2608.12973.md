## Foundations of Distributional Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning (RL) has traditionally been focused on maximizing the expected cumulative reward, often referred to as the value function. While modeling the expectation provides a useful scalar summary of performance, it ignores the inherent uncertainty and variability of the return. Distributional Reinforcement Learning (DRL) addresses this limitation by modeling the entire distribution of future returns rather than just their mean. This richer representation allows agents to account for risk and provides a more granular understanding of potential outcomes.

Because return distributions are infinite-dimensional objects, they must be approximated for computational tractability. One of the most effective paradigms for this approximation is quantile representation. In this approach, the distribution is characterized by a set of $m$ quantiles. Quantile Temporal Difference (QTD) learning is a fundamental algorithm in this domain. It updates these quantile estimates over time as the agent interacts with the environment, moving the current estimates toward the target distribution defined by the Bellman equation.

In a typical tabular setting with a fixed policy, the state-quantile parameters are denoted as $\theta^{(t)}(s,i)$, where $s$ is the state and $i = 1, \dots, m$ is the index of the quantile at level $\tau_i = \frac{2i-1}{2m}$. As the agent observes a transition consisting of a state $s$, reward $r$, and next state $s'$, the QTD algorithm adjusts the parameters based on the difference between the current estimate and the projected return.

## The Challenge of Statistical Inference in QTD

While QTD and its extensions, such as Quantile Regression DQN (QR-DQN), have demonstrated strong empirical performance, their theoretical properties have primarily been studied through the lens of convergence. Researchers have established that, given enough data, the iterates $\theta^{(t)}$ will eventually converge to the true quantile parameters $\theta_m$. However, knowing that an algorithm converges is different from understanding the precision of its estimates at any given time $T$.

In many high-stakes applications, such as medical decision-making or financial trading, it is not enough to have a point estimate of a quantile. One needs to know how much that estimate might fluctuate due to sampling noise. This requires valid uncertainty quantification, typically in the form of confidence intervals.

Providing a statistical inference theory for QTD is difficult because of the algorithm's mathematical structure. Unlike standard temporal difference learning, which is linear, the QTD update involves a non-smooth indicator function:

$$\theta^{(t+1)}(s, i) = \theta^{(t)}(s, i) + \alpha_t \left( \tau_i - \mathbb{I}\{r_t + \gamma \theta^{(t)}(s', j) < \theta^{(t)}(s, i)\} \right)$$

In this update, $\alpha_t$ is the step size, $\gamma$ is the discount factor, and the indicator function $\mathbb{I}\{\dots\}$ checks whether the realized return is less than the current quantile estimate. This non-smoothness means that the "error" surface is not differentiable, preventing the application of standard statistical tools used for smooth optimization. The transitions and updates also create complex dependencies across different quantile levels and states, especially when data is collected from a single trajectory (Markovian data).

## Theoretical Framework: Functional Central Limit Theorems

The core contribution of this research is the development of a statistical theory that describes the long-term behavior of QTD fluctuations. The authors achieve this by establishing Functional Central Limit Theorems (FCLTs) for QTD under three different data-generation scenarios: synchronous, asynchronous, and Markovian.

An FCLT is more powerful than a standard Central Limit Theorem. While a standard CLT tells us that the final estimate follows a normal distribution, an FCLT describes the behavior of the entire path (or partial sums) of the algorithm's iterates. Specifically, the authors prove that the normalized process:

$$T^{-1/2} \sum_{t=0}^{\lfloor Tu \rfloor} (\theta^{(t)} - \theta_m)$$

converges weakly to a continuous Gaussian process, specifically a scaled Brownian motion $G^{-1}\Gamma^{1/2}B(\cdot)$. Here, $B(\cdot)$ represents a multi-dimensional standard Brownian motion, $G$ is a Jacobian matrix reflecting the local geometry of the Bellman operator near the fixed point, and $\Gamma$ is a covariance matrix related to the noise in the rewards and transitions.

The analysis shows how different sampling strategies affect the precision of the estimates:
- **Synchronous Updates:** All states are updated at every step, leading to a symmetric influence of noise.
- **Asynchronous Updates:** States are sampled from a fixed distribution, and the asymptotic variance is scaled by the inverse of these sampling probabilities.
- **Markovian Updates:** Data comes from a single path in the environment. The precision here is influenced by the stationary distribution of the Markov chain, reflecting how often the agent naturally visits each state.

By rigorously proving these theorems, the researchers provide a mathematical guarantee that the fluctuations of QTD are asymptotically Gaussian, which is the necessary first step for constructing valid statistical tests and confidence intervals.

## Practical Online Inference via Random Scaling

While the FCLTs provide the theoretical distribution of the QTD iterates, using them in practice is difficult. The asymptotic variance involves the matrices $G$ and $\Gamma$, which depend on the underlying environment's transition dynamics and the density of the reward distributions—quantities that are usually unknown and difficult to estimate accurately.

To solve this, the paper introduces an online inference procedure based on a technique called "random scaling." This method allows for the construction of confidence intervals for any linear functional of the quantile parameters, such as $c^T \theta_m$ where $c \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times m}$. This could represent the estimate of a specific quantile at a specific state, or an average of quantiles.

The procedure relies on the Polyak-Ruppert averaged iterate, $\bar{\theta}_T = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \theta^{(t)}$, which is known to be more stable and statistically efficient than the raw last iterate. The authors construct a statistic using a random scaling matrix $\hat{V}_T$:

$$\hat{V}_T = \frac{1}{T^3} \sum_{t=1}^T \left( \sum_{k=1}^t \theta^{(k)} - t\bar{\theta}_T \right) \left( \sum_{k=1}^t \theta^{(k)} - t\bar{\theta}_T \right)^T$$

The breakthrough of this approach is that the resulting test statistic converges to a "pivotal" distribution:

$$V = \frac{B(1)}{\sqrt{\int_0^1 (B(u) - uB(1))^2 du}}$$

This distribution $V$ is universal—it does not depend on the unknown environment parameters ($G$ or $\Gamma$). It is a known distribution for which quantiles can be pre-calculated. Therefore, a practitioner can construct a $(1-\alpha)$-confidence interval for the quantile parameter as:

$$\left[ c^T\bar{\theta}_T - q_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{c^T\hat{V}_T c}, c^T\bar{\theta}_T + q_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{c^T\hat{V}_T c} \right]$$

where $q_{\alpha/2}$ is the corresponding quantile of the distribution $V$.

A critical feature of this method is its online nature. The average $\bar{\theta}_T$ and the scaling matrix $\hat{V}_T$ can both be updated recursively as new data arrives. There is no need to store the entire history of the agent's parameters, which would be memory-intensive. This makes the method suitable for real-time monitoring of an agent's learning progress.

## Significance and Broader Impact

This work bridges a significant gap between the empirical success of distributional reinforcement learning and its theoretical statistical grounding. By moving beyond simple convergence proofs, the research provides a toolkit for uncertainty quantification that is both theoretically sound and computationally efficient.

The move from point estimates to confidence intervals is essential for making RL safer and more interpretable. For example, if an agent learns that the 5th percentile of returns for a specific action is between -10 and -2, a human operator can make a much more informed decision than if they were simply given a single estimated value of -6. The ability to do this "on-the-fly" without complex post-processing or knowledge of the environment's internal densities makes it a versatile tool for the RL community.

Furthermore, the methodology established here for handling the non-smoothness of QTD provides a template for analyzing other distributional RL algorithms, such as those using different distance metrics or more complex function approximators. By identifying the FCLT as the appropriate tool for characterizing sampling fluctuations in model-free DRL, the authors have provided a roadmap for future statistical investigations in the field.
