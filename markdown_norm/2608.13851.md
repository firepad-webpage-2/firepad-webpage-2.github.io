## The Computational Challenge of Limited Dependent Variable Models

Limited dependent variable models (LDVMs) are a cornerstone of empirical economic analysis. These models describe outcomes that are not continuously distributed, such as binary choices (buying a product or not), multinomial choices (choosing between multiple brands), or censored outcomes (household expenditures that cannot be negative). The mathematical structure of these models typically involves underlying latent variables—unobserved factors like "utility" or "propensity"—that determine the observed discrete or censored outcome.

The primary hurdle in using LDVMs with large datasets is the evaluation of the likelihood function. For a sample of size $n$, the likelihood $p_\theta(y_{1:n}|x_{1:n})$ represents the probability of observing the data $y$ given the predictors $x$ and parameters $\theta$. Because the outcomes are determined by latent variables $z$, the likelihood is defined by an integral over these unobserved factors:

$$
p_\theta(y_{1:n}|x_{1:n}) = \int p_\theta(y_{1:n}, z_{1:n}|x_{1:n}) dz_{1:n}
$$

In many realistic settings, such as multinomial probit models with many alternatives or panel data models with complex random effects, this integral is high-dimensional. Calculating it directly is often impossible. Researchers have traditionally relied on simulation-based methods like Simulated Maximum Likelihood (SML), which approximates the integral by averaging over many simulated draws of $z$. However, SML requires the number of draws to increase with the sample size to maintain accuracy, making it computationally prohibitive as datasets grow into the millions of observations.

## Stochastically Estimated Gradient Ascent (SEGA)

To address these scalability issues, this research introduces Stochastically Estimated Gradient Ascent (SEGA). The method shifts the focus from approximating the likelihood function itself to estimating its gradient—the "score" function. The core of SEGA relies on Fisher’s identity, which relates the gradient of the log-likelihood (the observed score) to the gradient of the augmented-data log-likelihood (the score if we could observe the latent variables).

Fisher's identity states that the observed score $m_n(\theta)$ can be expressed as:

$$
m_n(\theta) = \frac{1}{n} \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(y_{1:n}|x_{1:n}) = \frac{1}{n} \int \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(y_{1:n}, z_{1:n}|x_{1:n}) p_\theta(z_{1:n}|y_{1:n}, x_{1:n}) dz_{1:n}
$$

In simple terms, the score is the expected value of the augmented score, taken over the conditional distribution of the latent variables given the observed data. Calculating this expectation still requires integration. However, SEGA sidesteps the integral by using a single random draw $\tilde{z}_{1:n}$ from the conditional distribution $p_\theta(z|y, x)$ to create a stochastic estimate of the score:

$$
\hat{m}_n(\theta) = \frac{1}{n} \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(y_{1:n}, \tilde{z}_{1:n}|x_{1:n})
$$

Because this estimate is unbiased, it can be used within a stochastic optimization framework. Instead of trying to find the perfect gradient at every step, SEGA moves in the direction of this noisy gradient, knowing that the noise will average out over many iterations.

## The Optimization Framework: SGA and Polyak-Ruppert Averaging

SEGA implements this idea using a Stochastic Gradient Ascent (SGA) algorithm. At each iteration $j$, the parameter vector is updated as follows:

$$
\theta_{j+1} = \theta_j + \eta_{j+1} \odot \hat{m}_n(\theta_j)
$$

Here, $\eta_{j+1}$ is a learning rate or step size. Unlike standard SGA used in machine learning, which typically uses "mini-batches" of data to introduce noise, SEGA uses the entire dataset but introduces noise via the simulation of latent variables. To ensure the algorithm adapts to different parameter scales, the authors utilize the ADADELTA method, which automatically adjusts the learning rate for each dimension based on the history of previous gradients.

While the individual iterates $\theta_j$ will fluctuate due to the simulation noise, the "Polyak-Ruppert averaging" technique is used to produce a stable final estimate. The final SEGA estimator $\theta_n$ is the average of the iterates after a certain "burn-in" period $B$:

$$
\theta_n = \frac{1}{K-B} \sum_{j=B+1}^K \theta_j
$$

This averaging process is crucial because it ensures that the simulation noise—even if generated from just one draw per iteration—cancels out, allowing the estimator to converge to the true Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) as the number of iterations $K$ increases.

## Theoretical Foundations and Inference

A key contribution of this work is the rigorous theoretical justification for using such a noisy gradient. The authors prove two main properties:

1.  **Finite-Sample Concentration:** For a fixed sample size $n$, the averaged estimator $\theta_n$ concentrates around the infeasible MLE (the value that would be obtained if one could perfectly calculate the likelihood) as the number of iterations $K$ increases. The error decreases at a rate of $O(k^{-p\alpha/2})$ where $k$ is the iteration count.
2.  **Asymptotic Equivalence:** If the number of iterations grows sufficiently fast relative to the sample size, the SEGA estimator is asymptotically equivalent to the MLE. This means that $\sqrt{n}(\theta_n - \theta_0)$ follows the same normal distribution as the MLE, where $\theta_0$ is the true parameter value.

For practical application, researchers need to quantify uncertainty via standard errors. SEGA facilitates this through a sandwich variance estimator:

$$
\hat{V} = \hat{H}(\theta_n)^{-1} \hat{I}(\theta_n) \hat{H}(\theta_n)^{-1}
$$

The terms $\hat{H}(\theta_n)$ and $\hat{I}(\theta_n)$ represent the Hessian (curvature) and the variability of the score, respectively. The authors leverage Louis' identity to estimate these quantities using conditional simulations of the latent variables at the final estimated parameter value. This allows for standard frequentist inference, such as constructing confidence intervals and performing Wald tests, without ever needing to evaluate the intractable likelihood function.

## Empirical Application: Multinomial Probit Brand Choice

To demonstrate the power of SEGA, the authors apply it to a large-scale marketing problem: brand choice for pasta. Using a dataset of $1.1$ million purchase observations across 10 brands, they estimate a Multinomial Probit (MNP) model. 

Traditional MNP models are notoriously difficult to estimate because the likelihood involves a $(J-1)$-dimensional integral, where $J$ is the number of brands. Most researchers are forced to assume a simple covariance structure (like the Independent Irrelevant Alternatives assumption) to make the problem tractable. With SEGA, the authors estimate a fully unrestricted covariance matrix. 

The results showed that the unrestricted model significantly improved fit and provided more realistic price elasticities. For instance, the model identified Barilla and San Giorgio as close substitutes, a finding that would be obscured by more restrictive models. Formal Wald tests enabled by the SEGA variance estimator decisively rejected simplified covariance structures, justifying the need for the more complex model that SEGA made possible.

## Empirical Application: Random Effects Tobit Model

The second application focuses on household-level pasta demand using a Random Effects Tobit model. This model accounts for the fact that many households record zero purchases in a given week (censoring) while also modeling household-specific preferences through random intercepts and random coefficients for price and state dependence (the effect of previous purchases).

Estimating this model on 200,000 households over 62 weeks requires integrating over household-specific random effects. SEGA handled this complex heterogeneity structure efficiently. The findings suggested a trade-off in household behavior: those with stronger habits (higher state dependence) tended to be more price-sensitive but had a lower baseline probability of purchasing. These insights into consumer heterogeneity were made accessible by SEGA's ability to handle large-scale panel data with multi-dimensional random effects.

## Comparison with Existing Methods

The research provides a detailed comparison between SEGA and other simulation-based approaches:

*   **Method of Simulated Scores (MSS):** MSS also uses score approximations but requires multiple fixed simulation draws per observation. In practice, MSS is often unstable because the simulation noise does not "smooth out" as easily as it does in the iterative averaging framework of SEGA. Numerical experiments in the paper showed that MSS estimates were highly sensitive to the number of simulation steps, whereas SEGA remained stable.
*   **Simulated Maximum Likelihood (SML):** While SML is the standard approach, its computational cost per iteration is much higher because it requires many draws ($R$) to approximate the likelihood accurately at every single step of the optimization. SEGA effectively spreads the simulation effort over the optimization path, achieving similar accuracy with far less computational time as the model dimension increases.

In tests with a 6-alternative MNP model ($J=6$), SEGA was more than seven times faster than SML while producing nearly identical parameter distributions. As the sample size $n$ increased, the distributions of SEGA, SML, and the exact MLE converged, validating the theoretical claim of asymptotic equivalence.

## Conclusion

SEGA represents a shift in how limited dependent variable models can be estimated. By replacing complex high-dimensional integration with a simple stochastic gradient ascent fueled by single latent variable draws, it enables the analysis of massive datasets that were previously out of reach for likelihood-based methods. Its reliance on Fisher's and Louis' identities provides a robust bridge between stochastic optimization and classical frequentist inference, ensuring that the resulting estimates are not just computationally efficient, but also statistically sound. This approach allows economists and data scientists to move away from restrictive model assumptions, paving the way for more nuanced and accurate empirical findings in large-scale data environments.
