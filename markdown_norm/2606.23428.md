## The Evaluation of Infinite Utility Streams

A fundamental problem in intergenerational social choice theory is the systematic ranking of infinite utility streams. When a social planner evaluates policies that affect an indefinite future, they must compare sequences of utility levels $x = (x_1, x_2, \dots) \in X$, where each $x_n$ represents the well-being of generation $n$. The set of all such streams is denoted by $X = Y^{\mathbb{N}}$, where $Y$ is the set of possible utility levels. The goal is to define a Social Welfare Order (SWO)—a complete and transitive binary relation—that can rank any two streams in $X$.

The challenge in this field arises from the tension between efficiency and equity. Efficiency typically requires that if at least one generation is better off and no one is worse off, the new stream should be preferred. Equity, on the other hand, demands that the distribution of utility across generations be considered, often favoring transfers from the "rich" to the "poor" or prioritizing those at lower utility levels.

While many mathematical proofs establish that such orders exist, they frequently rely on non-constructive tools like the Axiom of Choice or Szpilrajn’s Lemma. These tools guarantee the existence of an order but provide no explicit rule or formula to determine which stream is better. This research addresses this gap by investigating the conditions under which social welfare orders satisfying specific equity axioms can be explicitly constructed and when their existence inherently implies reliance on non-constructive mathematical objects.

## Formalizing Equity and Efficiency Axioms

To analyze the properties of social welfare orders, the paper utilizes several axiomatic requirements. These axioms define the moral and practical constraints placed on the social preferences.

**Efficiency Axioms**
The most common efficiency requirement is Strong Pareto (SP). A social welfare order satisfies SP if, for any two streams $x$ and $y$, whenever $x_n \geq y_n$ for all $n$ and $x_n > y_n$ for at least one $n$, then $x$ is strictly preferred to $y$ ($x \succ y$). A weaker version is Monotonicity (M), which requires that if $x_n \geq y_n$ for all $n$, then $x$ is at least as good as $y$ ($x \succeq y$).

**Equity Axioms**
The paper focuses on consequentialist equity axioms, which look at the outcomes (the utility levels) rather than the process.

1.  **Strong Equity (SE):** This axiom dictates that if we can reduce inequality by moving utility from a richer generation to a poorer one without changing their relative ranking or affecting others, the resulting distribution is strictly better. Formally, if there exist two generations $i$ and $j$ such that $y_i < x_i < x_j < y_j$ and $x_k = y_k$ for all $k \neq i, j$, then $x \succ y$.
2.  **Hammond Equity (HE):** A slightly less demanding version of SE. It suggests that if $y_i < x_i \leq x_j < y_j$ and $x_k = y_k$ for $k \neq i, j$, then $x$ is at least as good as $y$ ($x \succeq y$).
3.  **Pigou-Dalton (PD) Transfer Principle:** This principle focuses on transfers that preserve the total sum of utility. If $x$ is obtained from $y$ by a transfer from a richer generation $j$ to a poorer generation $i$ such that $x_i + x_j = y_i + y_j$ and $|x_i - x_j| < |y_i - y_j|$, while $x_k = y_k$ for all $k \neq i, j$, then $x$ is preferred to $y$.

The research investigates these axioms individually and in combination to determine how they influence the constructibility of the resulting social preferences.

## Explicit Construction via Lexicographic Coding

One of the primary findings of this research is that explicit construction of a social welfare order is possible if the utility domain $Y$ possesses certain order-theoretic properties. The paper introduces a method based on "binary threshold-utility codes" to achieve this.

For a domain $Y$ that is well-ordered (meaning every non-empty subset has a least element, such as the set of natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$ or any finite set), the paper constructs an SWO that satisfies both Strong Pareto (SP) and Strong Equity (SE). The construction works by evaluating how many generations fall below various utility thresholds. For each stream $x$, threshold $t$ in a subset $\bar{Y}$, and generation $n$, a binary code is defined:

$$
B_{t,n}(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x_n \leq t \\ 0 & \text{if } x_n > t \end{cases}
$$

The social planner then compares two streams by looking at these $B_{t,n}(x) \in \{0, 1\}$ values across all pairs of $(t, n)$. The pairs $(t, n)$ are ordered lexicographically in the set $\bar{Y} \times \mathbb{N}$. Specifically, one stream is preferred over another if, at the first threshold-generation pair where their codes differ, the preferred stream has a $0$ (meaning the generation is above the threshold) while the other has a $1$.

This construction effectively prioritizes the welfare of those at lower utility levels. If the binary codes are identical for all thresholds, a standard lexicographic tie-breaker is used to ensure the order satisfies Strong Pareto. This result is significant because it provides a concrete algorithm for ranking infinite streams without needing non-constructive axioms, provided the domain is simple enough.

Further results show that SE alone can be constructed under even broader conditions. If the set of potential "poor" utility levels $L(Y) = \{y \in Y : \exists y' \in Y, y < y'\}$ is well-ordered, the $B$-code method works. Conversely, if the set of potential "rich" utility levels $U(Y) = \{y \in Y : \exists y' \in Y, y' < y\}$ is reverse well-ordered, a similar construction using "reverse codes" $C_{t,n}(x)$ can be employed:

$$
C_{t,n}(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x_n < t \\ 0 & \text{if } x_n \geq t \end{cases}
$$

In this reverse case, the planner prefers $0$ over $1$ at the first point of difference, prioritizing the reduction of utility for the richest generations.

## The Limits of Constructibility and Non-Ramsey Sets

The paper establishes that explicit construction becomes impossible when the utility domain becomes more complex. This is demonstrated by showing that the existence of certain social welfare orders implies the existence of "non-Ramsey" collections. In set theory, a collection of infinite subsets of $\mathbb{N}$ is Ramsey if it is well-behaved under certain partitions; the existence of non-Ramsey sets is a hallmark of non-constructive mathematics, as they cannot be defined without the Axiom of Choice.

Specifically, the paper proves that if the domain $Y$ contains a subset that looks like the negative integers (order type $\omega^*$), any SWO satisfying both Strong Equity (SE) and Monotonicity (M) must be non-constructive. This is because such a domain allows for an infinite sequence of improvements that can be mapped to a non-Ramsey construction.

A similar result is found for the Pigou-Dalton (PD) principle. If the domain contains "descending blocks" with a constant utility gap—for example, sequences where $\alpha_m < \beta_m < \alpha_{\ell} < \beta_{\ell} \text{ for } m > \ell$ and $\beta_m - \alpha_m = \delta > 0$—then any SWO satisfying PD and Monotonicity is non-constructive.

Even without efficiency axioms, the structure of the domain can block construction. On a domain of order type $\mu$ (which contains an integer-like subset $\mathbb{Z}$), the existence of an SWO satisfying Strong Equity (SE) alone entails a non-Ramsey collection. These findings delineate a "complexity boundary": once the set of possible utility levels allows for certain types of infinite descending sequences or gaps, we can no longer describe the social welfare order explicitly, even if we can prove that one must exist.

## Representation through Social Welfare Functions

Beyond the construction of orders, the research examines when these preferences can be represented by a Social Welfare Function (SWF), which is a real-valued mapping $W: X \to \mathbb{R}$. An SWF is useful because it allows the planner to assign a single number to each stream, making comparisons trivial ($x \succeq y$ if and only if $W(x) \geq W(y)$).

The paper provides a contrast between countable and continuous utility domains. For a countable domain $Y$, it is proven that an SWF satisfying Strong Equity (SE) exists. This is achieved by partitioning the set of streams into equivalence classes based on their "tails" and using Szpilrajn’s Lemma to extend the SE requirements into a linear order that can be embedded into the rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$. However, it is noted that this existence proof is itself non-constructive; while the function exists, we cannot necessarily write down its formula.

In contrast, if the utility domain is the unit interval $Y = [0, 1]$, the paper proves an impossibility result: no real-valued SWF can satisfy Strong Equity. The proof uses a separability argument, showing that SE would require an uncountable collection of disjoint open intervals in $\mathbb{R}$, which is impossible. This demonstrates that for continuous utility levels, the demands of Strong Equity are fundamentally incompatible with the structure of the real numbers, regardless of whether any efficiency axioms are imposed.

## Redefining the Landscape of Social Choice

The significance of this work lies in its detailed mapping of the boundaries between what is mathematically possible, what is explicitly describable, and what is representable by numbers.

The results challenge the previous notion that the ability to represent an order with a function (representability) and the ability to describe that order (constructibility) are always linked. The paper shows that these properties depend heavily on the interaction between the specific equity axiom and the order-theoretic structure of the utility domain. For example, while a well-ordered domain allows for explicit construction of SE-satisfying orders, a domain containing negative-integer structures ($\omega^*$) forces us into the realm of non-constructive mathematics.

For researchers in social choice, this means that existence proofs are not enough to ensure a principle is operational. If a social welfare order is non-constructive, it cannot be used to create a clear set of rules for policy makers to follow. The paper suggests that future axiomatic studies should not only ask whether a welfare order exists but also investigate the "domain of constructibility" for those axioms. By identifying these limits, the research provides a framework for choosing equity principles that are not only ethically desirable but also practically applicable.
