## Mechanisms of Binary Black Hole Formation

The detection of hundreds of merging binary black holes (BBHs) by gravitational-wave observatories has transformed our understanding of the high-energy universe. Despite these observations, the specific pathways that lead two massive stars to evolve into black holes that eventually merge remain a central question in astrophysics. The two primary theoretical channels for isolated stellar binary evolution are the common envelope (CE) ejection channel and the stable mass transfer (SMT) channel.

Historically, the CE channel was favored because it provides a rapid way to shrink the binary's orbit. However, recent detailed modeling suggests that many massive binaries may instead undergo SMT, where mass is transferred from one star to another without the binary entering a shared envelope. A key observable for identifying these channels is the black hole spin. Previous models often suggested that SMT would result in black holes with very low or negligible spins. The work by Ma et al. (2026) challenges this assumption, demonstrating that SMT can produce a diverse distribution of black hole spins, ranging from nearly zero to moderate values ($a \approx 0.45$).

## Modeling Stellar Evolution and Tidal Interaction

To understand how black holes acquire spin in the SMT channel, the authors utilized the MESA (Modules for Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics) code to simulate the evolution of binary systems. Each system consists of a massive donor star ($30 M_\odot$ or $50 M_\odot$) and a point-mass companion representing a black hole. The study focuses on systems that undergo stable mass transfer and eventually merge within the age of the universe (13.8 billion years).

A critical component of this research is the treatment of tidal spin-up. When two objects are in a close orbit, tidal forces exert a torque that attempts to synchronize the star's rotation with the orbital period. In massive stars, this is primarily driven by "dynamical tides"—the excitation and dissipation of internal gravity waves. The researchers implemented a first-principles calculation of these tidal torques using a "mode decomposition" method, which sums the effects of individual oscillation modes within the star.

The evolution of the donor star's angular momentum, $J_{\text{spin}}$, is calculated by integrating the balance between tidal torques and angular momentum loss from stellar winds or mass transfer:

$$\frac{dJ_{\text{spin}}}{dt} = \tau_{\text{tide}} + \tau_{\text{mass loss}}$$

As the star eventually undergoes core collapse, the final dimensionless spin of the resulting black hole ($a$) is derived from the angular momentum and mass of the helium core:

$$a = \frac{cJ_{\text{He core}}}{GM_{\text{He core}}^2}$$

## The Diverse Pathways of Stable Mass Transfer

The research identifies three distinct evolutionary histories within the SMT channel, each leading to different spin outcomes for the second-born black hole. These histories are defined by when the mass transfer occurs relative to the star's internal development.

### Case B Systems: Moderate Spin
Case B mass transfer occurs after the donor star has finished burning hydrogen in its core and has begun expanding. In these systems, the donor is often only partially stripped of its hydrogen envelope. This causes the star to maintain a relatively large radius ($R_\star$) compared to the binary separation ($a$). Because the tidal torque is highly sensitive to the ratio $R_\star / a$, these "puffy" stars experience strong tidal forces that keep them nearly synchronized with the orbit during the core helium-burning phase. This leads to moderate final black hole spins, typically between $0.2$ and $0.45$.

### Case A + Case AB Systems: Low Spin
These systems undergo an initial phase of mass transfer while the donor is still on the main sequence (Case A), followed by another rapid phase later (Case AB). This sequence results in the star being almost completely stripped of its hydrogen envelope, leaving behind a compact helium core. Because the resulting star is so small, tidal torques become much weaker. Additionally, the rapid mass transfer phase efficiently removes the angular momentum the star had previously accumulated. Consequently, these systems produce black holes with negligible spins ($a < 0.1$).

### Case A Only Systems: Small but Non-negligible Spin
Found primarily among higher-mass donors (e.g., $50 M_\odot$), these systems experience Case A mass transfer but avoid the subsequent Case AB phase. The star is spun up during its main-sequence phase and, because it does not undergo further rapid stripping, it can retain a portion of that angular momentum as it evolves. This results in black holes with spins typically in the range of $0.1 \lesssim a \lesssim 0.2$.

## Correlations Between Spin and Mass Ratio

One of the most significant findings of this work is the predicted relationship between the effective inspiral spin parameter ($\chi_{\text{eff}}$) and the final mass ratio ($q$) of the merging binary. $\chi_{\text{eff}}$ is a measure of the spin components aligned with the orbital angular momentum, weighted by the masses of the two black holes.

The models show an anti-correlation: systems with lower mass ratios (more unequal masses) tend to have higher effective spins. This occurs because the highest spins are produced in Case B systems. These systems often undergo a "mass-ratio reversal," where the donor star (which becomes the second black hole) starts heavier but transfers significant mass to the first black hole. If the second black hole ends up more massive and is spinning moderately, it dominates the $\chi_{\text{eff}}$ calculation in a system that has an unequal final mass ratio.

## Interpreting Observed Gravitational Wave Events

The results of this study have direct implications for interpreting specific events detected by LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA. A notable example is GW190412, an event that stands out due to its asymmetric masses ($q \approx 0.31$) and a clearly positive effective spin ($\chi_{\text{eff}} \approx 0.22$).

Previously, it was difficult to explain GW190412 using standard isolated binary evolution models because they struggled to produce moderate spins in systems with such unequal masses. Some researchers suggested it might be the result of a "hierarchical merger" in a dense star cluster. However, the SMT Case B models presented in this paper naturally produce binaries with properties that match the 90% credibility interval of GW190412. This suggests that stable mass transfer is a viable and potentially common pathway for creating such "unusual" binaries.

## Significance for Future Research

By moving beyond simplified "naked helium star" models and using detailed stellar structures, Ma et al. (2026) have shown that the SMT channel is capable of producing a much wider variety of black hole spins than previously thought. The key lies in the residual hydrogen envelope and the specific timing of mass transfer, which dictate the strength of tidal interactions.

This diversity means that observations of spinning black holes do not automatically rule out the stable mass transfer channel. Furthermore, the predicted anti-correlation between $\chi_{\text{eff}}$ and $q$ provides a specific "fingerprint" that astronomers can look for in larger populations of gravitational-wave events to distinguish SMT from other formation mechanisms. Future work integrating these detailed physical models into large-scale population synthesis will be essential for refining our understanding of how the most massive stars in the universe end their lives.
