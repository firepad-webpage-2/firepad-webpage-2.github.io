## Overcoming Stationarity in Complex Time Series

Standard time series analysis frequently relies on the assumption of global stationarity, which posits that the underlying statistical properties of a process—such as its mean, variance, and autocorrelation—remain constant over time. While this assumption simplifies mathematical modeling, it is often incompatible with real-world data observed over long periods. Economic shifts, technological changes, and unprecedented global events like the COVID-19 pandemic can cause the dynamics of a system to evolve, either gradually or through abrupt transitions.

When these dynamics change, traditional Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) models, which assume fixed parameters, can provide misleading results. For instance, a model with static seasonal components cannot account for the way a pandemic might permanently or temporarily alter travel patterns or retail cycles. To address these challenges, researchers have developed time-varying models, yet the combination of time-varying Moving Average (MA) components and multiple seasonal periods has remained computationally and theoretically difficult.

The work by Fagerberg, Villani, and Kohn introduces a comprehensive framework for Time-Varying Multi-Seasonal ARMA (TV-SARMA) models. This approach allows for the simultaneous evolution of both regular and seasonal parameters in both the Autoregressive (AR) and Moving Average (MA) parts of the model. By utilizing an exact likelihood-based Bayesian inference scheme and a flexible prior for parameter evolution, the model can capture a wide range of temporal behaviors, from smooth transitions to sudden shocks, while ensuring the model remains mathematically stable and interpretable at every point in time.

## The TV-SARMA Model Architecture

The core of this research is the development of a multiplicative structure that can handle multiple seasonal periods simultaneously. A time series $y_t$ is modeled as a process where the current value depends on its own past (AR) and past noise terms (MA), but these dependencies are allowed to change at every time step $t$.

The general form of the TV-SARMA model is given by:

$$\phi_t(L) \prod_{j=1}^{M} \Phi_{jt}(L^{s_j}) y_t = \psi_t(L) \prod_{j=1}^{M} \Psi_{jt}(L^{s_j}) \epsilon_t$$

where $L$ is the lag operator (e.g., $L y_t = y_{t-1}$), and $\phi_t, \Phi_{jt}, \psi_t, \Psi_{jt}$ are time-varying polynomials representing regular and seasonal AR and MA components. The term $\epsilon_t$ represents the innovation or error term at time $t$, assumed to follow a normal distribution with time-varying variance $\sigma_t^2$:

$$\epsilon_t \sim N(0, \sigma_t^2)$$

This multiplicative structure is particularly powerful because it can represent complex interactions between regular monthly effects and longer-term seasonal cycles (like annual patterns) using a relatively small number of parameters. However, making these parameters time-varying introduces significant challenges. If an AR parameter drifts too far, the model might become explosive (non-stable); if an MA parameter drifts, the model might become non-invertible, meaning the noise terms cannot be uniquely recovered from the observations.

## Guaranteeing Stability and Invertibility

A central contribution of this work is the implementation of a parameterization that guarantees local stability and invertibility at every time step $t$. This is achieved by mapping the AR and MA coefficients to a different space: the space of partial autocorrelations.

Instead of directly modeling the coefficients $\phi$ or $\psi$, which must reside within complex geometric regions to remain stable, the authors model unrestricted parameters $\theta_t \in \mathbb{R}^p$. These are then mapped to partial autocorrelations $r_k \in (-1, 1)^p$ using the transformation:

$$r_k = \frac{\theta_k}{\sqrt{1 + \theta_k^2}}$$

Using a recursive algorithm, these partial autocorrelations are transformed back into the coefficients used in the model. For an AR process, the coefficients $\phi$ are updated as:

$$\phi_{k,j} = \phi_{k-1,j} - r_k \phi_{k-1,k-j}$$

By modeling the process in this unrestricted space $\theta_t$, the authors ensure that no matter how the parameters evolve over time, the resulting model at any specific time $t$ will always be stable and invertible. This is a critical requirement for generating reliable forecasts and for the mathematical validity of the likelihood function.

## Modeling Parameter Evolution via Dynamic Shrinkage

Real-world parameters rarely evolve in a simple, linear fashion. A model must be able to stay constant during stable periods but react quickly to shocks. To achieve this, the authors employ a Dynamic Shrinkage Process (DSP) prior.

The evolution of each unrestricted parameter $\theta_{kt}$ is modeled as a random walk, where the variance of the steps is itself time-varying. The innovation $\nu_{kt}$ for a parameter at time $t$ is:

$$\nu_{kt} \sim N(0, \exp(h_{kt}))$$

The log-variance $h_{kt}$ follows its own process:

$$h_{kt} = \mu_k + \kappa_k(h_{k,t-1} - \mu_k) + \eta_{kt}$$

The term $\eta_{kt}$ follows a heavy-tailed Z-distribution:

$$\eta_{kt} \sim Z(1/2, 1/2, 0, 1)$$

This specific distribution for the volatility of the parameters is what allows for "shrinkage." Most of the time, the innovations are very small, meaning the parameters $\theta_t$ (and thus the AR/MA coefficients) stay almost constant. However, the heavy tails of the Z-distribution allow for occasional large jumps, enabling the model to adapt to sudden structural breaks in the data, such as the onset of a financial crisis or a pandemic.

## Bayesian Inference and Exact Likelihood

Estimating MA models is inherently more difficult than AR models because the likelihood function is non-linear—the errors $\epsilon_t$ are not directly observed and must be inferred alongside the parameters. Many existing methods use a "conditional" likelihood, which assumes initial errors are zero. This work, however, utilizes the "exact" likelihood, which is more rigorous and yields better results for short or highly dependent series.

The authors develop a Gibbs sampling algorithm that treats the latent errors $\epsilon_t$ and the unobserved pre-sample observations as hidden states. The inference process involves several steps:

1.  **Sampling Parameters**: Given the current estimate of errors and volatilities, the time-varying parameters $\theta_{0:T}$ are sampled. Because the relationship between $\theta_t$ and the observation $y_t$ is non-linear due to the stability mapping and the MA terms, the authors use an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) within a Forward Filtering Backward Sampling (FFBSx) framework.
2.  **Sampling Errors and Pre-sample Data**: The model is recast into a state-space form where the errors $\epsilon_t$ are the states. This allows the use of standard Kalman filtering techniques to update the entire path of errors.
3.  **Sampling Stochastic Volatility**: The observation noise $\sigma_t^2$ is also allowed to evolve. The authors extend traditional stochastic volatility models by applying the same DSP prior to the noise process, allowing the model to distinguish between parameter changes and simple increases in noise.

## Application: Air Passenger Traffic and COVID-19

To demonstrate the real-world utility of the model, the authors analyzed monthly air passenger data for the route between JFK (New York) and Miami. This dataset is characterized by strong annual seasonality and was subject to a massive disruption during the COVID-19 pandemic.

The model revealed several key insights:
*   **Vanishing Seasonality**: During the height of the pandemic (2020), the seasonal MA coefficients dropped toward zero. This indicates that the typical seasonal patterns of travel were almost entirely disrupted, replaced by a process driven more by the immediate shocks of the health crisis.
*   **Increased Persistence**: The regular MA coefficient increased significantly during the pandemic, suggesting that shocks to passenger numbers had a more lasting impact during this period of high uncertainty than they did during the stable pre-pandemic years.
*   **Volatility Spikes**: The stochastic volatility component successfully identified a massive spike in noise during 2020. The use of the DSP prior allowed the model to produce a sharper spike than traditional models, better reflecting the suddenness of the pandemic's impact.

## Conclusion

The Time-Varying Multi-Seasonal ARMA framework provides a flexible and mathematically rigorous solution for analyzing non-stationary time series. By combining multiplicative seasonality, guaranteed parameter stability, and dynamic shrinkage priors, it allows researchers to model complex systems that change over time. The use of exact likelihood and efficient Bayesian sampling ensures that these models are not just theoretically sound but also practically applicable to high-frequency data where multiple seasonal cycles and structural breaks are the norm rather than the exception.
