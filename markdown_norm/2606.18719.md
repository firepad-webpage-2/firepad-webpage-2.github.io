## Reevaluating the Landscape of International Export Intermediaries

In the classical models of international trade, such as the Melitz (2003) model, manufacturers are typically viewed as the primary agents of export, selling their goods directly to foreign markets. However, empirical data has consistently shown that a significant portion of international trade is handled by firms that do not manufacture the goods they sell—commonly referred to as intermediaries. In many economies, these firms, typically classified within the wholesale and retail sectors, account for nearly half of all exporters and a quarter of total export value.

Recent research by Garmendia-Lazcano, Mínguez, and Minondo suggests that these figures may be misleading. By refining the classification of firms within the wholesale and retail sectors, the authors argue that the traditional definition of an "intermediary" encompasses a heterogeneous group of firms that serve very different functions in the global value chain. Their analysis indicates that once distribution arms of manufacturers and vertically integrated brand owners are separated, the role of "pure" intermediaries—firms that strictly buy from unrelated producers to sell abroad—is significantly smaller than previously estimated.

## The Heterogeneity of Wholesale and Retail Exporters

Standard economic research often identifies intermediaries using broad industrial classification codes, such as NACE Rev. 2 codes 46 (wholesale) and 47 (retail). While this approach captures firms that do not primarily engage in manufacturing, it fails to distinguish between different business models. The paper identifies three distinct categories within this sector:

1.  **Pure Intermediaries:** These are the archetypal intermediaries described in trade theory. They purchase products from unrelated manufacturers who may lack the scale or expertise to export directly. These firms specialize in marketing, logistics, and navigating foreign regulatory environments.
2.  **Export Arms:** These are distribution subsidiaries owned by manufacturing or primary-sector parent companies. While they are classified as wholesalers, they exclusively or primarily handle the products of their parent organization. Their purpose is often related to centralizing international operations, tax optimization, or risk management rather than bridging a productivity gap for the producer.
3.  **Vertically Integrated Firms:** These companies control the entire value chain, from design and branding to distribution. Although they may outsource the physical manufacturing process to third parties (common in the apparel and electronics industries), they own the intellectual property and manage the brand. They do not fit the intermediary model because they are the primary drivers of the product's market presence.

The distinction is critical because each type of firm responds to different economic incentives and requires different policy considerations. If the majority of "intermediary" exports are actually driven by vertically integrated firms or export arms, the perceived need for policies that match small manufacturers with third-party wholesalers might be overstated.

## Methodological Innovations in Firm Classification

Classifying thousands of firms into these nuanced categories presents a significant empirical challenge. Traditionally, researchers might rely on manual inspection, which is difficult to scale, or basic financial ratios, which can be imprecise. To address this, the authors utilized three reasoning-optimized Large Language Models (LLMs): ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini.

The methodology involved feeding the LLMs detailed descriptions of firms—derived from their names, activities, and public records—alongside a strict set of classification rules. The authors implemented a consensus-based assignment system: a firm was assigned to a category only if at least two of the three models agreed. This approach successfully categorized 98.3% of the 7,600 Spanish wholesale and retail exporters in the sample.

To ensure accuracy, the authors performed manual reviews of the top 200 exporters (accounting for 60% of the export value) and a random sample of 366 firms. The high level of agreement (over 90%) between the LLM consensus and the manual review demonstrates that these tools can effectively interpret complex business structures that traditional industrial codes miss.

## The Shrinking Share of Pure Intermediaries

The results of this refined classification are stark. When applying the standard definition (all wholesalers and retailers), these firms accounted for 40.2% of exporters and 26.0% of Spanish goods exports in 2024. However, after the reclassification, the figures changed dramatically:

*   **Pure Intermediaries:** Their share of total exports dropped from 26.0% to only 7.7%. While they still represent 23.4% of all exporters, they tend to be smaller firms with lower export volumes.
*   **Vertically Integrated Firms:** These firms emerged as a dominant force, accounting for 10.8% of total exports. Within the wholesale and retail sector specifically, they represent over 41% of the export value.
*   **Export Arms:** These distribution subsidiaries accounted for 7.5% of total exports, nearly equal to the share of pure intermediaries.

These findings suggest that a large portion of what was previously considered "intermediated trade" is actually "direct trade" conducted through different organizational structures. ## Comparative Analysis of Firm Characteristics

To further understand these differences, the authors employed an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression to compare the characteristics of the various exporter types. The model is structured as follows:

$$\ln y_i = \beta_1 \text{Export arm}_i + \beta_2 \text{Vertically integrated_i} + \beta_3 \text{Manufacturer}_i + \gamma_s + \gamma_k + \epsilon_i$$

In this equation, $y_i$ represents specific outcomes for firm $i$, such as the number of employees, value added per employee, or export value. The variables $\text{Export arm}_i$, $\text{Vertically integrated}_i$, and $\text{Manufacturer}_i$ are indicators for those firm types, with pure intermediaries serving as the reference group. The terms $\gamma_s$ and $\gamma_k$ represent fixed effects for size (employment decile) and product category (HS 6-digit code), respectively, while $\epsilon_i$ is the error term. The coefficients $\beta_1$, $\beta_2$, and $\beta_3$ measure the differences relative to pure intermediaries.

The regression results revealed several key patterns:
*   **Scale and Productivity:** Export arms and vertically integrated firms are significantly larger and more productive than pure intermediaries. Export arms employ roughly 2.3 times more workers, and vertically integrated firms employ 2.2 times more, compared to pure intermediaries.
*   **Value Added:** Even when controlling for size, export arms and vertically integrated firms exhibit higher value added per employee than pure intermediaries.
*   **Export Reach:** While pure intermediaries tend to serve a larger number of destination countries than export arms, they serve fewer than vertically integrated firms and manufacturers. This suggests that pure intermediaries may specialize in broad market access for many small products, whereas vertically integrated firms leverage their brand strength to penetrate markets more deeply.

## Implications for International Trade Policy

The findings of this study have significant implications for how governments approach export promotion. Traditional views suggest that because many small firms lack the productivity to export directly, policies should focus on connecting them with intermediaries. However, if pure intermediaries account for a much smaller share of aggregate exports than previously believed, the impact of such "matching" policies may be limited.

Instead, the prominence of vertically integrated firms and export arms suggests that the most successful "non-manufacturing" exporters are those that either control their own brands or are part of larger, integrated production networks. This shifts the focus toward policies that help firms develop intellectual property, branding, and sophisticated distribution networks.

Furthermore, the study highlights the limitations of using standard industrial classification codes in economic research. The ability of LLMs to provide a more nuanced view of firm activity suggests a new path for empirical trade research, allowing for a better understanding of how firms organize their global operations. By moving beyond the binary view of "manufacturer vs. wholesaler," researchers and policymakers can more accurately assess the health and dynamics of a nation's export sector.
