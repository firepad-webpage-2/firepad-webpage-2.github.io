## Moiré Superlattices and the Hubbard Model

The study of two-dimensional materials has been transformed by the discovery of moiré superlattices. When two layers of atomically thin materials, such as transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), are stacked with a small twist angle or a slight lattice mismatch, a large-scale interference pattern emerges. This moiré pattern creates a periodic potential that traps electrons, effectively slowing them down and making their mutual interactions—known as strong correlations—dominant over their kinetic energy.

Twisted bilayer TMDs have emerged as a particularly versatile platform for this physics. These systems can host a wide array of electronic states, ranging from correlated insulators to superconductors and topological phases like the quantum anomalous Hall effect. A central tool for describing the low-energy physics of these materials is the Hubbard model. This model simplifies the complex behavior of electrons into two main components: the "hopping" of electrons between neighboring sites on a lattice and the energy penalty ($U$) they pay when two electrons occupy the same site.

In twisted TMDs, the lattice is typically triangular. A unique feature of these materials is the influence of a displacement field—an external electric field applied perpendicular to the layers. While the displacement field is technically spin-independent, the way it interacts with the specific electronic structure of TMDs (which features "spin-valley locking") results in an effective spin-orbit coupling. This manifests as a spin-dependent "staggered flux" in the Hubbard model, where electrons of different spins experience different phases as they hop between sites.

## The Minimal Moiré Hubbard Model

To understand how the displacement field modifies the behavior of these systems, researchers utilize a minimal moiré Hubbard model on a triangular lattice. At "half-filling"—where there is exactly one electron for every lattice site—the competition between kinetic energy and interaction strength determines the ground state of the system. The Hamiltonian (the mathematical description of the system's energy) is given by:

$$
H = - \sum_{\langle i,j \rangle, \sigma} t e^{i\sigma\phi_{ij}} c^\dagger_{i\sigma} c_{j\sigma} + h.c. + U \sum_i n_{i\uparrow} n_{i\downarrow}
$$

In this equation, $t$ represents the hopping amplitude, and $U$ is the onsite repulsion. The index $\sigma = \uparrow, \downarrow$ denotes the spin (or more accurately, the spin-valley index). The term $e^{i\sigma\phi_{ij}}$ introduces the hopping phase $\phi$, which represents the effect of the displacement field. Crucially, this phase induces a staggered flux pattern: in a single triangular unit of the lattice, the phase accumulates to $+ 3\phi$ for spin-up electrons and $- 3\phi$ for spin-down electrons. 

This staggered flux breaks the rotational symmetry of the lattice and the high spin symmetry ($SU(2)$) usually found in the standard Hubbard model, reducing it to a simpler $U(1)$ symmetry. The primary goal of current research is to determine how this reduction in symmetry and the presence of the flux alter the phases of matter that can exist in the system.

## Computational Methodology: DMRG

To solve this model, the researchers employed the Density Matrix Renormalization Group (DMRG) method. DMRG is a powerful numerical technique used to find the ground state of strongly correlated systems with high precision. Because the triangular lattice is "frustrated"—meaning the geometry makes it impossible for all neighboring spins to align in a simple way—it is notoriously difficult to simulate.

The simulations were performed on cylindrical geometries, specifically "zigzag" cylinders (YC), which are standard for these types of problems. By using cylinders of varying circumferences (such as $L_y = 4$ and $L_y = 6$) and lengths (up to $L_x = 48$), the researchers can extrapolate the behavior of the system to the infinite two-dimensional limit. They explicitly preserved the symmetries of the system, specifically $U(1)_{\text{charge}} \times U(1)_{\text{spin}}$, and utilized bond dimensions up to $D = 10000$ to ensure that the numerical errors (truncation errors) remained extremely small, typically between $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-7}$ for insulating phases.

## Reshaping the Phase Diagram

At zero displacement field ($\phi = 0$), the triangular Hubbard model is known to exhibit a transition from a metal (at low $U$) to an antiferromagnet (at high $U$). In the middle, for $9 \lesssim U/t \lesssim 10.5$, a "Chiral Spin Liquid" (CSL) is believed to exist. The CSL is a rare state of matter where the spins do not order even at absolute zero, yet they break time-reversal symmetry.

The current research shows that a finite displacement field significantly reshapes this landscape. The most notable results include the rapid suppression of the CSL phase and the emergence of a "Chiral Antiferromagnet" (Chiral AFM). 

### Suppression of the Chiral Spin Liquid

The Chiral Spin Liquid is characterized by its lack of magnetic order and the presence of "scalar chirality." Scalar chirality is measured by the correlation function $\langle \chi_i \chi_j \rangle$, where the operator $\chi_i = \vec{S}_i \cdot (\vec{S}_{i+e_1} \times \vec{S}_{i+e_2})$ describes the non-coplanarity of three spins on a triangle. If this value is non-zero, it means the spins are "curled" in a specific direction.

The researchers found that the CSL phase is very fragile when a displacement field is applied. It survives only up to a very small phase of approximately $\phi \approx 0.02\pi$. This suggests that the lowering of symmetry caused by the displacement field reduces the quantum fluctuations needed to keep the spins in a liquid-like state, pushing them toward a more ordered magnetic state.

### Discovery of Chiral Antiferromagnetism

Perhaps the most significant finding is the nature of the system at strong coupling ($U/t = 12$). As the displacement field increases, the system transitions from a standard 120-degree antiferromagnet—where spins are arranged in a flat, coplanar pattern—into a "Chiral AFM."

In a standard antiferromagnet, the scalar chirality typically decays to zero over long distances. However, for $\phi \neq 0$, the researchers observed a persistent, long-range scalar chirality correlation $\langle \chi_i \chi_j \rangle$. This means that even though the spins have formed a settled magnetic pattern, they also maintain a persistent "chiral" twist. This state breaks a composite symmetry denoted as $\tilde{T} = e^{-i\pi S^z_{\text{tot}}} T$, which is a combination of a spin rotation and time-reversal. This is a unique state that bridges the properties of ordered magnets and those of topological spin liquids.

## Phase Transitions and Topological Continuity

A major question in condensed matter physics is how one phase of matter turns into another. When transitioning from the CSL to the Chiral AFM at a fixed phase (e.g., $\phi = 0.005\pi$), the researchers found evidence that the transition is continuous. 

This is unusual because the CSL is a "topologically ordered" phase (meaning it has hidden, global entanglement), while the Chiral AFM is a conventionally ordered phase. Usually, such transitions are abrupt (first-order). However, because the scalar chirality remains non-zero in both phases, the transition might be described by a theory of "spinon condensation." In this scenario, the fractionalized excitations of the spin liquid (spinons) "condense" to form the magnetic order, potentially leading to a state that retains some of the topological features of the liquid. This suggests the existence of "fractionalized quantum criticality," a topic of intense theoretical interest.

## Incommensurate Spin-Density Waves

At lower values of the interaction strength $U/t$, the system behaves differently. For $U/t \leq 8$ and a displacement field of $\phi = 0.04\pi$, the system develops Incommensurate Spin-Density Wave (IC-SDW) phases. 

In these phases, the magnetization of the system varies spatially with a wavelength that does not match the underlying lattice period (it is "incommensurate"). This behavior is driven by "Fermi surface nesting." Because the displacement field splits the Fermi surfaces of spin-up and spin-down electrons, certain parts of the surfaces can be mapped onto each other by specific vectors, triggering the formation of a wave-like magnetic pattern. The researchers identified both an IC-SDW metal (where electrons can still move) and an IC-SDW insulator (where they are frozen).

## Experimental Implications

The theoretical phase diagram produced in this work provides a roadmap for experimentalists studying twisted bilayer TMDs, such as WSe$_2$. Several key predictions emerge:

1.  **Detection of Chirality:** The persistence of scalar chirality in both the spin liquid and the antiferromagnetic regimes suggests that these materials should exhibit a strong Magneto-Optical Kerr Effect (MOKE) and a thermal Hall effect, even when they are not in a perfectly "liquid" state.
2.  **Displacement Field Tuning:** The phase diagram predicts that by simply adjusting the gate voltage (which controls the displacement field), researchers can drive the material through a sequence of exotic states: from a superconductor or metal to a CSL, and finally to a chiral antiferromagnet.
3.  **Intermediate Insulators:** Experimental observations of a "non-magnetic insulator" between superconducting and magnetic phases in WSe$_2$ match the region of the phase diagram where the CSL is predicted to exist.

By combining large-scale numerical simulations with the specific constraints of the moiré superlattice, this research establishes the moiré Hubbard model as a primary laboratory for exploring the intersection of topology, magnetism, and strong electron correlations.
