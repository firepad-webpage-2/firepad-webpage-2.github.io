## Reimagining Risk: From Prediction to Triage

Risk assessment instruments are fundamental to high-stakes decision-making in domains like healthcare and criminal justice. Conventionally, these "risk scores" estimate the probability that an individual will experience an undesirable outcome—such as failing to appear in court or experiencing a medical complication—under a baseline condition of "no intervention." When this estimated risk exceeds a specific threshold, an intervention is typically recommended.

However, this traditional approach is inherently one-sided. It focuses exclusively on what might happen if no action is taken, ignoring the potential outcomes that would result if an intervention were actually applied. In the context of pretrial justice, a judge deciding whether to release a defendant on their own recognizance (ROR) or impose cash bail needs to know more than just the baseline risk of reoffending. They need to understand how that risk changes under different conditions and account for the ethical and social costs of their decision, such as the "regret" associated with unnecessarily detaining someone who would not have committed a crime if released.

The research by Imai et al. introduces a new class of counterfactual risk assessment instruments called "triage scores." Unlike standard risk scores, triage scores are derived from a decision-theoretic framework that considers the joint distribution of potential outcomes under all available choices. This shift from simple prediction to counterfactual evaluation allows for decision-making that more closely aligns with maximizing overall societal welfare.

## The Limitations of Conventional Risk Scores

Traditional risk scores face three primary methodological and ethical hurdles. First, they provide **incomplete information**. By predicting only the outcome under no intervention, they fail to distinguish between individuals for whom an intervention is effective and those for whom it is not. A high-risk individual might reoffend regardless of whether they are granted ROR or given cash bail; in such a case, the intervention of cash bail may be futile.

Second, they struggle with **ethical and practical trade-offs**. A decision-maker often weighs the cost of an adverse outcome (a new crime) against the cost of the intervention itself (the loss of liberty and financial burden of bail). Standard risk scores do not explicitly incorporate these costs, nor do they account for the counterfactual "regret" of a decision—the realization that a different choice would have yielded a better outcome.

Finally, these scores are susceptible to the **selective labels problem**. In historical data, outcomes are only observed for the decision that was actually made. If a judge detains a defendant, we never observe whether that defendant would have committed a crime if they had been released. Evaluating and learning optimal policies requires a framework that can rigorously account for these missing counterfactual outcomes.

## Defining Triage Scores and Principal Strata

The core innovation of this work is the "triage score," which generalizes the standard risk score by looking at the vector of potential outcomes for each individual. For a decision $d$ out of $K$ possible choices, let $Y_i(d)$ be the potential outcome for individual $i$. The full vector of potential outcomes is $\mathbf{Y}_i = (Y_i(0), Y_i(1), \dots, Y_i(K-1))$.

To understand the utility of triage scores, consider the binary case of pretrial release ($d=0$ for ROR, $d=1$ for cash bail) and rearrest ($y=0$ for no crime, $y=1$ for crime). Individuals can be categorized into four "principal strata" based on their joint potential outcomes $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1))$:

1.  **Safe:** $(0, 0)$ — No rearrest under either ROR or cash bail.
2.  **Backlash:** $(0, 1)$ — Rearrest only if cash bail is imposed (e.g., due to the destabilizing effect of detention).
3.  **Preventable:** $(1, 0)$ — Rearrest only if released (the intervention works).
4.  **Hopeless:** $(1, 1)$ — Rearrest regardless of the decision.

A triage score assigns a utility to a decision based on which stratum an individual belongs to. While a standard risk score only looks at $Y_i(0)$, a triage score can differentiate between a "Preventable" case and a "Hopeless" case, even though both have a baseline risk of $Y_i(0)=1$. This distinction is vital: if an outcome is "Hopeless," imposing a costly intervention like cash bail may not be justified since it does not change the outcome.

## The Additive Counterfactual Utility Framework

To make the evaluation of triage scores mathematically tractable, the authors introduce the **additive counterfactual utility** assumption. This assumption states that the utility of a decision $d$ depends not only on the realized outcome $Y_i(d)$ but also on the counterfactual outcomes $\mathbf{Y}_i(-d)$ that would have occurred under alternative decisions. 

Formally, the utility $u(d, Y_i(d), \mathbf{Y}_i(-d))$ is additive if it can be decomposed as:

$$u(d, y, \mathbf{y}_{-d}) = v_d(y) + \sum_{d' \neq d} w_{d, d'}(y_{d'})$$

In this expression, $v_d(y)$ represents the utility associated with the choice $d$ and the resulting outcome $y$. The term $w_{d, d'}(y_{d'})$ captures the counterfactual utility—for example, the regret felt when a decision $d$ is made but a different decision $d'$ would have resulted in a better outcome $y_{d'}$. This additive structure is proven to be both necessary and sufficient for identifying the expected utility of a policy using only observed data, provided that the decisions are "unconfounded" (i.e., we have recorded all the information the decision-maker used).

## Statistical Identification and Evaluation

The research provides a framework for evaluating three types of decision-making systems: human-alone, human-with-AI recommendations, and AI-alone (automated) policies. The objective is to estimate the expected utility:

$$ \mathbb{E}[U(\delta)] = \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{d=0}^{K-1} \mathbb{1}\{\delta(X_i) = d\} u(d, Y_i(d), \mathbf{Y}_i(-d)) \right] $$

where $\delta(X_i)$ is the decision rule being evaluated. 

To estimate this, the authors utilize **Augmented Inverse Probability Weighting (AIPW)**. This estimator is "doubly robust," meaning it provides a consistent estimate if either the decision model (predicting the probability of a judge's choice $P(D_i = d | X_i, Z_i, R_i)$) or the outcome model (predictive probability of rearrest) is correctly specified. Here, $X_i$ denotes pre-treatment covariates, $Z_i$ is the randomized assignment of the AI tool, and $R_i$ is the AI's recommendation.

## Integrating Unstructured Data with GenAI-Powered Inference

A significant challenge in identifying causal effects from human decisions is ensuring that all relevant confounding information is accounted for. In the legal system, judges often rely on unstructured text, such as **probable-cause affidavits**, which contain details not found in structured databases. If a researcher ignores this text, the unconfoundedness assumption is likely violated, leading to biased estimates of utility.

The authors address this by incorporating **GenAI-powered inference (GPI)**. They use pre-trained large language models (LLMs) to extract low-dimensional features from the text of affidavits. These features are then fed into a "DragonNet" architecture—a neural network designed to learn a shared representation of the data that is optimized for both predicting the judge's decision and the eventual outcome. This allows the model to "deconfound" the analysis by effectively seeing what the judge saw.

## Empirical Insights: The Utah Pretrial Study

The researchers applied their triage score framework to data from a randomized controlled trial in Utah evaluating the Public Safety Assessment (PSA), a common pretrial risk tool. In the study, judges were randomly assigned to either receive the PSA recommendation ($Z=1$) or not ($Z=0$).

The analysis defined several key utility parameters to explore different ethical stances:
*   $c_{cash}$: The inherent cost of imposing cash bail (loss of liberty, economic hardship).
*   $c_{cash\_\text{crime}}$: The cost of an undesirable outcome (e.g., failure to appear) specifically when it occurs despite an intervention.
*   $r_{ror\_\neg \text{crime}}$: The regret associated with releasing someone on ROR when they do not commit a crime, compared to the "unnecessary" detention of that same person under cash bail.

The researchers set $c_{ror\_\text{crime}}=1$ as a baseline and defined the relative regret for cash bail as $r_{cash\_\neg \text{crime}} = r_{ror\_\neg \text{crime}} \frac{c_{cash\_\text{crime}}}{c_{ror\_\text{crime}}}$.

### Comparing Frameworks
The study compared four frameworks:
1.  **Standard Risk:** Focuses only on $Y_i(0)$ with no counterfactuals.
2.  **Standard Triage:** Considers joint outcomes but ignores counterfactual regret.
3.  **Counterfactual Risk:** Includes regret but only based on the baseline outcome.
4.  **Counterfactual Triage:** The full model incorporating joint outcomes and regret.

The results showed that the preferred decision policy changed significantly depending on the framework. For instance, the standard risk score framework often recommended more aggressive use of cash bail because it failed to account for the "regret" of unnecessarily detaining individuals who would have been safe if released.

### Optimal Policy Learning
When learning an optimal decision rule $\delta(X_i)$, the triage framework frequently yielded policies that recommended less cash bail than the human judges currently do, particularly when the cost $c_{cash}$ was high. Interestingly, the optimal policy derived from a triage score differed from the one derived from a risk score. The triage score was able to identify individuals for whom cash bail was likely to be counterproductive (the "Backlash" stratum), leading to more targeted and potentially more effective interventions.

## Implications for Algorithmic Decision Support

This work demonstrates that the way we define "risk" and "utility" fundamentally dictates the advice provided by algorithmic tools. By moving from simple risk scores to triage scores, we can create decision-support systems that:

*   **Explicitly model intervention effects**, rather than assuming an intervention is always beneficial if risk is high.
*   **Incorporate diverse ethical values**, such as the weight given to liberty versus public safety, through adjustable utility parameters.
*   **Utilize all available information**, including the rich context found in textual records, to ensure fair and accurate evaluations.

The development of triage scores provides a path toward algorithm-assisted decision-making that is more transparent, ethically grounded, and focused on the joint consequences of our choices. Future research may extend these concepts to "dynamic triage scores," where sequences of decisions are evaluated over time, further refining how AI can support complex human judgments in a changing world.
