## Bridging the Gap in Piano Performance Understanding

The field of Music Information Retrieval (MIR) and symbolic music generation has long been centered on the "ideal" performance. Datasets like ATEPP or ASAP consist primarily of high-quality, professional recordings from competitions and concerts. While these are invaluable for modeling virtuosity, they represent only a narrow slice of the musical world. In reality, the vast majority of piano playing occurs in practice rooms, during sight-reading sessions, and through pedagogical demonstrations by teachers and students of all ages.

Current computational models often struggle to generalize across this full spectrum. Models trained on professional data may fail to diagnose a beginner's mistake or provide meaningful feedback on a student's progress because they lack a representation of what "intermediate" or "developing" playing looks like. This research introduces MAJEPPA (Morphing and Assessing in a Unified Piano Performance Space), a framework designed to bridge this gap. By curating a diverse dataset and developing a Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA), the authors create a unified space that can both generate performances and provide abstract, transferable representations for understanding the pedagogical nuances of piano playing.

## A New Dataset for the Real-World Performance Spectrum

A core contribution of this work is the MAJEPPA dataset, which significantly expands the scope of symbolic music data available for research. Most existing datasets are biased towards professional excellence; MAJEPPA, however, captures the "messy" reality of music education.

The researchers collected 3,979 recordings totaling over 300 hours of piano performance. These recordings were sourced from YouTube by targeting keywords like "practice," "sight-reading," and "learning progress." This approach captured everything from a child's first few weeks with a method book (like Czerny or Burgmüller) to a teacher's slow demonstration and a virtuoso’s concert preparation.

Each recording was manually annotated along two primary axes:
1.  **Performer Expertise:** Categorized from "Child beginner" and "Adult intermediate" to "Piano teacher" and "Virtuoso."
2.  **Recording Context:** Categorized by the formality of the session, such as "Practice," "Sight-reading," "Demo," or "Concert."

To make this data useful for machine learning, the researchers used a state-of-the-art automatic piano transcription system to convert the audio into MIDI. They then aligned these performances with their corresponding musical scores using Subsequence Dynamic Time Warping (DTW). This alignment is critical because it allows the model to see exactly how a performer's physical execution deviates from the intended notation, capturing the essence of expression—and mistakes.

## Methodology: The LLM-JEPA Framework

The technical backbone of MAJEPPA is an adaptation of the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA). Most traditional music models are either purely generative (learning to predict the next note) or based on masked reconstruction (filling in blanks). While powerful, these methods can sometimes focus too much on low-level note details rather than high-level expressive or pedagogical structures.

The MAJEPPA framework uses an LLM-JEPA approach, where a musical score $s$ and a performance $p$ are treated as two different "views" of the same musical idea. The goal is to learn a representation where the model can predict the abstract embedding of a performance based solely on the score and some conditioning information.

### Architecture and Two-Pass Masking

The model uses a pre-trained transformer (Aria-medium) as its foundation. To learn a unified representation, it processes a sequence containing the score, conditioning tokens (like skill level), special predictor tokens, and the performance itself: `[score; COND; PRED; performance]`.

The key to the learning process is a unique two-pass attention masking strategy:

*   **Generative Pass:** The model uses a standard causal mask. The performance tokens can attend to everything before them, including the score. This allows the model to learn how to generate an expressive performance $p$ conditioned on a score $s$.
*   **JEPA Pass:** The model uses a "block-diagonal" mask that makes the score and performance views mutually invisible. The predictor token $PRED$ can only see the score and conditioning tokens. It must generate a prediction of the performance's latent state without actually seeing the performance tokens.

By forcing the model to predict the latent embedding of a performance $p$ from the score $s$ (represented as $z_s$ and $z_p$), the framework encourages the development of abstract embeddings that capture the "style" and "quality" of a performance rather than just the individual notes.

## Mathematical Objectives

The model is trained with a multi-task objective function that balances generation and representation learning. The total loss $L$ is defined as:

$$L = \lambda_1 L_{gen} + \lambda_2 L_{piece} + \lambda_3 L_{cond} + \lambda_4 L_{self}$$

Where:
*   **$L_{gen}$ (Generative Loss):** A standard cross-entropy loss for next-token prediction, ensuring the model remains capable of generating musical performances.
*   **$L_{piece}$ (Piece Alignment):** A contrastive loss (InfoNCE) that pulls the predicted score embedding $z_s$ and the actual performance embedding $z_p$ together. This teaches the model that different performances of the same piece should share a common latent relationship to the score.
*   **$L_{cond}$ (Cross-Condition Clustering):** A supervised contrastive loss that uses the categorical tags (like skill level). It encourages performances with similar expertise levels (e.g., two different "Adult beginners") to cluster together in the embedding space. This is achieved using similarity weights $w_{ij}$ based on the ordinal distance between tags.
*   **$L_{self}$ (Within-Performance Alignment):** This ensures that different segments from the same performance recording are more similar to each other than to segments from other recordings, promoting internal consistency.

The model also uses a projection head $\phi: \mathbb{R}^{1536} \to \mathbb{R}^{512}$ to map high-dimensional transformer states into a normalized $\ell_2$ space for these contrastive calculations.

## The EVPMR Benchmark

To evaluate how well these representations actually capture performance nuances, the researchers introduced the Evaluation of Piano MIDI Representations (EVPMR) benchmark. This suite covers several distinct tasks:

1.  **Performance Assessment:** Regressing a quality score for a performance. This tests if the embeddings correlate with human judgments of "good" playing.
2.  **Chopin Competition Ranking:** Predicting which of two performers advanced further in a world-class competition. This requires capturing subtle expressive differences at the highest level of skill.
3.  **Technique Detection:** Classifying specific piano techniques (e.g., scales, arpeggios, staccato).
4.  **Pianist Identification:** Identifying which of 8 famous pianists is playing.
5.  **Unreferenced Mistake Prediction (UMP):** Detecting "conspicuous mistakes" without having access to the original score during the evaluation. This is a difficult task that mimics a human listener noticing a "wrong note" simply because it sounds out of place.

## Insights from the Embedding Space

The results showed that MAJEPPA outperformed existing symbolic music models (like MusicBERT and Aria) across nearly all benchmark tasks. However, the qualitative findings offer perhaps the most interesting look into what the model has actually learned.

### Mapping the Learning Journey
The researchers analyzed "practice journeys"—series of recordings where a student practiced the same piece over 30 days. When visualizing the embeddings using Principal Component Analysis (PCA), they found a "monotonic drift." The embeddings for Day 30 were consistently far from Day 1, with a clear trajectory in between. This means the model's latent space has naturally discovered a representation of "progress" without being explicitly told which recording came first.

### A Topology of Mistakes
By visualizing mistake-heavy recordings using UMAP (a dimensionality reduction technique), the researchers found that different types of mistakes formed distinct clusters. "Timing-disruption" mistakes (like stopping to find a note or repeating a failed passage) were grouped separately from "pitch-deviation" mistakes (hitting a wrong note but keeping the rhythm). This demonstrates that the model doesn't just see a binary "correct vs. incorrect"; it understands the *nature* of the deviation.

## Significance for Music Education

The development of MAJEPPA has significant implications for the future of music technology, particularly in education. Current tools for piano students are often rigid—they might tell you that you hit a $C$ instead of a $C\#$, but they struggle to explain *why* a performance feels unexpressive or how your technique is improving over time.

By providing a unified space that understands the full range of skill levels, MAJEPPA enables:
*   **Sophisticated Diagnostic Tools:** Systems that can categorize mistake types and provide targeted pedagogical advice.
*   **Progress Tracking:** Visualizing a student’s growth over weeks or months through the embedding space.
*   **Adaptive Generation:** Generating "reference" performances that are appropriate for a student’s current level, rather than just providing a perfect, unattainable virtuoso recording.

Ultimately, this work moves symbolic music AI away from the narrow goal of mimicking the masters and towards a broader, more human-centric understanding of how we learn, practice, and express ourselves through music.
