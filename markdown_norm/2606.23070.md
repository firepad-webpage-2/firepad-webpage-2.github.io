## Introduction to Liquidity Provision in AMMs

Decentralized finance (DeFi) relies heavily on Automated Market Makers (AMMs) to enable the trading of digital assets without traditional intermediaries. In these systems, Liquidity Providers (LPs) deposit assets into a pool, allowing traders to swap one token for another. In return, LPs earn a portion of the trading fees. While earlier AMM designs required LPs to provide liquidity across the entire price range from zero to infinity, concentrated liquidity models, most notably Uniswap v3, allow LPs to allocate their capital within specific price intervals.

While concentrated liquidity significantly improves capital efficiency, it also exposes LPs to higher risks. The primary challenge is adverse selection: when the market price of an asset changes on external platforms like Centralized Exchanges (CEXs), the AMM price becomes "stale" until someone trades against it. Arbitrageurs are usually the first to act, buying underpriced assets or selling overpriced ones to the pool. This systematic loss for LPs is known as Loss-Versus-Rebalancing ($LVR$).

Recent research suggests that the static fee structures used by most AMMs are often insufficient to cover these $LVR$ costs. This paper investigates whether dynamic fee schedules—fees that adjust in real-time based on market conditions—can mitigate these losses and make liquidity provision more sustainable.

## The Problem of Adverse Selection and LVR

To understand the core of the problem, we must distinguish between different types of traders. "Noise traders" or "uninformed traders" swap assets based on personal needs or random signals; their trades are generally profitable for LPs because they pay fees without necessarily moving the price against the LP's interests. In contrast, "informed traders" or arbitrageurs exploit price discrepancies between the AMM and an external reference market.

Because blockchains process transactions in discrete intervals (blocks) rather than continuously, the AMM price often lags behind the global market price. If the price of an asset rises on a CEX, the AMM remains a "cheap" place to buy until an arbitrageur exhausts the lower-priced liquidity. The cost incurred by the LP in this scenario is $LVR$. It represents the difference between the value of the LP's position and a hypothetical "rebalancing" strategy that tracks the market price without being forced to trade at stale quotes.

The authors evaluate LP performance using "Hedged PnL," which isolates the profit from providing liquidity by removing the directional risk of the assets themselves:

$$\text{Hedged PnL} = \text{Fees} - LVR$$

If this value is negative, the LP would have been better off simply holding the assets and rebalancing them on a CEX rather than providing liquidity to the AMM.

## An Agent-Based Modeling Approach

To study these dynamics, the paper employs an Agent-Based Model (ABM). Unlike purely analytical models that rely on simplified assumptions, an ABM simulates a complex ecosystem by modeling individual "agents" with specific behaviors and observing how they interact. This is particularly useful for DeFi, where the interaction between block times, gas fees, and different types of liquidity providers creates a highly non-linear environment.

The simulation includes several key components:
1.  **Reference Market (CEX):** A stochastic price process (Heston model) that represents the global market price, including price impact from trades.
2.  **Blockchain Microstructure:** Transactions are grouped into blocks. Agents observe the state of the pool at the end of a block and submit their trades to a "mempool" for the next block.
3.  **Heterogeneous Agents:** 
    *   **Arbitrageurs:** They monitor the gap between the CEX and AMM and trade to close it, prioritizing their transactions to be the first in a block.
    *   **Passive LPs:** They provide liquidity over wide price ranges and rarely change their positions.
    *   **Active LPs:** They provide concentrated liquidity and frequently adjust their ranges to stay near the current price.
    *   **Just-In-Time (JIT) LPs:** Sophisticated agents who add liquidity right before a large trade and remove it immediately after to capture fees without holding the assets long-term.
    *   **Smart Routers:** They decide whether to route a trade to the AMM or the CEX based on which venue offers the best price after fees.

## Proposals for Dynamic Fees

The central question of the research is how to set the taker fee $f_t$ dynamically to protect LPs. The authors propose and test several controllers:

*   **Static Fee:** A constant fee $f_0$, serving as a baseline.
*   **Volatility-Based Fee:** The fee increases when market volatility is high. This is based on the idea that high volatility increases the probability of large price gaps, and thus higher $LVR$. The authors test signals based on both CEX volatility ($vol_{obs,t}$) and DEX volatility.
*   **Toxicity-Based Fee:** This is a more targeted approach. It looks at the "basis" $B_{obs,t}$, which is the deviation between the AMM price and the CEX price. If the deviation exceeds the current fee, the trade is considered "toxic" because it is likely driven by arbitrage. The fee is then adjusted to encompass this gap.

All dynamic rules are bounded by a minimum fee $f_{min}$ and a maximum fee $f_{max}$ to ensure the pool remains usable.

## Results: Can Dynamic Fees Save LPs?

The simulation results provide a clear picture of the trade-offs involved in AMM design.

### Impact on Profitability
Under static fee regimes, the study finds that standing LPs (both active and passive) consistently lose money on a hedged basis. Active LPs, who provide concentrated liquidity, suffer the most because their positions are highly sensitive to price movements.

When dynamic fees are introduced, the toxicity-based controller proves to be the most effective. In several scenarios, it is the only rule that manages to push the hedged PnL of both passive and active LPs into positive territory.

*{fig:hedged-pnl-heatmap}: Average hedged PnL across different models and fee regimes. Red indicates losses, while blue indicates profits. Toxicity-based fees (Model1 — toxicity) significantly improve outcomes for both passive and active LPs compared to static fees.*

Interestingly, the improvement doesn't come from a significant reduction in $LVR$. Instead, it comes from much higher fee revenue collected during periods of market stress. When the price is moving fast, the toxicity controller raises fees, forcing arbitrageurs to pay more to the LPs for the privilege of closing the price gap.

### The Trade-off: Market Share
There is no "free lunch" in market design. While dynamic fees protect LPs, they make the AMM more expensive for traders. The simulation shows that as fees become more responsive and higher on average, the "Smart Router" routes more trades to the CEX. The "DEX share"—the percentage of volume handled by the AMM—drops when dynamic fees are implemented. However, the authors note that the AMM remains relevant and continues to facilitate a significant portion of trades, suggesting that LPs can be made profitable without completely alienating traders.

### The Role of JIT Liquidity and MEV
The inclusion of JIT (Just-In-Time) liquidity providers adds another layer of complexity. JIT LPs are highly efficient at capturing fees because they only enter the pool when a large, profitable trade is guaranteed. The study finds that while dynamic fees increase the total fee pool, a significant portion of this extra revenue can be captured by JIT LPs, potentially at the expense of the standing LPs the fees were meant to protect. This highlights a critical challenge: dynamic fees alone may not be enough if sophisticated MEV (Maximal Extractable Value) searchers can front-run or dilute the benefits.

## Evaluating Fee Coverage

To analyze how well fees cover $LVR$ on a block-by-block basis, the authors use the ratio $R_t$:

$$R_t = \frac{\Delta LVR_t}{\Delta F_t}$$

Where $\Delta LVR_t$ is the marginal loss and $\Delta F_t$ is the marginal fee income in block $t$.
*   If $R_t > 1$, the loss exceeds the income.
*   If $R_t < 1$, the fee income covers the loss.

In static fee models, $R_t$ is frequently above 1, especially for concentrated liquidity. The toxicity-based fee rule successfully brings this ratio below 1 as latency (block size) increases, demonstrating that it effectively scales the "cost of trading" to match the "cost of providing liquidity" in volatile environments.

## Conclusion and Significance

This research demonstrates that the current standard of static fees in AMMs is structurally biased against liquidity providers, particularly in the face of adverse selection. By using a granular Agent-Based Model, the authors show that dynamic fees—specifically those that respond to the "toxicity" of the order flow rather than just general volatility—can significantly improve LP sustainability.

The unique contribution of this work lies in its realistic simulation of blockchain microstructure. It accounts for the fact that price discovery happens in blocks and that different types of LPs (passive, active, and JIT) compete for the same fee pool. The findings suggest that protocol designers should consider adaptive fee mechanisms to protect LPs, but must also be mindful of the trade-offs regarding market competitiveness and the influence of MEV-driven liquidity strategies.

For the broader DeFi ecosystem, this suggests a move toward more "intelligent" pools that can sense and respond to market imbalances, potentially leading to more robust and less subsidized decentralized markets.
