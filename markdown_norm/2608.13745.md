## Foundations of Catastrophe Risk and Physical Mitigation

In the realm of catastrophe insurance, managing risk traditionally involves financial mechanisms like reinsurance, dividend adjustments, or premium setting. However, insurers face a complex dual uncertainty: the unpredictable timing and severity of catastrophe events (jump risks) and the continuous fluctuation of reconstruction costs. The latter is often exacerbated post-disaster by a phenomenon known as "demand surge," where the high demand for labor and materials drives up the price of rebuilding.

This research addresses these challenges by modeling "physical hedging"—a proactive, pre-emptive mitigation strategy. Unlike financial hedging, which compensates for losses after they occur, physical hedging aims to reduce the physical and monetary severity of a catastrophe *before* it impacts an insurer's surplus. The study focuses on how an insurer should dynamically allocate resources to this mitigation in an environment where reconstruction prices are volatile and where individual insurer decisions are influenced by the aggregate state of the entire industry.

By treating reconstruction costs as an exogenous factor that amplifies catastrophe losses, the researchers isolate the specific impact of replacement-cost exposure. This allows for a detailed analysis of how an insurer's capitalization and the prevailing economic conditions should dictate their mitigation efforts.

## The State-Space Model: Surplus and Reconstruction Costs

The paper models the insurer's environment using a two-dimensional state process consisting of the insurer's surplus $X$ and a reconstruction-cost factor $Y$. This system is described as a controlled jump-diffusion process.

The reconstruction-cost factor $Y_s$ represents the logarithm of the reconstruction-cost index. It is modeled as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process, which is a common choice for modeling mean-reverting behavior in financial indices. The evolution of $Y_s$ at time $s$ is given by:

$$dY_s = \kappa(\vartheta - Y_s) ds + \sigma_I dW^I_s$$

In this equation, $\kappa$ is the speed of mean reversion, $\vartheta$ is the long-run average log-cost level, and $\sigma_I$ represents the volatility of the reconstruction market. The term $W^I_s$ is a standard Brownian motion. This process ensures that if reconstruction costs stray far from their historical average, they tend to drift back toward $\vartheta$ over time. The actual reconstruction-cost index used in loss calculations is $e^y$.

The insurer’s surplus $X_s$ evolves according to a jump-diffusion process that incorporates the effects of premiums, non-catastrophe fluctuations, and discrete catastrophe losses. Its dynamics are defined as:

$$dX_s = b(X_{s-}, Y_{s-}, u_s) ds + \sigma_X(X_{s-}, Y_{s-}, u_s) dW^X_s - \int_E \ell(Y_{s-}, z, u_s) \tilde{N}(ds, dz)$$

The drift $b$ accounts for the net income from premiums and investment minus the costs of physical hedging $u_s$. The diffusion term $\sigma_X$ captures smaller, continuous fluctuations in the balance sheet. The final term represents the impact of catastrophes, where $\tilde{N}(ds, dz)$ is a compensated Poisson random measure describing the frequency and severity of events. Here, $z$ represents the "mark" or magnitude of the catastrophe, and the integral accounts for the total loss transmitted to the surplus.

## Physical Hedging as a Stochastic Control

The central innovation in this model's control mechanism is the loss amplitude function $\ell(y, z, u)$. This function determines the exact monetary loss incurred by a catastrophe of mark $z$ when the reconstruction cost factor is $y$ and the hedging level is $u$:

$$\ell(y, z, u) = e^y a(z) h(u)$$

This structure highlights three multiplicative components of a loss:
1.  The replacement cost factor $e^y$, which acts as a multiplier. If reconstruction costs are high, the same physical damage results in a larger monetary loss.
2.  The baseline damage $a(z)$, which represents the raw physical destruction caused by the event.
3.  The residual-loss fraction $h(u)$, which is the primary control variable.

The function $h(u)$ represents the effectiveness of physical hedging. It is assumed that $h(0)=1$ (no mitigation) and that $h(u)$ decreases as $u$ increases, meaning higher investment in mitigation reduces the portion of the loss that hits the surplus. Because this control affects the amplitude of the jump, it directly influences every moment of the catastrophe-loss distribution. This is a significant departure from standard models where controls often only affect the drift or diffusion of a process.

## The Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman Framework for Nonlocal Jumps

To find the optimal hedging strategy, the problem is formulated as a stochastic optimal control problem. The goal is to minimize a discounted social cost criterion over a finite time horizon $[0, T]$. This criterion includes the costs of hedging, penalties for low capitalization, and the financial burden of catastrophe losses. The value function $V(t, x, y)$ is defined as the minimum expected cost starting from state $(x, y)$ at time $t$:

$$V(t, x, y) = \inf_{u \in \mathcal{U}} \mathbb{E} \left[ \int_t^T e^{-\rho(s-t)} f(X_s, Y_s, u_s) ds + e^{-\rho(T-t)} g(X_T, Y_T) \right]$$

This minimization problem leads to a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation. Because the surplus process includes jumps, the resulting HJB equation is "nonlocal." This means that the equation at a specific point $(x, y)$ depends on the values of the value function at other points in the state space—specifically, the points the surplus might "jump" to after a catastrophe. The nonlocal term takes the form of an integral:

$$\int_E [V(t, x - \ell(y, z, u), y) - V(t, x, y)] \nu(dz)$$

Because the value function $V$ might not be smooth enough to solve this equation in the classical sense, the researchers employ the theory of viscosity solutions. They prove that $V$ is the unique viscosity solution within a specific growth class $G_q$, ensuring that the problem is well-posed and that the solution is mathematically sound even in the presence of heavy-tailed catastrophe risks.

## Mean Field Games: Modeling Population-Level Interaction

The paper extends the single-insurer problem to a population of many insurers using the Mean Field Game (MFG) framework. In this setting, an individual insurer's costs are influenced by the aggregate distribution of capitalization and reconstruction-cost exposure across the entire industry. This distribution is denoted as $m_t$.

The strategic interaction is captured by adding "vulnerability costs" to the individual objective function: $F(s, (X_s, Y_s), m_s)$ for running costs and $G((X_T, Y_T), m_T)$ for terminal costs. These functions represent the idea that an insurer faces higher costs (e.g., regulatory pressure, reputational risk, or difficulty in sourcing contractors) when the industry as a whole is weakly capitalized or highly exposed to reconstruction price surges.

An equilibrium in this Mean Field Game is a state where:
1.  Given the population distribution $m_t$, each insurer follows an optimal hedging policy derived from the backward nonlocal HJB equation.
2.  The aggregate behavior of all insurers, each following their optimal policy, actually produces the population distribution $m_t$ via a forward Kolmogorov (Fokker-Planck) equation.

The researchers establish the existence of this equilibrium using fixed-point arguments and prove uniqueness under specific monotonicity conditions. This framework allows for the analysis of how industry-wide trends influence individual hedging incentives.

## Numerical Methodology and Algorithmic Design

Solving the coupled HJB and Kolmogorov equations requires sophisticated numerical techniques. The model is first calibrated using empirical data. For catastrophe losses, the authors use a tempered Pareto distribution. This distribution allows for the heavy-tailed behavior characteristic of catastrophes (where extreme events are rare but possible) while ensuring that the distribution has finite moments $p^\star$ required for mathematical stability.

The numerical solution involves several steps:
1.  **Discretization**: The state space is discretized using finite-difference schemes. For the nonlocal jump operator, a positive quadrature rule is used to ensure the stability of the solution.
2.  **HJB Solver**: A backward-in-time monotone finite-difference scheme is applied. The minimization over the control $u$ is solved at each grid point using policy iteration (Howard's algorithm).
3.  **Kolmogorov Solver**: The population distribution $m$ is advanced forward in time using a scheme that preserves the total probability mass and ensures the distribution remains non-negative.
4.  **Fixed-Point Iteration**: The equilibrium is found by iteratively solving the HJB equation (backward) and the Kolmogorov equation (forward) until the population flow $m$ converges to a stable state.

Diagnostics, such as checking residuals and grid refinement, are used to ensure the numerical results are reliable and not artifacts of the discretization.

## Analysis of Optimal Strategies and Systemic Outcomes

The numerical experiments provide several key insights into the behavior of optimal physical hedging.

The optimal strategy $u^\star(0, x, y)$ is found to be highly state-dependent. One prominent finding is the response to reconstruction costs. When the reconstruction cost factor $y$ increases, the optimal hedge $u$ also increases. For example, at a baseline surplus $x=1.00$, the optimal hedge might rise from $0.00$ at $y=-0.20$ to $0.20$ at $y=0.40$. This confirms that physical hedging becomes more economically valuable when replacement costs are high, as the mitigation offsets the amplification effect of the demand surge.

Additionally, capitalization levels significantly influence hedging behavior. Insurers with lower surplus (weak capitalization) are found to hedge more aggressively than those with high surplus. At a fixed cost level $y=0.20$, an insurer with $x=0.40$ might hedge at a level of $0.40$, while an insurer with $x=1.60$ might not hedge at all ($u=0.00$). This suggests that physical hedging serves as a crucial protective measure for insurers with less financial cushion.

The value function results quantify the economic burden of these uncertainties. For a baseline insurer at $x=1.00$, the social cost rises from $0.00685$ at $y=-0.20$ to $0.01979$ at $y=0.40$. The Mean Field Game analysis further shows that strategic interactions increase the perceived social cost. At the same state ($x=1.00, y=0.20$), the value function increases from $0.01006$ in the independent case to $0.01579$ in the mean field equilibrium.

These results illustrate that the combined effect of high reconstruction costs and industry-wide vulnerability significantly increases the incentives for physical mitigation. The model provides a rigorous framework for insurers and regulators to understand how physical hedging can be used as a dynamic tool to stabilize the insurance market against catastrophe-driven shocks.
