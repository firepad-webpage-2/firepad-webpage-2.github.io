## Understanding Partial Identification in Infinite Dimensions

In many economic research scenarios, the variables of interest are not exactly "point identified." This means that even with an infinite amount of data, the data generating process might only narrow down a parameter to a range of possible values rather than a single number. This situation is known as partial identification. While econometricians have developed robust tools for managing partial identification for single numbers (finite-dimensional parameters), many modern problems involve functions or distributions—objects that are infinite-dimensional.

Examples of these infinite-dimensional parameters include the conditional average treatment effect (CATE) function, which describes how a policy's impact varies across different continuous covariates like age or income, or the entire distribution of treatment effects in a population. When these functions are partially identified, researchers face the challenge of constructing confidence regions that hold "uniformly" across the entire function.

A uniform confidence band is a set of two curves—an upper and a lower bound—that are designed to contain the entire true function with a pre-specified probability (such as 95%). This is significantly more demanding than "pointwise" confidence intervals, which only guarantee coverage at any single specific point. The paper by Shunsuke Imai and Yuta Okamoto provides a new methodology for constructing these uniform confidence bands for infinite-dimensional parameters when they are only partially identified.

## The Foundation: The Imbens-Manski-Stoye Framework

The researchers build upon a specific approach to partial identification known as the Imbens-Manski-Stoye (IMS) framework. In the finite-dimensional case, if a parameter is known to lie between a lower bound $\theta_l$ and an upper bound $\theta_u$, the width of this interval $\Delta = \theta_u - \theta_l$ significantly impacts how we should think about uncertainty.

If the interval is very wide relative to the sampling error, the uncertainty about the true parameter is dominated by the lack of identification, and a one-sided critical value is sufficient for coverage. If the interval is very narrow (approaching point identification), the uncertainty is dominated by sampling error, and a two-sided critical value is required. The IMS framework provides an "adaptive" critical value that smoothly transitions between these cases, ensuring the confidence interval is as sharp (narrow) as possible while maintaining valid coverage.

Imai and Okamoto extend this logic to the functional setting. They define the identified set $\Theta_n(F)$ as the collection of all functions $\theta$ that satisfy:

$$\Theta_n(F) := \{\theta: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R} : \theta_{l,n}(x; F) \leq \theta(x; F) \leq \theta_{u,n}(x; F) \text{ for all } x \in \mathcal{X}\}$$

Here, $\theta_{l,n}$ and $\theta_{u,n}$ represent the lower and upper bounds of the identified interval at each point $x \in \mathcal{X}$, given a data distribution $F$. The challenge is that these bounds themselves must be estimated from data, introducing sampling uncertainty across the entire domain of $x$.

## Constructing the Uniform Confidence Band

The authors propose a uniform confidence band $\widehat{\text{CI}}_{\text{fIM}}(x)$ defined by the estimated bounds and a specific critical value $\widehat{c}_{\text{fIM},n}$. The band is written as:

$$\widehat{\text{CI}}_{\text{fIM}}(x) := \left[\widehat{\theta}_{l,n}(x) - \widehat{c}_{\text{fIM},n} \widehat{\sigma}_{l,n}(x), \widehat{\theta}_{u,n}(x) + \widehat{c}_{\text{fIM},n} \widehat{\sigma}_{u,n}(x)\right]$$

In this expression, $\widehat{\theta}_{l,n}(x)$ and $\widehat{\theta}_{u,n}(x)$ are the estimated boundary functions, and $\widehat{\sigma}_{l,n}(x)$ and $\widehat{\sigma}_{u,n}(x)$ are their respective standard error estimates. The heart of the contribution lies in how the critical value $\widehat{c}_{\text{fIM},n}$ is determined to ensure uniform coverage.

The theoretical critical value $c_{\text{fIM},n}$ is defined to satisfy a specific probability condition. It accounts for the fact that the true parameter $\theta(x)$ can lie anywhere within the identified interval. To remain valid for any possible true function, the researchers take an infimum over all possible "position" functions $v(x) \in [0,1]$ that describe where the true parameter sits relative to the bounds:

$$\inf_{v \in \{0,1\}^{\mathcal{X}}} P_F \left[\sup_{(t,x) \in \{u,l\} \times \mathcal{X}} (G(\tilde{\psi}_{t,x}) - B_n(t, x; v)) \leq c_{\text{fIM},n}\right] = 1 - \alpha$$

In this equation:
- $G(\tilde{\psi}_{t,x})$ represents a Gaussian process that approximates the random fluctuations of our estimators around the true boundary functions.
- $B_n(t, x; v)$ is a "bias" term that incorporates the width of the identified interval. This term is what allows the critical value to adapt. If the interval is wide at point $x$, the bias term becomes large, reducing the impact of one of the boundaries on the critical value.
- $1 - \alpha$ is the desired coverage level (e.g., 0.95).

By taking the infimum over $v$, the method ensures that the coverage holds even in the "worst-case" scenario for the location of the true parameter within the identified set.

## Estimation via Multiplier Bootstrap

Because the distribution of the supremum of these random processes is generally unknown and depends on the underlying data distribution $F$, the authors provide a practical way to estimate $\widehat{c}_{\text{fIM},n}$ using a multiplier bootstrap procedure.

The multiplier bootstrap is a computationally efficient way to simulate the distribution of an empirical process. Instead of re-sampling the data with replacement (as in a standard bootstrap), it assigns random weights $\{w_{i}^{*,b}\}_{i=1}^n$ to each observation. The procedure involves several steps:

1.  **Generate Weights:** For many iterations, generate sets of independent weights (usually from a standard normal distribution).
2.  **Calculate Bootstrapped Processes:** For each iteration, compute a version of the empirical process $G_n^{*,b}(\tilde{\psi}_{\text{s.a.},t,x})$ using the weights and the influence functions (scores) of the boundary estimators.
3.  **Incorporate Bias:** For a given position function $v$, compute the bootstrapped statistic $\widehat{T}_n^{*,b}(v)$ which includes the estimated bias $\widehat{B}_n(t, x; v)$. This bias term is crucial as it depends on the estimated width of the identified interval $\widehat{\Delta}_n(x)$.
4.  **Find the Quantile:** For each $v$, find the $1 - \alpha$ quantile of the simulated statistics, denoted as $\widehat{q}_n^*(v)$.
5.  **Final Critical Value:** The final critical value $\widehat{c}_{\text{fIM},n}$ is the maximum (supremum) of these quantiles across all possible position functions $v$.

This process effectively builds a critical value that is large enough to cover the true function regardless of where it lies within the identified range, while remaining as small as possible to keep the band informative.

## Theoretical Rigor and Asymptotic Validity

The significance of the paper is supported by a rigorous proof of "uniform asymptotic validity." This means that as the sample size $n$ grows, the probability that the confidence band contains the true function is guaranteed to be at least $1 - \alpha$, regardless of the underlying distribution $F$ (within a broad class) or the specific true parameter $\theta$. Formally, the authors prove:

$$\liminf_{n \to \infty} \inf_{F \in \mathcal{F}} \inf_{\theta \in \Theta_n(F)} P_F (\theta(x) \in \widehat{\text{CI}}_{\text{fIM}}(x) \text{ for all } x \in \mathcal{X}) = 1 - \alpha$$

This result relies on sophisticated tools from empirical process theory, particularly the Gaussian approximation for the supremum of non-centered processes. These tools allow the researchers to handle "high-dimensional" scenarios where the complexity of the function space (often measured by entropy or covering numbers) is large.

The authors make several technical assumptions to reach this result. They require that the boundary estimators are "asymptotically linear," meaning they can be approximated by a sum of independent influence functions. They also require the function classes to be "VC-type," a standard condition in machine learning and statistics that limits the complexity of the functions to prevent overfitting and ensure convergence.

## Significance in Econometric Practice

The introduction of uniform confidence bands for infinite-dimensional partially identified parameters is a significant step for applied economic research. Many standard econometric models naturally lead to partial identification. For example:

*   **Interval Data:** If an outcome like "income" is only reported in ranges (e.g., $\$50k - \$75k$), the conditional mean function is only partially identified.
*   **Missing Data:** In studies with non-random attrition, the distribution of outcomes for the group that dropped out is unknown, leading to identified sets for the overall population distribution.
*   **Treatment Effects:** In experiments where researchers cannot assume "rank invariance" (that the person most helped by treatment A would also be most helped by treatment B), the full distribution of treatment effects is typically not point identified.

In these cases, researchers previously had to choose between pointwise intervals—which are easier to compute but do not provide a "global" guarantee—and more conservative methods that might produce bands so wide they are uninformative. The IMS-type uniform band provides a middle ground: it is theoretically valid across the entire domain of $x$ while being "sharp" by adapting to the local width of the identification region.

By providing both the theoretical justification and a concrete bootstrap-based algorithm, Imai and Okamoto offer a tool that allows for more reliable and nuanced policy analysis. Researchers can now visualize the entire range of plausible functions and have a statistically sound measure of uncertainty that accounts for both sampling error and the inherent limits of identification.
