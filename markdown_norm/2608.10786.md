## The Role of Atmospheric Profiling in Ground-Based Astronomy

Ground-based astronomical observatories face a persistent challenge: the Earth's atmosphere. Turbulent cells of air with varying temperatures and densities act like a series of moving lenses, distorting the light from distant stars. This effect, known as "seeing," limits the angular resolution of even the largest telescopes to a fraction of their theoretical potential. To counteract this, astronomers use Adaptive Optics (AO), hardware systems that measure these distortions in real-time and use deformable mirrors to correct the wavefront.

The efficiency of an AO system depends heavily on the vertical structure of the atmosphere. Understanding where turbulent layers are located, how fast they move, and in what direction they travel is essential for optimizing system performance. This is particularly true for Ground Layer Adaptive Optics (GLAO), which targets the turbulence closest to the Earth's surface to provide moderate corrections over a wide field of view. By identifying the specific velocities and altitudes of these layers, researchers can better design control algorithms that predict atmospheric behavior.

The research presented here focuses on a wavefront profiling method applied to the 'imaka GLAO demonstrator, located at the Maunakea observatory in Hawai’i. By analyzing the data (telemetry) from the instrument's wavefront sensors, the authors aim to build a detailed picture of the atmosphere above one of the world's premier astronomical sites. This work moves beyond simple site testing by evaluating an automated method to distinguish between the turbulent ground layer and the free atmosphere.

## The 'imaka GLAO Instrument and Open-Loop Telemetry

The 'imaka instrument is a GLAO demonstrator installed on the University of Hawaii 2.2-meter telescope. Unlike traditional AO systems that often focus on a single, narrow field of view, GLAO is designed for wide-field imaging. To achieve this, 'imaka uses up to five natural guide stars distributed over a field of approximately 24 by 18 arcminutes.

Each guide star is monitored by a Shack-Hartmann wavefront sensor (WFS). These sensors work by dividing the incoming light from a star into an $8 \times 8$ grid of smaller "subapertures." Each subaperture produces a small image of the star, and the displacement of this image from its ideal center (the "slope") reveals the local tilt of the wavefront.

For this study, the researchers used "open-loop" telemetry. In open-loop mode, the AO system measures the turbulence but does not attempt to correct it with the deformable mirror. This preserves the raw signal of the atmosphere, allowing for a pure analysis of the turbulent layers. The data analyzed consisted of thousands of frames recorded at a rate of approximately 180 Hz, providing high temporal resolution of the passing air masses.

## Methodology: The Correlation Technique

The core principle behind this profiling method is the "frozen flow hypothesis." This hypothesis assumes that turbulent eddies remain relatively stable in their structure as they are blown across the telescope's line of sight by the wind. If this holds true, a pattern of wavefront distortions measured at one moment should reappear a short time later, shifted in position.

The researchers use two types of statistical correlations to extract this information from the WFS slopes.

### Auto-covariance
Auto-covariance correlates the data from a single wavefront sensor with itself at different spatial and temporal offsets. This helps identify all turbulent layers that pass through the line of sight of that specific star. The auto-covariance function $A(\delta i, \delta j, \delta t)$ is defined as:

$$
A(\delta i, \delta j, \delta t) = \frac{\langle \sum_{i,j} s_{ij}(t)s_{i+\delta i, j+\delta j}(t + \delta t) \rangle}{O(\delta i, \delta j)}
$$

where $s_{ij}(t)$ is the slope at a specific subaperture $(i,j)$ at time $t$, $\delta i$ and $\delta j$ are the spatial shifts, $\delta t$ is the time delay, and $O(\delta i, \delta j)$ is a normalization factor based on the overlap of the sensors.

### Cross-covariance
Cross-covariance involves correlating the data between two different wavefront sensors looking at different stars. Because the lines of sight for different stars diverge as they gain altitude, the spatial offset at which the correlation peaks occur can be used to calculate the altitude of the turbulent layer. This is expressed mathematically as:

$$
C(\delta i, \delta j, \delta t) = \frac{\langle \sum_{i,j} s_{ij}(t)s'_{i+\delta i, j+\delta j}(t + \delta t) \rangle}{O(\delta i, \delta j)}
$$

Here, $s$ and $s'$ represent slopes from two different wavefront sensors. By tracking how these correlation peaks move over time, the researchers can calculate the velocity (speed and direction) of the layers.

## Refining the Signal: Pre-processing and Background Subtraction

Raw telemetry data is often "noisy," containing signals from telescope vibrations, tracking errors, and static aberrations within the instrument itself. To isolate the atmospheric signal, the authors implemented a rigorous pre-processing pipeline:

1.  **Static Slope Subtraction:** An average map of all slopes over a 30-150 second interval was calculated and subtracted. This removes persistent hardware-related patterns and slow-moving "dome seeing" (turbulence inside the telescope structure).
2.  **Global Tip-Tilt Removal:** The average tilt across all subapertures at each frame was subtracted. This filters out errors caused by the telescope's tracking mechanism.
3.  **Temporal Background Subtraction:** This is a critical step for enhancing moving peaks. By subtracting a moving average of the correlation map (using a window of $n$ time steps), the researchers can suppress the bright central peak caused by non-moving turbulence.

The choice of the window length $n$ significantly impacts the results. A short window (e.g., 50 frames) is highly effective at revealing fast-moving layers but may "erase" slow-moving ones. Conversely, a longer window (e.g., 200 or 400 frames) preserves slow layers but can be noisier.

## Validation Through Simulated Layer Injections

To ensure the reliability of their automated detection algorithm, the researchers performed calibration tests using simulated data. They generated synthetic turbulent "phase screens" with known characteristics—defined by the Fried parameter $r_0$ (which measures turbulence strength) and the outer scale $L_0$ (the size of the largest eddies).

They injected these simulated layers into real on-sky telemetry data and attempted to "recover" them using their algorithm. Key findings from these simulations included:
*   **Sensitivity to Strength:** The method is most successful for stronger layers. For layers with $r_{0,inj} = 0.10$ to $r_{0,inj} = 0.25$, the recovery rate was high. Weaker layers ($r_0 > 0.40$) were often lost in the noise.
*   **Speed Coverage:** The algorithm successfully detected layers moving at speeds between $1 \text{ m/s}$ and $36 \text{ m/s}$.
*   **Optimal Thresholds:** A detection threshold of 3 standard deviations above the background noise was found to be the best balance between finding real layers and avoiding false positives.

## On-Sky Performance and Meteorological Comparison

Applying the method to hundreds of telemetry files from the 'imaka demonstrator revealed distinct atmospheric structures. The automated system identified a ground layer (GL) in roughly 70-80% of all observations. In contrast, free atmosphere (FA) layers—those at higher altitudes—were detected in less than 10% of the data. This discrepancy is partly due to the fact that high-altitude layers are typically weaker and move faster, making them harder to isolate from background noise.

The researchers compared their findings with independent meteorological data:
*   **Ground Layer vs. CFHT:** The wind speeds measured by the AO telemetry generally matched the trends seen at the nearby Canada-France-Hawaii Telescope weather station. However, there was a noticeable discrepancy in wind direction (roughly 45 degrees). This suggests that the turbulence the AO system sees may be highly localized to the telescope dome or influenced by the specific topography of the summit.
*   **Upper Layers vs. GFS:** The high-altitude detections were compared to the Global Forecast System (GFS), a weather model. The study used the $250\text{ mb}$ and $300\text{ mb}$ pressure layers (approximately 9-10 km above sea level) as proxies for the upper atmosphere. While the sample size for FA layers was small, the detected speeds showed a bias toward the slower end of the GFS predictions.

## Significance and Future Directions

This work demonstrates that spatial-temporal correlation of WFS telemetry is a viable tool for automated atmospheric profiling. By utilizing the telemetry already produced by AO systems during science observations, astronomers can gain a "free" and continuous stream of data regarding the turbulent environment.

The unique contribution of this research lies in its systematic evaluation of the method's sensitivity limits. The finding that background subtraction window lengths must be tuned to specific speed regimes provides a roadmap for more robust future implementations. Furthermore, the discrepancy between the AO-derived wind directions and the weather station data emphasizes that "seeing" is not always a direct reflection of the ambient wind, but rather a complex interaction of the wind with the observatory's local environment.

The next steps for this technique involve improving the detection of free-atmosphere layers. By implementing multi-scale background subtraction—simultaneously looking for fast and slow layers—researchers could create a more complete vertical profile. Such detailed profiles will be instrumental for the next generation of Extremely Large Telescopes (ELTs), where complex Multi-Conjugate Adaptive Optics (MCAO) systems will require precise knowledge of the atmospheric layers to achieve diffraction-limited imaging across wide fields.
