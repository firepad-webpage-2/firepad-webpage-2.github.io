In many operational domains, decisions are not isolated events but sequences of interventions that accumulate costs over time. Whether a hospital manages a clinical treatment protocol, an airline schedules maintenance for engine components, or a marketing firm implements a customer retention program, these actions consume resources from a fixed budget. However, because the trajectory of a patient, machine, or customer is inherently stochastic, the total cost of any given strategy is a random variable rather than a deterministic value.

A common approach in decision-making is to constrain the expected, or mean, cumulative cost. While mathematically convenient, this risk-neutral perspective fails to account for the "tail" of the cost distribution—the probability of extreme budget overruns. Two strategies might have the same average cost, but one could have a much higher likelihood of exceeding the allocated budget. Furthermore, choosing the best strategy often requires evaluating counterfactuals: what would have happened if we had followed a strategy different from the one observed in historical data?

This paper addresses these challenges by proposing a "predict-then-optimize" framework for selecting sequential intervention strategies. The core objective is to maximize a desired outcome while ensuring that the probability of the cumulative cost exceeding a budget $B$ remains below a user-defined threshold $\epsilon$.

## The Problem with Mean-Based Constraints

Current approaches in prescriptive analytics often focus on single-point decisions or sequential decisions that limit the expected cumulative cost. In many real-world scenarios, however, a budget violation is not just a statistical variation but an operational failure. For example, in a medical setting, a strategy that averages out to a certain cost might still frequently lead to "financial toxicity" for patients or resource exhaustion for a clinic.

The authors identify two primary deficiencies in existing methods:
1. **The Optimizer's Curse:** When we select a strategy based on estimated mean costs, the optimization process tends to favor strategies where the cost was underestimated due to sampling error. This leads to frequent budget overruns in practice.
2. **Ignoring Tail Risk:** Mean-based constraints do not distinguish between a strategy with a stable cost and one with highly volatile costs. 

To address this, the paper formulates the decision as a chance-constrained selection problem. Instead of requiring $E[C(g)] \leq B$, where $E[C(g)]$ is the expected cost of strategy $g$, the proposed rule requires:
$$
Pr(C(g) > B) \leq \epsilon
$$
where $\epsilon$ is the tolerated probability of budget violation. This formulation is equivalent to requiring the $(1-\epsilon)$-quantile of the cost distribution, also known as the Value-at-Risk ($VaR_{1-\epsilon}(C(g))$), to be within the budget $B$.

## Identifying Counterfactual Costs and Outcomes

A significant hurdle in this research is that the data used for decision-making is typically observational. We only see the costs and outcomes for the specific sequences of actions taken in the past. To evaluate a new strategy $g$, we must estimate what its outcome and cost *would have been*.

The authors operate within the sequential potential-outcome framework. To identify these counterfactuals from data, they rely on three standard causal inference assumptions:
*   **Sequential Ignorability:** Treatment assignments at each step are independent of future potential outcomes, given the history of observed covariates and previous treatments.
*   **Positivity:** There is a non-zero probability of receiving any treatment dictated by the candidate strategies for any history that might occur.
*   **Consistency:** The observed outcome for an individual matches the potential outcome if their treatment history matches the strategy being evaluated.

Under these assumptions, the authors use the g-computation formula to estimate the distribution of trajectories under a given strategy. From these trajectories, they derive two critical quantities for every candidate strategy $g$:
1.  **Outcome Value $V(g)$:** The probability of a successful terminal state (e.g., patient survival or machine availability).
2.  **Cumulative Cost $C(g)$:** The sum of treatment costs over the entire decision horizon.

Crucially, the authors prove that while the expected cumulative cost $E[C(g)]$ can be decomposed into a sum of expected costs at each stage, the tail probability $Pr(C(g) > B)$ cannot. This "non-decomposability" means that risk-sensitive optimization cannot be easily broken down into stage-wise sub-problems. Instead, the decision-maker must evaluate the full distribution of the cumulative cost for each strategy as a whole.

## The Chance-Constrained Selection Rule

The paper compares several decision rules to demonstrate the advantages of the chance-constrained approach. 

The **Naive Rule** ($g^{*}_{naive}$) simply maximizes the estimated outcome $\hat{V}(g)$ subject to the estimated mean cost $\hat{C}(g) \leq B$. As shown in the experiments, this rule often leads to significant budget violations because it ignores both the variability of costs and the estimation error.

The **Chance-Constrained Rule** ($g^{*}_{cc}$) maximizes $\hat{V}(g)$ subject to the constraint that the estimated probability of exceeding the budget is no more than $\epsilon$. This can be expressed as:
$$
1 - \hat{F}_g(B) \leq \epsilon
$$
where $\hat{F}_g$ is the empirical cumulative distribution function of the cost for strategy $g$. 

By varying $\epsilon$ from 0 to 1, the framework allows decision-makers to trace a **safety-utility frontier**. This frontier explicitly shows the trade-off: how much additional "utility" (outcome) can be gained by accepting a higher "risk" (probability of budget violation). This allows stakeholders to choose an operating point based on their specific risk tolerance rather than a fixed, arbitrary margin.

## Theoretical Guarantees for Finite Samples

Because cost distributions are estimated from finite samples, there is always a risk that the empirical estimate $\hat{F}_g$ deviates from the true distribution $F_g$. The authors provide distribution-free guarantees to bound this uncertainty.

One key result is the **Feasibility Bound**. They show that with high probability ($1-\eta$), any strategy admitted by the empirical chance-constrained rule will have a true budget violation probability bounded by $\epsilon + \delta$, where $\delta$ is a "slack" term. This term $\delta$ is $O(n^{-1/2})$, meaning it shrinks as the sample size $n$ increases, and it grows only logarithmically with the number of candidate strategies $|\mathcal{G}|$. This indicates the method is robust and scales well to larger sets of potential strategies.

Furthermore, they provide a **Regret Bound**, which ensures that the outcome of the chosen strategy $V(g^{*}_{cc})$ is close to the outcome of the best possible strategy that would be truly safe at a slightly more stringent violation level. These bounds provide a rigorous foundation for using the empirical selection rule in practice, giving decision-makers a "certified ceiling" on their risk exposure.

## Experimental Results Across Diverse Domains

The authors evaluated their framework across five different environments, ranging from clinical simulators to predictive maintenance data:
1.  **Sepsis Simulator:** A discrete model of patient survival under different medical interventions.
2.  **Tumor-Growth Model:** A heterogeneous population model where treatment reduces tumor size but accumulates costs.
3.  **MIMIC-IV (Semi-Synthetic):** Utilizing real ICU patient covariates with a synthetic outcome model to test the framework on complex, real-world data structures.
4.  **C-MAPSS:** A predictive maintenance dataset for turbofan engines, where the goal is to maximize availability under a crew-hour budget.
5.  **Drink Less:** A real-world micro-randomized trial for a digital health intervention, testing engagement outcomes.

The experiments consistently showed that the Naive Rule frequently violated the expected budget, sometimes in up to 18.7% of cases, and often selected strategies where nearly half the cost distribution exceeded the budget. In contrast, the Chance-Constrained Rule successfully maintained zero violation of the expected budget across almost all scenarios and kept the realized tail probabilities within the specified tolerance $\epsilon$. 

The "cost" of this safety was a lower average outcome compared to the Naive Rule, reflecting the intrinsic trade-off between maximizing performance and minimizing risk. The authors also found that the framework was robust to moderate levels of estimation error, such as mean-zero noise or systematic cost underestimation, which typically cause mean-based rules to fail.

## Significance and Practical Impact

This research provides a systematic way to bridge the gap between causal inference and risk-sensitive optimization. By focusing on the tail of the cost distribution rather than just the mean, it offers a more reliable tool for decision-makers in high-stakes environments where budget overruns have serious consequences.

The significance of this work lies in its:
*   **Operational Interpretability:** The parameter $\epsilon$ has a clear meaning (e.g., "We can tolerate a budget overrun in 5% of cases"), making it easier for non-experts to set and adjust.
*   **Theoretical Rigor:** The finite-sample guarantees ensure that the empirical results translate to reliable performance in the real world.
*   **Modular Design:** The predict-then-optimize structure allows users to plug in different causal estimators and update their constraints without needing to re-run the entire prediction pipeline.

While the current work focuses on choosing from a finite set of candidate strategies, it lays the groundwork for future research into more adaptive, rolling-horizon decisions and the development of predictors specifically optimized for tail-risk estimation. For now, it provides a principled framework for selecting safe and effective intervention strategies from the complex, confounded data common in modern operations.
