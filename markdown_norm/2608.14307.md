## Nonlinearity in Financial Stability and Policy

Since the 2008 global financial crisis, economists have shifted their focus from analyzing average economic trends to understanding "downside risk"—the probability of extreme economic contractions. It has become clear that financial stress does not interact with the real economy in a simple, linear way. Instead, when the financial system is under duress, shocks are amplified, leading to severe recessions that a standard economic model might fail to predict.

To manage these risks, macroprudential authorities use tools like the Countercyclical Capital Buffer (CCyB). The logic is straightforward: during periods of economic expansion and low financial stress, banks are required to set aside extra capital. When a crisis hits, regulators release this buffer, allowing banks to absorb losses and continue lending to the real economy, thereby softening the downturn.

While the theory of CCyB is well-established, measuring its effectiveness empirically is difficult. Most existing frameworks, such as Growth-at-Risk (GaR), rely on quantile regressions that require decades of data to accurately estimate the "tail" of the distribution. For many economies, especially emerging markets or small open economies like Hungary, this data simply does not exist. This paper addresses this gap by introducing a Threshold Bayesian Vector Autoregression (TBVAR) approach, which provides a more robust way to analyze regime-dependent policy effects in data-constrained environments.

## Overcoming Data and Endogeneity Constraints

The most popular tool for measuring downside risk today is the Growth-at-Risk (GaR) framework. GaR uses financial conditions to predict the lower 5th or 10th percentile of future GDP growth. However, this approach has two primary limitations. First, it is often univariate, meaning it looks at how finance affects growth without fully accounting for how growth, in turn, affects finance (endogeneity). Second, estimating the extreme tails of a distribution requires a very large sample size; otherwise, the estimates become highly unstable.

The authors propose the TBVAR as a multivariate alternative. Instead of trying to estimate a specific quantile (like the 5th percentile) directly, the TBVAR divides the world into two "regimes": a "normal" regime and a "stress" regime. In each regime, the economy follows a different set of mathematical rules. This approach is motivated by economic theory, specifically models with "occasionally binding constraints." In these models, a financial accelerator only kicks in when a certain threshold of stress is reached—for instance, when banks run out of excess capital or when collateral values drop too low.

By identifying these regimes, the TBVAR can approximate the insights of the GaR framework while being much more efficient with data. It treats financial stress not just as a predictor, but as an endogenous variable that interacts with GDP, credit, and regulatory policy simultaneously.

## The Threshold Bayesian VAR Framework

The core of the methodology is a two-regime TBVAR model. The model tracks a vector of variables $Y_t$, which includes monthly GDP growth, the Real Effective Exchange Rate (REER), credit growth, a financial stress index, free bank capital, and regulatory capital. The behavior of these variables changes depending on the state of the financial system.

The transition between states is governed by a threshold variable—in this case, the Factor-based Index of Systemic Stress (FISS). The FISS, denoted as $z_t$, is a high-frequency measure of stress in the Hungarian financial system. The model assumes that if the stress level at some previous time $t-d$ (where $d$ is a delay parameter) is below a certain threshold $z^*$, the economy is in the normal regime ($N$). If it exceeds $z^*$, the economy enters the stress regime ($S$).

The dynamics are represented by the following display equations:

$$
Y_t = c_N + \sum_{j=1}^M B_{N,j} Y_{t-j} + \Sigma_N^{1/2} \epsilon_t \quad \text{if } z_{t-d} \leq z^*
$$

$$
Y_t = c_S + \sum_{j=1}^M B_{S,j} Y_{t-j} + \Sigma_S^{1/2} \epsilon_t \quad \text{if } z_{t-d} > z^*
$$

In these equations, $c_i$ represents the regime-specific intercept, $B_{i,j}$ are the coefficients describing the relationships between variables, $M$ is the number of lags (set to 6 months), and $\Sigma_i$ is the error covariance matrix. The parameters, including the threshold $z^*$ and the delay $d$, are not chosen manually; they are estimated using a Bayesian Gibbs sampler. This method allows the researchers to find the threshold that most cleanly separates the two sets of economic behaviors observed in the historical data.

## Identifying Stress and Normal Regimes

For the model to be useful for policy, the identified "stress" periods must align with actual historical events where a macroprudential authority might have considered releasing a capital buffer. Using data for Hungary, the model identifies a clear threshold $z^* \approx 0.39$.

The model successfully identifies two major stress episodes in Hungary's recent history:
1.  **The Global Financial Crisis (2008–2010):** A period of severe financial dislocation where GDP contracted sharply and financial stress spiked.
2.  **The Post-2022 Period:** A period characterized by high inflation, energy price shocks, and significant monetary tightening.

Interestingly, the model does not classify the initial 2020 COVID-19 shock as a sustained "financial stress" regime. While GDP dropped significantly due to lockdowns, the underlying financial system remained relatively stable compared to 2008, illustrating the model's ability to distinguish between a "real" economic shock and a "financial" systemic shock.

## Asymmetric Outcomes of Capital Regulation

The central contribution of this paper is the analysis of how regulatory capital shocks—mimicking the activation or release of the CCyB—affect the economy differently in each regime. To do this, the authors use Generalized Impulse Response Functions (GIRFs). Unlike standard linear impulse responses, GIRFs account for the fact that the system might switch regimes during the forecast period. The GIRF at horizon $k$ for a shock $\mu_t$ given history $\Omega_{t-1}$ is defined as:

$$
GIRF(k, \mu_t, \Omega_{t-1}) = E[Y_{t+k} | \mu_t, \Omega_{t-1}] - E[Y_{t+k} | \Omega_{t-1}]
$$

The results provide striking evidence for the countercyclical design of capital buffers:

### The Stress Regime: Benefits of Release
In the stress regime, a reduction in regulatory capital (a buffer release) leads to a significant and persistent boost to GDP growth. The response peaks at roughly 1.4 standard deviations and remains positive for a year. This suggests that during crises, easing capital requirements is an effective tool for supporting economic activity.

### The Normal Regime: Costs of Accumulation
Crucially, the study finds that increasing regulatory capital (buffer accumulation) during normal times has a negligible impact on GDP. The median response is close to zero, and the statistical uncertainty suggests no meaningful economic drag. This is a vital finding for policymakers, as it suggests that the "insurance" provided by the CCyB can be purchased at a very low cost during good times.

### The Credit Channel
The response of new credit is more complex. While a capital release in a stress regime helps GDP, the volume of new credit does not always show a simple, immediate increase. This implies that the policy may work through channels other than just the quantity of new loans, such as improving lending terms (lower interest rates or collateral requirements) or boosting general market confidence.

## Significance and Policy Significance

This research offers three main contributions to the field of macroprudential policy. First, it provides robust aggregate empirical evidence that countercyclical capital buffers work as intended. The asymmetry found—high benefits during stress and low costs during booms—validates the core logic used by central banks around the world.

Second, it offers a methodological "bridge." While quantile-based Growth-at-Risk models are powerful, they are often out of reach for researchers in smaller economies with shorter data histories. The TBVAR framework demonstrated here provides a tractable alternative that captures the same nonlinear tail risks without requiring the same volume of data.

Finally, by using a specific systemic stress index (FISS) as the regime trigger, the paper creates a direct link between financial monitoring and policy action. Regulators can use such indices not just to observe stress, but to estimate when the economy has crossed into a regime where a buffer release would be most effective. This moves macroprudential policy toward a more data-driven and predictable framework, helping to stabilize the real economy against the inevitable cycles of the financial system.
