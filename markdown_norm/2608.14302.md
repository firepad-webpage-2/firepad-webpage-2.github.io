In the field of material science, the quest for multifunctional materials—substances that can perform multiple roles like harvesting solar energy while simultaneously managing heat—is a primary driver for sustainable technology. A common hurdle in this search is the complexity of synthesizing entirely new chemical compounds. This study explores an alternative strategy: phase engineering. By applying external pressure, existing materials can be coaxed into different structural arrangements (phases), revealing hidden properties without the need for chemical alteration.

The research focuses on the $KMgX$ family (where $X$ represents the pnictogen elements phosphorus ($P$), arsenic ($As$), antimony ($Sb$), and bismuth ($Bi$)). These compounds are already known for their versatility, typically crystallizing in a tetragonal structure at room temperature. However, this study identifies a pressure-induced transition to an orthorhombic phase that significantly enhances their potential for both photovoltaic and thermoelectric applications.

## The Mechanics of Pressure-Induced Phase Transitions

To understand how pressure influences material structure, the researchers employed the third-order Birch–Murnaghan Equation of State (EOS). This mathematical model relates the energy of a material to its volume, allowing for the determination of the most stable structural phase at any given pressure $P$. The EOS is expressed as:

$$E(V) = E_0 + \frac{9V_0 B_0}{16} \left \{ \left [ \left ( \frac{V_0}{V} \right )^{2/3} - 1 \right ]^3 B_0' + \left [ \left ( \frac{V_0}{V} \right )^{2/3} - 1 \right ]^2 \left [ 6 - 4 \left ( \frac{V_0}{V} \right )^{2/3} \right ] \right \}$$

Where $E_0$ and $V_0$ are the energy and volume at zero pressure, $B_0$ is the bulk modulus, and $B_0'$ is its derivative with respect to pressure. By comparing the energy-volume curves of the tetragonal phase (space group $P4/nmm$) and the orthorhombic phase (space group $Pnma$), the study determined the critical transition pressure $P_{\text{trans}}$ at which the orthorhombic structure becomes energetically favorable.

The calculated transition pressures are $12.01\text{ GPa}$ for $KMgP$, $10.32\text{ GPa}$ for $KMgAs$, $6.02\text{ GPa}$ for $KMgSb$, and $6.27\text{ GPa}$ for $KMgBi$. These values indicate that the structural shift occurs under relatively moderate pressures achievable in laboratory settings. Importantly, once the transition occurs, the researchers found that these phases are not just theoretical constructs but are stable enough to potentially persist or be utilized under specific conditions.

## Validating Structural Stability

Before investigating the functional properties, it was necessary to confirm that the orthorhombic phase is physically viable. The study utilized three distinct types of stability analysis:

1.  **Thermodynamic Stability:** The formation enthalpy per atom, $\Delta H_f^{\text{atom}}$, was calculated to ensure the compounds would not spontaneously decompose into their constituent elements ($K$, $Mg$, and $X$). Convex hull diagrams (as seen in {fig:phase-diagrams-and-convex-hulls}) were constructed to visualize the energy landscape of the $K-Mg-X$ system, confirming that the orthorhombic phase lies on or very near the "hull of stability" at high pressures.
2.  **Dynamic Stability:** This involves calculating the phonon dispersion—the way atoms vibrate within the lattice. If a material is unstable, its atoms will have "imaginary" vibration frequencies. The calculations for the $KMgX$ family showed no such imaginary frequencies across the entire Brillouin zone, confirming that the orthorhombic structure is a local energy minimum.
3.  **Mechanical Stability:** The researchers applied the Born stability criteria, which involve checking the elastic constants of the crystal. All members of the $KMgX$ family in the orthorhombic phase met these requirements, showing that the materials are robust against small mechanical deformations.

## Electronic and Optical Characteristics

The core of the multifunctionality lies in the electronic structure. The researchers used high-level Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations, specifically employing the HSE06 hybrid functional and accounting for spin–orbit coupling (SOC). SOC is a relativistic effect that becomes significant in heavier atoms like $Sb$ and $Bi$, often causing shifts in the energy levels of electrons.

The study found that $KMgP$, $KMgAs$, and $KMgSb$ are semiconductors with direct band gaps ($E_g$). A direct band gap is highly desirable for optical applications because it allows electrons to transition between states by absorbing a photon without needing a change in momentum, leading to much higher light absorption efficiency. The calculated band gaps are $2.41\text{ eV}$ for $KMgP$, $1.60\text{ eV}$ for $KMgAs$, and $1.58\text{ eV}$ for $KMgSb$.

The optical potential was further quantified using the Spectroscopic Limited Maximum Efficiency (SLME). Unlike the standard Shockley-Queisser limit, SLME accounts for the actual absorption spectrum $\alpha(\omega)$ and the thickness of the material. The results were impressive:
-   The absorption coefficients reached $10^5\text{ cm}^{-1}$ in the visible range.
-   For a thin film of just $0.6\ \mu\text{m}$ thickness, the maximum efficiency was calculated at $27.12\%$ for $KMgAs$ and $26.40\%$ for $KMgSb$.
-   These values surpass several established photovoltaic materials, suggesting that these pressure-induced phases could be highly effective as solar cell absorbers.

Conversely, $KMgBi$ was found to be semi-metallic when SOC was included. While this makes it unsuitable for standard photovoltaics, it hints at potential applications in topological electronics, though the study focused primarily on the semiconducting members for energy conversion.

## Thermoelectric Potential and Waste Heat Recovery

Beyond light harvesting, the $KMgX$ family shows significant promise in thermoelectrics—the direct conversion of temperature differences into electricity. The efficiency of a thermoelectric material is measured by the dimensionless figure of merit, $zT$:

$$zT = \frac{S^2 \sigma T}{\kappa_e + \kappa_L}$$

In this equation, $S$ is the Seebeck coefficient (voltage generated per degree of temperature difference), $\sigma$ is the electrical conductivity, $T$ is the absolute temperature, and $\kappa_e$ and $\kappa_L$ are the electronic and lattice thermal conductivities, respectively. For a high $zT$, a material needs high electrical conductivity and Seebeck coefficient (the numerator, called the "power factor") but low thermal conductivity (the denominator) to prevent heat from leaking back across the device.

The study used the AMSET code to solve the Boltzmann Transport Equation, considering various scattering mechanisms like Polar Optical Phonon (POP) and Acoustic Deformation Potential (ADP) scattering. They found that POP scattering is the dominant factor limiting electron movement in these materials.

Among the family, $KMgSb$ emerged as the top performer. At a temperature of 900 K, it achieved:
-   A lattice thermal conductivity ($\kappa_L$) as low as $0.871\text{ W m}^{-1}\text{ K}^{-1}$.
-   A maximum $zT$ of $0.65$ for p-type (hole) doping and $0.58$ for n-type (electron) doping.

A particularly advantageous finding is that $KMgSb$ performs well for both n-type and p-type doping. Most thermoelectric devices require a pair of n-type and p-type materials to function; finding a single material system where both versions are efficient simplifies manufacturing and ensures compatible thermal expansion properties.

## Significance of the Findings

The primary contribution of this work is the rigorous demonstration that $KMgX$ compounds can be transformed into a stable, orthorhombic "multifunctional" state through pressure. By bridging the gap between structural phase transitions and energy applications, the study provides several key insights:

First, it highlights that the orthorhombic phase is not just a high-pressure curiosity but a viable candidate for thin-film solar cells. The combination of direct band gaps and high absorption coefficients allows these materials to be thinner and potentially more efficient than many current market alternatives.

Second, the discovery of high $zT$ values in the same phase suggests the possibility of "tandem" energy systems. One could envision a device where the $KMgAs$ or $KMgSb$ layer absorbs sunlight to generate electricity, and the heat generated by the sun (or waste heat from other processes) is simultaneously converted into more power by the thermoelectric property of the same material.

Finally, this research serves as a roadmap for experimentalists. By providing specific transition pressures and predicting the resulting physical properties, it reduces the "trial and error" involved in synthesizing high-performance materials. The use of advanced computational tools like HSE06 and the Bethe–Salpeter equation (BSE) ensures that these predictions are grounded in the fundamental quantum mechanics of the system, offering a reliable target for future experimental verification and device integration.
