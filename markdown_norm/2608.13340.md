## Bridging AMMs and Options Pricing

In traditional finance, implied volatility is a forward-looking measure derived from the market price of an option. It represents the market's expectation of how much an underlying asset's price will fluctuate until the option expires. In decentralized finance (DeFi), specifically within automated market makers (AMMs) like Uniswap v3, there are no directly quoted option premiums. Instead, liquidity providers (LPs) commit capital to specific price ranges, earning fees as traders swap assets through those ranges.

Recent research has highlighted that providing concentrated liquidity in an AMM is economically similar to selling a short-dated option. When an LP provides liquidity in a very narrow price range, they exhibit "negative convexity." If the price stays within the range, they collect fees (similar to an option premium); if the price moves outside the range, they are left with the less valuable asset (similar to being exercised on a short option). However, because there is no explicit "option price," calculating a corresponding implied volatility has remained a challenge.

This paper addresses this gap by deriving a DEX-native proxy for implied volatility, denoted as $\sigma_{fee}$. This metric is calculated solely from on-chain data—specifically volume, active liquidity, and fee tiers—without requiring external price oracles. By establishing a formal link between the time decay of a Black-Scholes option and the fee-earning mechanics of a Uniswap v3 pool, the research provides a way to quantify market activity through the lens of volatility theory.

## The Logic of Theta Concentration

The derivation begins with the Black-Scholes model, specifically the concept of "theta" ($\theta$), which measures the rate at which an option's value declines as it approaches expiration. For a standard European option, as the time to maturity $t$ approaches zero, the theta becomes extremely concentrated around the strike price $K$. 

Mathematically, as $t \to 0$, the theta function behaves like a scaled Dirac delta distribution:
$$\theta(S, t) \to \frac{K^2 \sigma^2}{2} \delta(S-K)$$
where $S$ is the spot price and $\sigma$ is the volatility. The total "mass" of this decay—the sum of all theta across all possible prices—converges to a specific value:
$$\int_0^\infty \theta(S, t) dS = \frac{K^2 \sigma^2}{2}$$

This mathematical property suggests that for an extremely short-dated option, all the value decay (the "premium") is generated only when the price is exactly at the strike. In the context of an AMM, a "narrow range" LP position acts like a collection of these short-dated options. The "premium" the LP receives is the stream of trading fees generated while the spot price is within their chosen liquidity tick.

## Deriving the $\sigma_{fee}$ Proxy

To translate this theoretical theta into a practical DEX metric, the research maps the continuous Black-Scholes model onto the discrete "tick" architecture of Uniswap v3. In Uniswap v3, LPs provide liquidity between two price points, $P_a$ and $P_b$. The width of this range is defined by the pool's tick spacing $t_S$. 

A narrow range can be characterized by its geometric mean price $K = \sqrt{P_a P_b}$ and a range factor $r = \sqrt{P_b / P_a}$. For the smallest possible range allowed by the protocol (one tick), the price difference is approximately:
$$P_b - P_a \approx K \frac{t_S}{10^4}$$

The researcher assumes that the total theta mass identified earlier is spread uniformly over this narrow range. This creates a "theoretical premium intensity." To find the volatility proxy, this intensity is equated to the "observable fee intensity"—the actual fees earned by LPs per unit of liquidity. 

The observable fee intensity is defined by the pool's $\text{feeRate}$, the traded $\text{Volume}$, and the active liquidity $L_{tick}$ at the current price. By setting the theoretical intensity equal to the observed intensity:
$$K \frac{\sigma_{fee}^2}{2} \frac{10^4}{t_S} = K \text{feeRate} \frac{\text{Volume}}{L_{tick}}$$

Using the empirical relationship in Uniswap v3 where $t_S \approx 2 \cdot 10^4 \text{feeRate}$, the equation simplifies into a remarkably clean formula for the DEX-native fee implied volatility proxy:
$$\sigma_{fee} = 2 \text{feeRate} \sqrt{\frac{\text{Volume}}{L_{tick}}}$$

This formula allows anyone to calculate a volatility-like number using only three pieces of data easily retrieved from a blockchain: the pool's fee tier (e.g., 0.3%), the total volume over a period, and the amount of liquidity currently active at the spot price.

## The Challenge of Structural Identification

A critical contribution of this paper is the distinction between a "proxy" and "true" latent volatility. While $\sigma_{fee}$ behaves like volatility, the author argues that it cannot be used to perfectly identify the structural volatility $\sigma^*$ of the underlying asset from pool data alone. 

This is due to the fundamental economics of AMM liquidity provision. The total economic cost of a short option position ($\Pi_{short}$) consists of two parts:
$$\Pi_{short} = \text{Fees} + \text{HedgingCost}$$

The "Fees" are what the LPs collect from traders. The "HedgingCost" represents the losses LPs incur due to price movements, often referred to as "Loss Versus Rebalancing" (LVR). In a perfectly efficient market, the LVR for a constant product market maker is:
$$\text{LVR} = \frac{\sigma^2}{8}$$

The problem is that fees and hedging costs scale differently. Fees scale linearly with trading volume, while hedging costs scale quadratically with price variance. The research introduces the "fee capture ratio" $\alpha$:
$$\alpha = \frac{\text{Fees}}{\Pi_{short}}$$

The relationship between the proxy and the true volatility is $\sigma_{fee}^2 = \alpha \sigma^{*2}$. Because $\alpha$ depends on external factors—such as the percentage of "informed" versus "uninformed" traders and the speed at which arbitrageurs correct the price—it is not observable from the pool itself. Without knowing $\alpha$, one cannot work backward from the fees to find the exact structural volatility. Thus, $\sigma_{fee}$ is best understood as a "fee implied activity index."

## Empirical Performance and Interpretation

To validate the proxy, the research analyzed the ETH/USDC 30bps pool from early 2023 to mid-2026. The study compared the calculated $\sigma_{fee}$ against the realized volatility (RV) of the Ether spot price. 

The findings show a high correlation of 0.87, indicating that the proxy is a reliable indicator of market turbulence. On average, the proxy trades at a slight premium to realized volatility, which is consistent with the "volatility risk premium" observed in traditional options markets. Specifically, $\sigma_{fee}$ was higher than realized volatility on approximately 68% of the days analyzed.

The research also tracked the ratio $\beta_{RV}(t) = \left(\frac{\text{IV}(t)}{\text{RV}(t)}\right)^2$. While one might expect this ratio to stay below 1.0 (if fees only capture a fraction of the total cost), it frequently spiked much higher. These spikes occur because trading volume (and thus fees) can explode during volatile periods far faster than the 30-day trailing realized volatility can adjust. This instability reinforces the author's argument that the proxy measures "trading intensity" rather than just price diffusion.

## Applications and Significance

The derivation of $\sigma_{fee}$ has immediate practical applications for the DeFi ecosystem, particularly for protocols like Panoptic that offer oracle-free perpetual options.

1.  **Oracle-Free Risk Management**: Protocols can use $\sigma_{fee}$ to adjust margin requirements or collateral buffers based on real-time on-chain activity. If the "activity index" spikes, the protocol can programmatically increase the cost of maintaining positions to protect against increased risk.
2.  **Premium Scaling**: For protocols that stream premiums to LPs, $\sigma_{fee}$ provides a mathematical basis for scaling those payments. It ensures that LPs are compensated proportionally to the volume-to-liquidity ratio of the pool.
3.  **Market Monitoring**: LPs can use the proxy to compare the "attractiveness" of different pools. A pool with a high $\sigma_{fee}$ relative to its historical realized volatility may be generating more fee income per unit of risk than a pool with a lower proxy value.
4.  **Protocol Design**: The work provides a blueprint for Uniswap v4 "hooks" that could calculate and act upon volatility metrics in real-time, enabling more sophisticated automated trading and hedging strategies.

In summary, this research provides a rigorous link between the continuous-time mathematics of options and the discrete-tick mechanics of modern AMMs. By acknowledging the limits of what can be "known" from pool data alone, it offers a robust, oracle-free tool for quantifying the intense relationship between trading volume and liquidity.
