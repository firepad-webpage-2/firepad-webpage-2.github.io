## Introduction to Label-Free Subsurface Nerve Detection

In modern surgery, one of the most persistent risks is iatrogenic nerve injury—accidental damage to nerves during medical procedures. These injuries can lead to permanent functional loss, chronic pain, and a significantly diminished quality of life for patients. The difficulty in preventing these accidents stems from the fact that small peripheral nerves are often visually indistinguishable from surrounding muscle or connective tissue. Furthermore, nerves are frequently buried beneath intact superficial layers of tissue, making them invisible to the surgeon's naked eye or standard surgical cameras until they are accidentally cut or exposed.

![handheld-multimodal-oct-probe](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13807v1/octsystem.png)
*{fig:handheld-multimodal-oct-probe}: The multimodal handheld imaging system integrates swept-source Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) with co-registered white-light and autofluorescence imaging to provide subsurface guidance.*

To address this, researchers from the University of Arizona and the University of Pennsylvania have developed a new framework combining a handheld multimodal Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) probe with a specialized deep learning architecture named NerveDetNet. Unlike previous methods that rely on toxic fluorescent dyes or are limited to viewing only exposed surfaces, this system can detect and resolve the depth of peripheral nerves concealed up to $1.4\text{ mm}$ beneath intact tissue. By relying on the intrinsic structural patterns of nerves, the system provides real-time, label-free guidance, potentially allowing surgeons to "see through" tissue layers and avoid critical nerves before they are ever exposed to risk.

## The Limitations of Current Visualization Techniques

Existing intraoperative nerve identification techniques each face significant hurdles. Electrophysiological monitoring, while a standard of care, provides functional confirmation (e.g., muscle twitch) rather than spatial imaging; it tells the surgeon *that* a nerve was stimulated, but not exactly *where* it is located or how deep it lies. Optical methods like fluorescence imaging offer high contrast but typically require the injection of exogenous contrast agents. These agents face strict regulatory hurdles and introduce potential toxicity concerns, which complicates their routine use in the operating room.

Label-free optical methods, such as intrinsic autofluorescence or diffuse reflectance, avoid these dyes but are fundamentally restricted to the tissue surface. They cannot visualize nerves buried under even thin layers of muscle or fascia. While Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) has been used to image nerves before, most research has focused on exposed nerves or utilized polarization-sensitive OCT (PS-OCT). PS-OCT relies on the birefringence (double refraction) of the nerve sheath, but this contrast mechanism degrades quickly as light scatters deeper into the tissue. The framework presented here shifts the focus to intensity-based structural signatures, which remain detectable even in deeper, scattering environments where polarization information is lost.

## Hardware Design: The Multimodal Handheld Probe

The hardware developed for this study is a custom handheld probe designed to fit into the surgical workflow. The system is built around a swept-source OCT (SS-OCT) engine centered at $1300\text{ nm}$. This wavelength was chosen specifically for its ability to penetrate scattering biological tissues. The light source is a MEMS-VCSEL laser with a $200\text{ kHz}$ sweep rate and $30\text{ mW}$ output, providing an axial resolution of $7.5\text{ }\mu\text{m}$ and a lateral resolution of $21\text{ }\mu\text{m}$.

The probe integrates three distinct imaging modalities:
1.  **OCT:** Provides depth-resolved structural information of the tissue.
2.  **White-Light Imaging:** A 1080p camera provides the standard surgical view, which is co-registered with the OCT data using a dichroic mirror.
3.  **Autofluorescence:** UV LEDs provide excitation for intrinsic fluorescence, serving as a secondary cross-reference for tissue types.

The probe's extension measures $18 \times 18 \times 100\text{ mm}$, allowing it to reach confined surgical sites, while a $10\text{ mm}$ working distance ensures the probe does not need to touch the patient. A critical technical challenge in handheld scanning is the distortion caused by MEMS mirrors. The researchers solved this using a non-rigid thin-plate spline (TPS) model to calibrate and correct the images, ensuring that the OCT data and the white-light view align perfectly at the pixel level.

## Identifying Nerves through the "Bands of Fontana"

The primary mechanism for nerve detection in this work is a unique biological signature known as the "Bands of Fontana." When viewed in en-face (top-down) OCT projections, peripheral nerves exhibit a quasi-periodic, wavy banded texture. This pattern arises from the undulating, spiral-like arrangement of nerve fiber bundles within the nerve sheath.

![structural-signatures-of-nerves](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13807v1/nervefeature.jpg)
*{fig:structural-signatures-of-nerves}: The "Bands of Fontana" appear as periodic intensity variations in OCT projections. (a) Frame-to-frame evolution of the banded pattern. (b-c) Quantitative spatial frequency analysis shows a consistent peak distinguishing nerve from muscle.*

Quantitative analysis by the researchers showed that these bands have a consistent spatial frequency—peaking at approximately 31 pixels in their system—that remains stable across different depths. This stability is crucial: it means that even as the overall signal weakens as the probe looks deeper, the *texture* of the nerve remains identifiable. This provides a robust, label-free contrast mechanism that distinguishes nerves from surrounding muscle, which tends to have a more linear or stochastic scattering pattern.

## NerveDetNet: A Lightweight 2.5D Deep Learning Architecture

To process the OCT data in real-time, the researchers developed NerveDetNet. In medical imaging, full 3D convolutional neural networks are often prohibitively expensive in terms of memory and processing time. Conversely, simple 2D networks ignore the valuable spatial context between adjacent slices. NerveDetNet adopts a "2.5D" approach, processing ordered stacks of $N=5$ B-scans (cross-sectional images) simultaneously.

A major innovation of NerveDetNet is the **Nerve Feature Correlation Module (NFCM)**. This module is designed to handle "sparse-frame" data, where the system only processes every few slices to save time. The NFCM extracts three types of cues:
*   **Spatial cues:** Local structural details.
*   **Order cues:** The relative position of frames in the stack.
*   **Shift-correlation cues:** These detect how the "Bands of Fontana" shift slightly from one frame to the next, helping the network distinguish persistent nerve structures from random noise or speckle patterns.

![detection-workflow](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13807v1/workflow.jpg)
*{fig:detection-workflow}: The processing pipeline includes distortion correction (a, f), nerve segmentation via NerveDetNet (b), tissue surface detection (c), and depth estimation (e).*

By fusing these cues in a lightweight decoder, NerveDetNet achieves high accuracy with only 13.79 million parameters—roughly half the size of standard medical segmentation models like U-Net++.

## Experimental Results and Performance

The researchers benchmarked NerveDetNet against six other state-of-the-art segmentation models using a five-fold cross-validation on ex vivo tissue samples. The system was tested at varying frame spacings, defined by the variable $SF = 0, 5, 11, 23$, representing how many slices were skipped during sparse sampling.

![benchmark-comparison](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13807v1/fivefold_benchmark.jpg)
*{fig:benchmark-comparison}: Comparative performance of NerveDetNet against other architectures across different frame spacings. NerveDetNet consistently maintains higher Dice scores as sampling becomes sparser.*

Key findings from the performance evaluation include:
*   **Accuracy:** NerveDetNet achieved a mean Dice score (a measure of overlap between predicted and true nerve location) of $0.795 \pm 0.045$ for dense sampling and $0.725 \pm 0.054$ for the sparsest sampling ($SF=23$).
*   **Speed:** The total processing time for a volume at high sparsity was $0.392 \pm 0.013\text{ s}$, which is fast enough for near-real-time surgical updates.
*   **Reconstruction:** After reconstructing the full 3D volume from sparse slices, NerveDetNet reached an Intersection over Union (IoU) of $0.664 \pm 0.054$ and a projected clDice (measuring the continuity of the nerve trajectory) of $0.791 \pm 0.051$.

## Depth-Resolved Subsurface Localization

The ultimate goal of the system is to calculate exactly how deep a nerve is buried. The depth $d$ is determined by finding the distance between the detected tissue surface and the nerve mask:

$$d = \frac{\Delta z}{n}$$

where $\Delta z$ is the optical path difference and $n \approx 1.4$ is the estimated refractive index of biological tissue.

![subsurface-detection-examples](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13807v1/result.jpg)
*{fig:subsurface-detection-examples}: The final system output overlays depth-resolved nerve maps onto the white-light surgical view. Nerves are detected at depths where they are invisible in the standard camera view (bottom right).*

The framework successfully detected and resolved the depth of nerves at ranges between $0.8\text{ mm}$ and $1.4\text{ mm}$ below the surface. At these depths, the nerves were completely invisible in the standard white-light camera view. The system correctly captured the way nerves "dip" and "rise" as the thickness of the overlying tissue changed, providing a dynamic 3D map of the subsurface anatomy.

## Conclusion and Significance

This research represents a significant step forward in surgical guidance technology. By combining high-speed, depth-resolved OCT imaging with a customized 2.5D deep learning network, the researchers have demonstrated a practical way to visualize hidden nerves without the need for dyes or invasive procedures. The ability to detect nerves through intact tissue—reaching nearly the full $1.8\text{ mm}$ imaging depth of the OCT system—addresses a critical safety gap in the operating room.

Future developments may include moving to MHz-class swept sources to further increase acquisition speed, as well as refining the algorithms to identify other structures like blood vessels. By providing surgeons with a real-time, depth-resolved "augmented reality" view of subsurface nerves, this technology has the potential to drastically reduce the incidence of accidental nerve damage and improve long-term outcomes for patients undergoing complex surgeries.
