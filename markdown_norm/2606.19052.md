## Understanding Credit Risk Stress-Testing in Modern Banking

Financial institutions operate in an environment where the ability to withstand economic shocks is a regulatory mandate and a strategic necessity. Central to this is credit risk stress-testing, a process that evaluates the resilience of a bank's loan portfolio under "severe but plausible" adverse conditions. Regulatory frameworks like the International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) 9 and the Basel accords require banks to maintain sufficient capital reserves. These reserves are calculated based on risk parameters such as the Probability of Default ($PD$), Loss Given Default ($LGD$), and Exposure at Default ($EAD$).

Current stress-testing methodologies, while functional, often rely on simplifying assumptions that may not fully capture the complexity of real-world banking operations. Many models assume a static balance sheet—meaning they do not account for new loans being issued during a crisis—and often treat risk parameters as independent variables. The Extendable, Integrated, and Dynamic Forecasting and Stress-Testing (EIDFAST) approach introduced by researchers at the University of Pretoria addresses these gaps. It provides a simulation-based framework that integrates risk parameters through cash flow modeling, incorporates dynamic loan origination, and operates at the granular, individual loan level.

## The Limitations of Current Stress-Testing Frameworks

To appreciate the design of the EIDFAST approach, one must understand the three primary limitations identified in existing credit risk models:

1.  **Static Portfolio Assumption:** Standard stress tests often analyze a "frozen" snapshot of a bank's portfolio. In reality, banks continue to lend, albeit under different criteria, during economic shifts. A static model fails to account for how new, potentially riskier or more conservatively priced loans change the portfolio's risk profile over time.
2.  **Implicit Integration of Risk Parameters:** $PD$, $LGD$, and $EAD$ are naturally correlated. For example, during a recession, the probability of a borrower defaulting ($PD$) increases, and the value of collateral often decreases, leading to a higher $LGD$. Many models either ignore these correlations or attempt to link them indirectly through macroeconomic variables. This can lead to an underestimation of total risk.
3.  **Portfolio-Level Aggregation:** Modeling at the portfolio level is computationally simpler but masks idiosyncratic risks. Loan-level modeling, which tracks the specific attributes and payment behaviors of individual borrowers, provides a more realistic and nuanced view of how losses materialize.

## The Pillars of the EIDFAST Approach

The EIDFAST approach is built on three core concepts designed to resolve the issues mentioned above.

### Integration through Receipt Forecasting
Instead of modeling $PD$ and $LGD$ separately, the EIDFAST approach focuses on "receipt forecasting"—the prediction of actual cash flows (receipts) from borrowers. By modeling the expected payments, the framework implicitly captures the combined effect of default probability and loss severity. If a borrower makes no payment, it reflects a default; if they make a partial payment or settle after a delay, it reflects recovery. This receipt-centric view naturally preserves the correlation between different risk dimensions.

### Extendability via Dynamic Loan Production
The approach is "extendable" because it includes a component for new loan production. It simulates the monthly origination of new loans, assigning each new advance a specific principal amount, term, and interest rate. During a stress scenario, the model can adjust these distributions—for instance, by reducing the volume of new loans or increasing interest rates to reflect tighter credit conditions.

### Dynamic Loan-Level Granularity
By using a transition-based simulation (specifically a Markov chain), the model tracks the "state" of each loan over time. This allows the framework to account for time-dependent phenomena, such as how long a borrower stays in delinquency before a loan is finally written off.

## The Loan Production Component

The first stage of the EIDFAST simulation involves populating the portfolio with new loans over the forecast horizon. For each month, the model determines the number of new monthly advances by drawing from a truncated Normal distribution. Once the volume is determined, the characteristics of each individual loan are simulated:

*   **Principal Amount ($B_0$):** Simulated using a right-skewed Beta distribution to reflect that most loans are for moderate amounts, with fewer very large loans.
*   **Original Term ($T$):** Drawn from a truncated Normal distribution (e.g., centered around 240 months for mortgages).
*   **Nominal Monthly Interest Rate ($r$):** Also drawn from a Beta distribution, allowing the model to reflect risk-based pricing.

With these three variables, the monthly instalment ($I_t$) for each loan is calculated. As the simulation progresses under stress, the parameters of these distributions are shifted to mimic a bank's response to an adverse economy, such as decreasing the average loan size or increasing interest rates.

## The Receipt Forecasting Component

The heart of the credit risk assessment lies in predicting whether a borrower will pay their instalment in any given month. The model assigns each loan to one of four states ($Y_t$): **Payment (P)**, **Delinquency (D)**, **Settlement (S)**, or **Write-off (W)**.

The movement between these states is governed by a transition matrix ($M$), containing probabilities ($p_{kl}$) of moving from state $k$ to state $l$.
*   **Performing Loans:** A loan in state P that stays in state P results in a receipt equal to the instalment ($R_t = I_t$).
*   **Delinquency:** A loan in state D may still yield a payment with a certain probability ($b_t$). If a loan stays in D for too long (surpassing a threshold $d$), it moves toward write-off.
*   **Sojourn Time and Write-off:** The model introduces a "sojourn time" ($\tau$), which is the duration an account remains in default before being written off. The final receipt at the time of write-off ($t_w$) is calculated as:

$$R_{t_w} = B_{t_w-1} \cdot (1+r) \cdot (1-w)$$

where $B_{t_w-1}$ is the outstanding balance and $w$ is the simulated write-off rate (the proportion of the balance that is lost).

## Implementing Stress Scenarios

Stress testing is achieved by systematically varying the parameters of the underlying distributions over the forecast horizon. The researchers use a linear shift function to transition parameters from a baseline state to a stressed state:

$$\kappa(t_i) = \kappa(t_n) + (t_i - t_n) \cdot \Delta$$

Here, $\kappa(t_i)$ represents the parameter value at time $t_i$, $\kappa(t_n)$ is the initial value, and $\Delta$ is the marginal change per period. 

In a stress scenario, this function might be used to gradually increase the probability of transitioning from "Payment" to "Delinquency," or to decrease the probability $b_t$ that a delinquent borrower makes a payment. The flexibility of this simulation-based approach allows risk managers to test a wide variety of "what-if" scenarios by simply adjusting the distribution parameters.

## Results: Baseline vs. Stress

The study demonstrates the framework by comparing a stable baseline to an adverse stress scenario over a 36-month horizon. The simulation results show that the EIDFAST approach produces intuitive and significant shifts in risk metrics.

Under stress, the average 12-month default rate rose from roughly 3.5% to over 11%. This was driven by the increased transition probabilities into delinquency and write-off states. Similarly, the loss rate (the proportion of the balance at default that is ultimately unrecoverable) doubled over the same period.

![loss-rate-comparison](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.19052v1/MC_LossRate_Comb.png)
*{fig:loss-rate-comparison}: The portfolio loss rate increases steadily under stress, reflecting higher write-off amounts and longer durations in default.*

The "extendable" nature of the model was also evident: the volume of new loans decreased, and the average principal amount fell, reflecting a contraction in credit supply. Simultaneously, interest rates for new loans increased, showing the model's ability to capture the cost-of-risk adjustments banks make during downturns.

## Significance for the Financial Industry

The EIDFAST approach represents a move toward more realistic and integrated credit risk modeling. By focusing on receipt generation, it avoids the pitfalls of assuming $PD$ and $LGD$ are independent, leading to more accurate capital reserve estimates. The inclusion of dynamic loan production ensures that stress tests reflect the evolving nature of a bank's balance sheet rather than a static historical snapshot.

For regulators and bank managers, this framework offers a flexible tool. It can be calibrated to specific types of credit—such as mortgages or retail credit cards—and can handle complex scenarios where macroeconomic variables might have non-linear impacts on borrower behavior. As the financial sector faces increasing pressure to provide transparent and robust risk assessments, integrated approaches like EIDFAST provide a sophisticated path forward for ensuring institutional stability.
