## Understanding Sharpness in Image Restoration

Image deblurring has undergone a significant transition from traditional mathematical modeling to Deep Neural Network (DNN) architectures. While these modern models excel at handling complex blur kernels, a fundamental discrepancy persists between algorithmic optimization and the Human Visual System (HVS). Most deblurring models optimize for pixel-wise accuracy or structural similarity relative to a "ground truth" reference. However, sharpness—a primary determinant of perceived image quality—is rarely targeted explicitly in the loss functions of these models.

A critical challenge in this field is that standard Image Quality Assessment (IQA) metrics, such as Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) and Structural Similarity Index (SSIM), often fail to distinguish between desirable sharpness and undesirable artifacts. For instance, over-sharpening frequently introduces "ringing" artifacts—spurious oscillations near high-contrast edges. Traditional metrics may inadvertently reward these artifacts because they appear as high-frequency "details," even though they are perceptually jarring to a human observer.

This work addresses these gaps through a comprehensive subjective study and the introduction of a new class of Sharpness Informed (SI) metrics. By quantifying how humans perceive sharpness and over-sharpening, the researchers propose objective measures that explicitly penalize ringing, providing a more accurate reflection of perceived quality than current standard metrics.

## Human Preference for Sharpness

To understand the relationship between mathematical sharpness and human preference, the researchers conducted four distinct subjective protocols. These experiments aimed to determine if the "ground truth" is actually the perceptual ideal and whether incorporating sharpness metrics into model training improves aesthetic outcomes.

The first protocol, the Method of Adjustment, allowed participants to manually tune the sharpness of an image using a slider. The results revealed a consistent trend: observers generally prefer images that are sharper than the original ground truth.

![preference-for-increased-sharpness](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13989v1/protocol1_results/Q_scatter_original_vs_sharpened.png)
*{fig:preference-for-increased-sharpness}: Scatter plot comparing the sharpness (Q) of original images versus the sharpness level preferred by subjects. Data points appearing above the red diagonal indicate a preference for higher sharpness than the reference.*

This finding suggests that deblurring models should potentially aim for a "super-sharp" restoration rather than just a perfect reconstruction of the reference. The study also noted that this preference is content-dependent; images with many flat areas tolerate more sharpening, while images already rich in high-frequency textures require more conservative adjustments.

In subsequent experiments, including a Two-Alternative Forced Choice (2AFC) protocol, participants compared images deblurred with a standard loss function against those deblurred with a sharpness-aware loss function. In 78% of the cases, subjects identified the images produced with the sharpness-aware loss as being perceptually sharper. Furthermore, Pairwise Comparison Evaluations showed that while absolute Mean Opinion Scores (MOS) were close, the sharpness-aware restorations were preferred in 67% of direct aesthetic comparisons.

## Sharpness-Aware Loss Functions in Deep Learning

Based on the observation that humans prefer sharper restorations, the researchers integrated a no-reference sharpness metric, $Q$, into the training of DNN deblurring architectures like ARKNet and XY-Deblur. The proposed composite loss function $L_c$ is defined as:

$$
L_c = L(I, \tilde{I}) - \beta \cdot Q(\tilde{I})
$$

In this equation, $I$ represents the ground truth image, $\tilde{I}$ is the restored image, $L$ is the standard loss of the network (often MSE or Charbonnier loss), and $Q(\tilde{I})$ is the sharpness of the restoration. The hyper-parameter $\beta$ controls the strength of the sharpness enhancement.

Training with this loss forces the network to not only minimize the distance to the reference but also maximize the inherent sharpness of the output. However, increasing $\beta$ too much can lead to ringing. This trade-off necessitates a more sophisticated way to measure quality—one that can detect when "sharpness" becomes "artifact."

## The Sharpness Informed (SI) Class of Metrics

The core contribution of the paper is the development of SI-PSNR and SI-SSIM. These metrics modify traditional formulas by incorporating a "Ringing Detection Ratio," denoted as $\alpha$. The ratio $\alpha$ quantifies the structural degradation between a reference patch and a restored patch based on their respective sharpness values $Q$ and $\tilde{Q}$:

$$
\alpha = \frac{|\tilde{Q} - Q|}{Q}
$$

When the sharpness of the restored patch deviates significantly from the reference—often a sign of ringing or blurring—the value of $\alpha$ increases. This ratio is then used to weight the error calculations in standard metrics. For example, the Sharpness Informed Mean Squared Error (SI-MSE) for an image patch $K$ is defined as:

$$
SI\text{-}MSE(I_K, \tilde{I}_K) = MSE(I_K, \tilde{I}_K) \cdot \alpha^\rho
$$

Here, $\rho$ is an exponent used to adjust the penalty's severity. This weighted error is then used to calculate the Sharpness Informed PSNR:

$$
SI\text{-}PSNR = 10 \cdot \log_{10} \left( \frac{255^2}{SI\text{-}MSE(I, \tilde{I})} \right)
$$

Similarly, SI-SSIM is calculated by applying the same weighting factor to the standard SSIM value:

$$
SI\text{-}SSIM(I, \tilde{I}) = SSIM(I, \tilde{I}) \cdot \alpha^\rho
$$

By penalizing regions with high $\alpha$, these metrics effectively "downvote" images that achieve high sharpness through artificial ringing, even if their pixel-wise error is low.

## Performance and Empirical Validation

The effectiveness of these new metrics was evaluated against existing state-of-the-art measures, including neural-network-based metrics like LPIPS and traditional ones like PSNR-HVS. Two datasets were used for benchmarking: the widely recognized KADID-10K (which contains various sharpness and contrast distortions) and a new dataset created by the authors specifically for sharpness perception.

In the KADID-10K evaluation, the proposed SI-PSNR achieved a Pearson Linear Correlation Coefficient (PLCC) of 0.905, significantly outperforming all other PSNR variants and ranking second only to the much more computationally expensive LPIPS (PLCC 0.921). On the authors' new dataset, SI-SSIM performed excellently with a PLCC of 0.910, comparable to the best-performing standard SSIM.

| Metric | KADID-10K (PLCC) | New Dataset (PLCC) |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| PSNR | 0.675 | 0.748 |
| SSIM | 0.791 | 0.912 |
| LPIPS | 0.921 | 0.852 |
| **SI-PSNR** | **0.905** | **0.857** |
| **SI-SSIM** | **0.841** | **0.910** |

The results indicate that the SI class of metrics offers a robust balance. They provide a high correlation with human perception, especially in the presence of sharpening artifacts, without requiring the heavy computational resources of deep-learning-based metrics. This makes them suitable for integration into real-time image processing pipelines or as loss functions themselves.

## Significance for Future Research

This work demonstrates that "accuracy" in image restoration is not merely a matter of pixel-wise fidelity. By proving that human observers prefer slightly over-sharpened images, the researchers have highlighted a path for improving deblurring models to be more perceptually satisfying.

The introduction of SI-PSNR and SI-SSIM provides the community with a set of tools to evaluate these models more effectively. Unlike traditional metrics that are blind to ringing, or neural metrics that operate as "black boxes," SI metrics provide a transparent and mathematically grounded way to penalize artifacts while rewarding genuine sharpness. Furthermore, the newly introduced dataset serves as a specialized benchmark for testing future IQA metrics against sharpness-specific degradations, filling a notable gap in existing image quality databases. 

By aligning objective metrics more closely with the nuances of the Human Visual System, this research facilitates the development of restoration algorithms that do not just recover lost data, but enhance the overall aesthetic experience of the viewer.
