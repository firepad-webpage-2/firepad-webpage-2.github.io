High-order numerical methods, such as the discontinuous Galerkin (DG) approach, are preferred for fluid dynamics simulations due to their low dissipation and dispersion errors, which allow for the accurate capture of unsteady flow features. However, these methods often encounter stability issues when modeling complex fluids described by non-ideal equations of state (EOS). In regimes such as transcritical or supercritical flows, spatial variations in thermodynamic properties can trigger spurious pressure oscillations, which often lead to simulation failure.

Traditional stabilization techniques typically rely on artificial viscosity or shock-capturing limiters that require carefully tuned heuristic parameters. This research investigates an alternative approach: building physical consistency directly into the numerical discretization. By developing schemes that are exactly or approximately pressure equilibrium conserving (EPEC/APEC) and enforcing a mathematical entropy inequality through entropy correction, the authors provide a framework for simulating real-gas effects with enhanced robustness and minimal numerical dissipation.

## Governing Equations and Thermodynamic Modeling

The study focuses on the compressible Euler equations, which describe the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy. For a fluid in $d$ dimensions, the system is expressed as:

$$\n\partial_t u + \sum_{m=1}^d \frac{\partial f_m(u)}{\partial x_m} = 0\n$$

where $u$ is the vector of conservative variables and $f_m(u)$ represents the flux in the $m$-th coordinate direction. Unlike ideal gases, real fluids under high pressure or near the critical point require complex EOS to relate pressure $p$ and internal energy $e$ to density $\rho$ and temperature $T$. This work utilizes the van der Waals and Peng-Robinson equations of state, which account for molecular volume and intermolecular forces.

A key property of these equations is the existence of a convex entropy function $S(u)$. For a physically consistent solution, the system must satisfy the entropy inequality:

$$\n\frac{\partial S(u)}{\partial t} + \sum_{m=1}^d \frac{\partial F_m(u)}{\partial x_m} \leq 0\n$$

where $F_m(u)$ is the entropy flux. In the context of non-ideal EOS, the mathematical entropy is defined as $S(u) = -\rho s$, where $s$ is the specific thermodynamic entropy. The enforcement of this inequality at the discrete level is a primary mechanism for ensuring the stability of high-order simulations.

## Preserving Pressure Equilibrium

Spurious oscillations often arise in multi-component or non-ideal flows when the numerical scheme fails to preserve a state of uniform pressure and velocity. If a fluid is at a constant pressure $p$ and velocity $v$, a pressure equilibrium preserving (PEC) scheme ensures that these quantities remain constant over time, regardless of variations in density or internal energy.

The authors categorize PEC schemes into two types:
1.  **Exactly Pressure Equilibrium Conserving (EPEC):** These schemes satisfy the PEC condition to machine precision. The paper introduces a new EPEC flux based on a generalized version of Tadmor’s shuffle condition. This involves a specific density averaging technique to maintain the balance between internal energy and pressure gradients. A proposed density average takes the form:
    $$\n\rho_{avg} = \frac{\rho_L \sqrt{\rho_R} + \rho_R \sqrt{\rho_L}}{\sqrt{\rho_L} + \sqrt{\rho_R}}\n$$
2.  **Approximately Pressure Equilibrium Conserving (APEC):** These schemes maintain equilibrium only approximately but are often more robust for highly non-ideal fluids. In APEC formulations, the density average is decoupled from other thermodynamic averages, typically using a simple arithmetic mean.

Numerical experiments show that while EPEC fluxes provide superior accuracy in maintaining constant pressure states, they can be highly sensitive to the time-step size (CFL number). In contrast, APEC fluxes demonstrate greater stability in transcritical regimes, where the fluid transitions through state regions with high thermodynamic gradients.

## The Nodal Discontinuous Galerkin Framework

The methods are implemented using a nodal DG approach, which represents the solution within each element $D_k$ using polynomial basis functions at specific quadrature nodes, such as the Legendre-Gauss-Lobatto (LGL) nodes. The discretization utilizes "flux differencing," a technique that replaces standard derivatives with a sum of two-point volume fluxes.

The semi-discrete formulation for an element is given by:
$$\n\frac{d u_i}{d t} + \text{Volume Terms} + \text{Interface Terms} = 0\n$$
The volume terms involve a consistent and symmetric volume flux $f_{vol}(u_L, u_R, n_{LR})$, while the interface terms use a numerical flux $f_n^*(u_L, u_R)$ to communicate between elements. By choosing $f_{vol}$ to be an EPEC or APEC flux, the scheme inherits the desired pressure preservation properties.

## Robustness through Entropy Correction

Even with PEC fluxes, high-order DG methods can become unstable due to aliasing errors or under-resolved gradients. To address this, the authors employ an entropy correction (EC) method based on flux-corrected transport (FCT). The core idea is to blend a high-order flux ($f_{high}$) with a low-order, dissipative, and entropy-stable flux ($f_{low}$) using an element-wise blending coefficient $\theta$:

$$\nf = (1 - \theta) f_{high} + \theta f_{low}\n$$

The coefficient $\theta$ is calculated dynamically based on a cell entropy residual $\delta_k(u_h)$. If the high-order scheme violates the discrete entropy inequality ($\delta_k(u_h) < 0$), the blending coefficient increases to introduce just enough dissipation from the low-order flux to satisfy the inequality.

The advantage of this approach is its minimal impact on accuracy. In smooth regions of the flow, $\theta$ remains near zero, preserving the high-order accuracy of the DG scheme. In regions with shocks or under-resolved turbulence, EC provides the necessary stabilization without the need for manual parameter tuning.

## Numerical Results and Transcritical Flow Applications

The researchers evaluated the combined APEC and EC framework on several challenging benchmarks, including transcritical density waves and two-dimensional mixing layers.

### Transcritical Mixing Layer
In a mixing layer involving Peng-Robinson fluids, the APEC flux differencing scheme was tested to see how long it could maintain stability. While the scheme without EC eventually diverged at long simulation times (e.g., $4t_c$), the addition of entropy correction allowed the simulation to continue stably. The blending coefficient $\theta$ was found to be extremely small (on the order of $10^{-4}$ or $10^{-5}$), indicating that very little dissipation was required to maintain global stability.

### Two-Dimensional Transcritical Jet Injection
One of the most complex tests performed was the injection of a liquid-like transcritical jet into a gas-like environment. This problem features strong gradients in the adiabatic index $\gamma$ and density. The APEC+EC formulation successfully captured the jet breakup and turbulent structures. The authors noted that for this specific problem, a slight "smoothing" and scaling of the blending coefficient $\theta$ helped stabilize the calculation and allowed for larger time steps. This suggests that while EC is powerful, the spatial distribution of dissipation may require consideration in highly chaotic flows.

## Significance of the Work

This study represents a step forward in making high-order numerical methods practical for real-world engineering problems involving non-ideal fluids. By moving away from purely heuristic stabilization and toward schemes that respect the underlying physics—specifically pressure equilibrium and entropy stability—the authors have developed a more general and robust simulation framework.

Key takeaways include:
*   The quantification of pressure errors introduced by different EPEC and APEC fluxes in the DG context.
*   The demonstration that APEC schemes are generally more robust than EPEC schemes for highly non-ideal equations of state under extreme conditions.
*   The validation of entropy correction as a minimally dissipative stabilization mechanism that enhances the long-time robustness of under-resolved simulations.
*   The successful application of these methods to multi-dimensional transcritical flows, showing they can handle the complex thermodynamic variations found in modern propulsion and energy systems.

Future research directions identified by the work include the development of limiters that can guarantee the positivity of density and temperature while simultaneously respecting pressure equilibrium, further closing the gap between mathematical theory and robust engineering simulation.
