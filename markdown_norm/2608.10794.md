## The Challenge of Nonlinear Optics and the ENZ Solution

Nonlinear optics (NLO) is a cornerstone of modern photonics, governing phenomena where light interacts with matter in a way that the material's response depends non-linearly on the strength of the optical field. These processes are essential for a vast array of applications, ranging from classical telecommunications and signal processing to the frontiers of quantum information science and sensing. However, a significant hurdle in the miniaturization of photonic components is the inherently weak nonlinear response of most natural materials. Typically, achieving significant nonlinear effects requires high-power laser systems, which is incompatible with the growing demand for low-power, integrated optical devices.

To address this, researchers have turned to specialized material platforms that can enhance light-matter interactions. Among these, Epsilon-Near-Zero (ENZ) materials have emerged as a particularly promising candidate. ENZ materials are defined by having a real part of their dielectric permittivity $\epsilon \approx 0$ at a specific frequency or wavelength. In this regime, the optical wave undergoes a dramatic transformation: its phase velocity becomes effectively infinite, and the wavelength stretches. More importantly, the continuity of the normal component of the electric displacement field leads to a massive enhancement of the electric field within the material. This local field enhancement can significantly boost nonlinear processes like Third-Harmonic Generation (THG), where three photons of a pump frequency $\omega_0$ combine to create a single photon at the tripled frequency $3\omega_0$.

Indium Tin Oxide (ITO) is a prominent example of a tunable ENZ material. By adjusting its carrier concentration—often through annealing—the ENZ wavelength can be shifted across the near-infrared spectrum. While experimental studies have demonstrated the high nonlinear efficiency of ITO, developing a theoretical framework that accurately describes these processes in the quantum regime has remained a challenge. Existing models often rely on semi-classical hydrodynamic equations, which provide microscopic detail but are computationally expensive and struggle to describe fully quantum light-matter interactions, such as those involving single photons or quantum noise.

## The Macroscopic Quantum Electrodynamics Framework

The research presented here overcomes these theoretical hurdles by utilizing the framework of Macroscopic Quantum Electrodynamics (QED). Unlike microscopic models that track every individual charge carrier, macroscopic QED describes the electromagnetic field in the presence of dispersive and lossy media using coarse-grained material parameters like permittivity $\epsilon \approx 0$ and nonlinear susceptibility $\chi^{(3)}$. This approach is particularly advantageous for ENZ materials, which are inherently lossy (characterized by the imaginary part of the permittivity).

At the heart of this framework is the Green's tensor quantization method. In classical electrodynamics, a Green's tensor $\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}', \omega)$ describes the field produced at a point $\mathbf{r}$ by a source at $\mathbf{r}'$. In the quantum regime, this tensor is used to define the electric field operator $\hat{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r}, \omega)$, which describes the quantum fluctuations and dynamics of the light field. Because the material absorbs energy (loss), the framework must include "noise currents" to satisfy the laws of thermodynamics and quantum mechanics. These noise currents are represented by local polariton operators $\hat{f} \text{ and } \hat{f}^\dagger$, which describe the combined excitations of the electromagnetic field and the material's dipoles.

By linking the losses in the material (the imaginary part of the permittivity) to the field fluctuations through the Fluctuation-Dissipation Theorem, macroscopic QED provides a rigorous way to study NLO in realistic, lossy environments. This allows researchers to incorporate experimentally measurable quantities directly into the quantum model, bridging the gap between abstract theory and laboratory observations.

## Quantizing Third-Harmonic Generation

To model the specific case of THG, the researchers derived a closed-form analytical expression for the electric field operator at the third-harmonic frequency. The derivation starts by assuming a strong pump field, which can be treated using the Slowly-Varying Amplitude Approximation (SVAA). This approximation is valid when the envelope of the light pulse changes much more slowly than its optical carrier frequency. The third-order nonlinear polarization, which acts as the source for the new $3\omega_0$ signal, is then calculated using the nonlinear susceptibility $\chi^{(3)}$.

The resulting quantum expression for the electric field at frequency $\omega$ is governed by the following integral:

$$
\hat{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r}, \omega) = i \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{\pi \epsilon_0}} \frac{\omega^2}{c^2} \int d^3\mathbf{r}' \sqrt{\text{Im}[\epsilon(\mathbf{r}', \omega)]} \mathbf{G}(\mathbf{r}, \mathbf{r}', \omega) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{r}', \omega)
$$

In this equation, $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant, $\epsilon_0$ is the vacuum permittivity, and $c$ is the speed of light. The term $\text{Im}[\epsilon(\mathbf{r}', \omega)]$ accounts for the material losses, ensuring that the quantum noise is correctly modeled. By solving the Helmholtz equation with the nonlinear polarization as a source, the researchers obtained a specific operator for the THG field.

The primary metric for success in these experiments is the THG generation efficiency, $\eta_{THG}$. This efficiency is defined as the ratio of the integrated peak intensity of the generated third-harmonic signal to the intensity of the incident pump field. The developed model provides a direct way to calculate $\eta_{THG}$ as a function of incident angle, wavelength, and material thickness, accounting for the complex interplay between dispersion, absorption, and local field enhancement.

## Experimental Characterization of ITO Nanolayers

To validate the theoretical framework, the researchers performed experiments using thin films of Indium Tin Oxide (ITO). The samples consisted of 31 nm thick annealed ITO layers deposited on a glass (SiO2) substrate. The first step was to precisely characterize the linear optical properties of these nanolayers using spectroscopic ellipsometry.

![figures-1-4](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10794v1/img-1.jpeg)
*{fig:figure-0}-4: (a-b) Spectroscopic ellipsometry data showing the measured angles $\Psi$ and $\Delta$ alongside theoretical fits for different incident angles. (c) Extracted refractive index $n$ and extinction coefficient $k$, identifying the ENZ wavelength where the real part of the permittivity vanishes.*

As shown in the ellipsometry data ({fig:figure-5}), the ENZ wavelength was identified at $\lambda_{ENZ} = 1219.8 \text{ nm}$. At this specific wavelength, the refractive index $n$ and the extinction coefficient $k$ cross, indicating the point where the real part of the permittivity $\epsilon$ is zero. This characterization is crucial because the performance of ENZ devices is extremely sensitive to the exact position of this zero-crossing point and the magnitude of the residual losses (represented by $k$).

The experimental setup for measuring THG efficiency ({fig:figure-5}) utilized an Optical Parametric Oscillator (OPO) to generate high-intensity, TM-polarized pulses in the near-infrared range ($1150-1230 \text{ nm}$). TM-polarization (transverse magnetic) is essential because it allows the electric field to have a component normal to the film surface, which is the specific configuration required to trigger the ENZ field enhancement. The pulses had a duration of $125 \text{ fs}$ and a peak intensity of $0.51 \text{ GW/cm}^2$. The transmitted THG signal was then carefully filtered and detected using a monochromator and a photomultiplier tube.

## Benchmarking the Theoretical Model

The core result of the paper is the comparison between the predicted $\eta_{THG}$ from the macroscopic QED model and the experimental measurements. The theoretical model incorporates an effective nonlinear coefficient, $\chi^{(eff)}$, which accounts for the local field enhancement factors $\mathcal{L}(\omega)$ at both the pump and harmonic frequencies. In thin films, these factors are particularly strong near the ENZ wavelength and at specific incident angles, such as the Brewster angle or the Berreman mode resonance.

The experimental results ({fig:figure-6}) show a clear angular dependence for $\eta_{THG}$. The efficiency is low at near-normal incidence but rises sharply as the angle increases, peaking around $60^\circ$. This peak is a direct consequence of the field enhancement occurring when the light is coupled into the thin ENZ layer.

![figure-6](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10794v1/img-6.jpeg)
*{fig:figure-6}: Comparison of experimental THG efficiency (dots) and the theoretical model (solid lines) as a function of incident angle $\theta$ for various pump wavelengths. The model successfully captures the efficiency peak around $60^\circ$.*

The model showed good agreement with the experimental data for incident angles greater than $20^\circ$. It correctly predicted the shape of the angular curves and the relative ordering of efficiencies for different wavelengths. Quantitatively, the experimental values were somewhat lower than the theoretical ones, which the authors attribute to the finite collection efficiency of the experimental setup and the use of a $\chi^{(3)}$ value measured from a thicker sample, which might not perfectly match the properties of the 31 nm nanolayer.

## Locality, Nonlocality, and Future Horizons

One of the most interesting findings was a discrepancy observed at small incident angles, specifically in the range of $0^\circ \text{ to } 20^\circ$. In this region, the experimental THG efficiency decreased significantly, a trend not captured by the theoretical model. The authors identify this as a signature of "nonlocal" effects. The macroscopic QED framework used in this study is inherently "local," meaning it assumes the polarization at a point depends only on the electric field at that same point. In very thin ENZ films, however, the response of electrons can become spatially dispersive, meaning the material's properties depend on the wavevector $k$ as well as the frequency $\omega$.

Despite this limitation at small angles, the research establishes a robust and accessible framework for studying quantum NLO in complex media. Unlike hydrodynamic models that are restricted to specific geometries, the Green's tensor approach can be applied to virtually any structure, including multi-layered stacks and nano-antennas. Furthermore, while this work focused on THG, the framework is easily extendable to other third-order processes like four-wave mixing or cross-phase modulation, as well as second-order processes in materials that lack centrosymmetry.

The significance of this work extends into the realm of quantum technologies. The ability to accurately model quantum noise and field enhancement in ENZ materials is vital for developing high-sensitivity quantum sensors and novel light sources for quantum communication. Moreover, the strong light-matter interaction in the ENZ regime provides a potential path toward quantum nondemolition measurements, where the state of a photon can be detected without destroying it. By providing a practical, analytical platform that bridges material science and quantum optics, this research paves the way for the next generation of integrated quantum photonic devices.
