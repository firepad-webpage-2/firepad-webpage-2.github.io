Matching theory has long been a cornerstone of economic analysis, providing a framework to understand how students are assigned to schools, how organs are allocated to patients, and how workers find employers. Since the seminal work of Gale and Shapley in 1962, the standard model has relied on the assumption that agents only care about their direct partner. In a simple marriage market, for instance, a person's happiness depends solely on whom they marry, regardless of who else marries whom.

However, many real-world scenarios violate this "no-externalities" assumption. A researcher’s productivity might depend on whether their frequent collaborator is also hired by the same university. A doctor’s job satisfaction might be tied to whether their spouse finds a position in the same city. In these cases, agents have preferences over the entire matching structure of the market, not just their own match. This introduces "externalities," which significantly complicate the definition of stability. If two agents consider "blocking" the current matching to form a new partnership, they must speculate about how the rest of the market will react. Will other pairs dissolve? Will new ones form? These speculations, or conjectures, are the focus of "Rationalizable Behavior in Matching with Externalities."

## The Problem with Traditional Stability

In classical matching, a matching is stable if no individual wants to be single and no pair of agents would rather be with each other than with their current partners. With externalities, a "blocking pair" $(i, j)$ cannot simply look at each other; they must consider the "residual matching"—the way everyone else in the market is matched once $i$ and $j$ leave their current partners to join each other.

Previous attempts to solve this, such as **P-stability**, made a restrictive assumption: when a pair deviates, they assume everyone else stays exactly where they were, and the former partners of the deviating agents simply become single. This "quasifixed belief" is often unrealistic. It assumes that the rest of the market is static and unresponsive. More importantly, P-stable matchings often do not exist. In markets with couples, for example, the rigid assumptions of P-stability frequently lead to a mathematical vacuum where no stable outcome can be found.

This paper proposes a new solution by borrowing the concept of **rationalizability** from non-cooperative game theory. Instead of assuming a fixed reaction, the authors argue that agents should only entertain "credible" conjectures about how the rest of the market will rearrange itself. A conjecture is credible if it doesn't assume other agents will act irrationally.

## Defining Rationalizable Conjectures

The core innovation of the paper is the **conjecture-rationalizable procedure**. This is an iterative process that disciplines what agents believe about the residual market. 

Imagine a set of agents $A$ and $B$. A matching $\mu$ is a mapping where:

$$\mu(i) = j \iff \mu(j) = i$$

where $i$ and $j$ are agents from opposite sides or an agent is matched to themselves (indicating they are single). In a market with externalities, agent $i$ has a preference relation $\succsim_i$ over the set of all possible matchings $M$.

When a pair $(i, j)$ considers matching, they form a **conjecture**, which is a belief about the residual matching $\mu_{-i,j}$ involving all agents except $i$ and $j$. The authors define a system of conjectures $C$ as a set of possible residual matchings that agents consider plausible. A matching $\mu$ is then **C-stable** if:
1. No agent $i$ prefers all possible outcomes of being single over their current match.
2. No pair $(i, j)$ prefers all possible outcomes of matching together over their current matches.

To find the "right" $C$, the authors use an elimination process. They start with $C^0$, which contains all possible matchings. Then, they remove any conjecture that assumes someone else in the residual market would act in a way that is "dominated." For example, if a conjecture $\mu_{-i,j}$ assumes agent $k$ is matched to $l$, but $k$ would strictly prefer to be single regardless of what everyone else does, that conjecture is irrational and is discarded. By repeating this process, the sets of conjectures shrink:

$$C^n(i, j, \succsim) \subseteq C^{n-1}(i, j, \succsim) \subseteq \dots \subseteq C^0(i, j, \succsim) = M_{-i,j}$$

The process eventually settles on a set of **rationalizable conjectures** $C^\infty$:

$$C^\infty(i, j, \succsim) = \bigcap_{n \geq 0} C^n(i, j, \succsim)$$

A matching is **conjecture-rationalizable stable** if it is stable according to these refined, mutually consistent beliefs.

## Existence and the "Residual Market"

A major result of this research is the proof that **conjecture-rationalizable stable matchings always exist**. This is a significant improvement over P-stability. The authors prove this by showing that even in the most complex markets with externalities, there is always at least one matching that no pair can "credibly" block.

The logic relies on the fact that as we eliminate irrational conjectures, the requirements for a pair to block a matching become more stringent. In the authors' "conservative" framework, a pair only deviates if they are better off under *every* remaining plausible conjecture. Since the set of rationalizable conjectures $C^\infty$ is smaller than the initial set $M$, agents are more likely to find a "blocking" move risky, which helps in sustaining stability.

Furthermore, the paper establishes that these stable matchings are **consistent**. If a matching $\mu$ is stable, then the actual configuration of the rest of the market $\mu_{-i,\mu(i)}$ must be one of the conjectures that agent $i$ considers possible. This ensures that the stability isn't based on agents being fundamentally wrong about the equilibrium they are in.

## Epistemic Foundations: Why Do They Match?

To provide a deeper justification for their model, the authors delve into **epistemic game theory**. They ask: "What must agents believe about each other for this stability concept to make sense?"

They identify three key ingredients for conjecture-rationalizable stability:
1.  **Pairwise Rationality (PR)**: Agents don't make moves that they know will make them worse off.
2.  **Common Belief in Pairwise Rationality (CBPR)**: I believe you are rational, you believe I believe you are rational, and so on. This is what the iterative elimination of conjectures formally represents.
3.  **Belief Correctness (BC)**: In the actual matching that occurs, agents' beliefs about who is matched with whom must match reality.

The paper proves that rationalizable matchings are exactly those consistent with PR and CBPR. When you add Belief Correctness, you get conjecture-rationalizable stability.

This contrasts sharply with **P-stability**. The authors show that P-stability essentially assumes **Quasifixed Beliefs (QFB)**—the idea that others simply don't react. This is an "epistemically simple" but often "behaviorally implausible" assumption. It doesn't require agents to think about each other's rationality; it just requires them to be stubborn in their belief that the world is static.

## Application: The Case of Couples

The "matching with couples" problem is a classic hurdle in matching theory. When two people (a couple) apply for jobs, their preferences are intrinsically linked. They might prefer a city where both get decent jobs over a city where one gets a "dream job" and the other is unemployed. Because the couple acts as a single unit in many ways, but their presence affects two separate job slots, standard Gale-Shapley algorithms can fail to find a stable matching.

The paper applies its rationalizable framework to this problem. In a standard example where P-stability fails to exist, conjecture-rationalizable stability finds a solution. It does so by recognizing that a couple might refrain from "blocking" a current assignment if they realize that their move would trigger a sequence of job changes by other doctors that could ultimately leave one of the partners in a worse position. By endogenizing these "counter-reactions" through rationalizable conjectures, the model captures a more realistic level of strategic caution.

## Significance of the Work

This paper bridges a gap between two major fields: the cooperative/reduced-form analysis of matching markets and the non-cooperative analysis of strategic games. By applying the "Pearce-Bernheim program" to matching with externalities, the authors provide a framework that is:

*   **Robust**: It guarantees existence where previous models failed.
*   **Theoretically Grounded**: It replaces arbitrary counterfactual assumptions with the standard game-theoretic logic of rationalizability.
*   **Epistemically Clear**: It specifies exactly what agents need to know and believe for stability to hold.

While the paper focuses on "one-to-one" matching (like the marriage market), the implications extend to "many-to-one" markets (like firms and workers) and even more general coalitional games. The research suggests that in any system where agents' welfare is interconnected, stability cannot be assessed in a vacuum. It must be assessed through the lens of what agents can reasonably expect others to do in response to change. By providing the tools to discipline these expectations, Nicolò, Salmaso, and Saulle have provided a more reliable way to predict and analyze the outcomes of complex, interconnected markets.
