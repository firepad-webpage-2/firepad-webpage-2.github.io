## Overcoming Noise in Low-Dose PET Imaging

Positron Emission Tomography (PET) is a cornerstone of modern medical imaging, providing critical insights into physiological processes for diagnosing oncology, neurology, and cardiology patients. However, the procedure requires injecting radioactive tracers, which exposes patients to ionizing radiation. Reducing this dose is a primary clinical objective, especially for children or patients needing frequent follow-up scans. 

Low-dose PET (LD-PET) acquisitions, unfortunately, suffer from significantly degraded image quality. The lower count of detected photons leads to high noise levels and a loss of structural detail, which can obscure small lesions or subtle pathological changes. Recovering standard-dose (SD) quality from LD-PET data is the central challenge addressed by SinoDiff.

Current approaches to this problem face several hurdles. Supervised deep learning models typically require paired LD and SD scans for training. Acquiring such pairs is often impractical or unethical. Furthermore, these models are often "dose-specific"—a model trained to recover a 10% dose may perform poorly on a 25% dose. Self-supervised methods, which do not need paired data, often struggle with "oversmoothing," where fine anatomical details are lost in the denoising process. SinoDiff addresses these issues by operating directly in the sinogram domain—the raw measurement space of the scanner—and by embedding the fundamental physics of PET acquisition into a self-supervised diffusion framework.

## The Physics of PET Degradation

The quality of a PET image is fundamentally limited by the number of detected coincidences, which follows Poisson counting statistics. The detection process can be modeled as a Poisson random variable $X$ with an expected rate:

$$X \sim \text{Poisson}(\tau \Lambda(\lambda))$$

where $\Lambda(\lambda)$ is the expected rate of occurrences induced by the tracer activity $\lambda$ and $\tau$ represents the acquisition duration. Reducing the dose or the time $\tau$ reduces the total count, increasing the relative noise.

SinoDiff utilizes a mathematical operation known as **Poisson thinning** to simulate this degradation. Instead of adding generic Gaussian noise (as seen in typical diffusion models), the forward process in SinoDiff removes events from a high-dose sinogram to create a low-dose one. If the probability of retaining an event is $\alpha_t$, the resulting sinogram $X_t$ is:

$$X_t = \mathcal{P}_{\alpha_t}(X_{t-1})$$

where $\mathcal{P}_{\alpha_t}(\cdot)$ is the thinning operator. A key property of this approach is that if $X_{t-1}$ is Poisson-distributed, then the thinned version $X_t$ is also Poisson-distributed:

$$X_t \sim \text{Poisson}(\bar{\alpha}_t \tau \Lambda(\lambda))$$

This ensures that the "noise" introduced during training is physically consistent with the noise encountered in real clinical low-dose scans. This allows the model to learn the specific statistical structure of PET data.

## SinoDiff: A Physics-Consistent Diffusion Model

SinoDiff is structured as a diffusion model that operates on sinograms. Diffusion models generally work through a two-step process: a forward process that gradually destroys data and a reverse process that learns to recover it.

### Forward Diffusion (Dose Degradation)
The model defines a sequence of dose levels $X_0 \to X_1 \to \cdots \to X_T$, where $X_0$ is the standard-dose sinogram and $X_T$ is a highly degraded low-dose version. At each step $t$, the sinogram is thinned using a retention probability $\alpha_t \in (0, 1)$. This creates a set of nested observations, allowing the model to learn how to recover counts at any level of degradation.

### Reverse Diffusion (Dose Recovery)
The goal of the network $G(\Theta; X_t, \gamma(t))$ is to reverse this thinning. Since $X_{t-1}$ contains all the events in $X_t$ plus some missing events, the model learns to estimate the missing increment $\Delta X_{t-1}$:

$$X_{t-1} = X_t + \Delta X_{t-1}$$

By breaking the recovery into small, incremental steps, the model achieves higher stability compared to models that try to jump directly from a 10% dose to 100% in one step. During inference, a single model can be used to recover a standard-dose sinogram from any starting dose level $t_d$ by iteratively applying this recovery process.

## Capturing Long-Range Dependencies in Sinograms

One unique challenge of working in the sinogram domain is that anatomical structures are not represented as local blobs, but as sine-wave patterns that span across projection angles and detector bins. Standard convolutional neural networks (CNNs) excel at local patterns but often fail to capture these long-range global dependencies.

To solve this, SinoDiff introduces a **Frequency Domain Convolution (FDC)** kernel. The FDC module processes data in two parallel branches:
1.  **Spatial Branch:** A standard $3 \times 3$ convolution to capture local details.
2.  **Frequency Branch:** This branch transforms the data using a Fourier transform, applies a learnable complex mixing matrix $W_f$, and then transforms it back.

By operating in the frequency domain, the network can capture correlations between distant points in the sinogram, which correspond to the global geometry of the anatomical structures being imaged. The outputs are fused using learnable weights $\omega_s$ and $\omega_f$ to balance local and global information.

## Training and Unified Inference

The model is trained using only standard-dose sinograms. Because the forward process (Poisson thinning) is a known physical operation, the model can generate its own training pairs $(X_t, X_{t-1})$ purely from SD data. The training objective combines three loss terms:

$$L_{total} = L_{MSE} + \beta_{SSIM}L_{SSIM} + \beta_{Freq}L_{Freq}$$

*   **$L_{MSE}$:** Ensures pixel-wise accuracy of the counts.
*   **$L_{SSIM}$:** Preserves structural similarity and anatomical edges.
*   **$L_{Freq}$:** Matches the frequency characteristics in the Fourier domain to ensure global consistency.

Once trained, the "Unified SinoDiff" model is dose-agnostic. A clinician can feed it a 10% dose scan, a 50% dose scan, or any other predefined level, and the same weights will perform the recovery. The recovered sinogram is then reconstructed into a final image using standard clinical algorithms like OSEM.

## Experimental Results and Performance

SinoDiff was evaluated on two distinct clinical datasets: 18F-FDG (commonly used for glucose metabolism) and 18F-FDOPA (used for imaging dopamine systems). It was compared against supervised models (UNet, RED) and self-supervised image-domain models (DIP).

### Image Quality Analysis
SinoDiff demonstrated superior performance in preserving anatomical detail compared to other self-supervised methods. While image-domain methods like DIP tended to oversmooth or introduce "blotchy" artifacts at very low doses (10%), SinoDiff maintained clear structural boundaries. Quantitative metrics such as SSIM (Structural Similarity) and PSNR (Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio) showed that SinoDiff consistently outperformed supervised models when those models were not specifically retrained for the testing dose.

A critical metric for PET is count consistency—whether the total number of detected events in the recovered image matches the true physical count. SinoDiff achieved the lowest Global Count Bias (GCB) and Angular Count Bias (ACB) across almost all dose levels. This indicates that the physics-consistent thinning process during training successfully taught the model to respect the underlying conservation laws of PET acquisition.

## Clinical Significance and Future Impact

The introduction of SinoDiff marks a shift toward more robust and physically grounded deep learning for medical imaging. Its contributions are three-fold:

1.  **Safety:** By enabling high-quality imaging at dose levels as low as 10% of the standard, it significantly reduces radiation risks for vulnerable patients.
2.  **Practicality:** It removes the need for paired training data. This means hospitals can train the model using their existing high-quality SD datasets without needing to perform specialized low-dose "paired" research scans.
3.  **Efficiency:** A single model can handle various dose protocols. In a busy clinic where different patients might have different scan durations or tracer amounts, having one "unified" model simplifies the software infrastructure.

While the model currently uses predefined dose steps, its ability to generalize to unseen intermediate doses (e.g., recovering a 15% dose scan when trained on 10% and 20% steps) suggests high flexibility. Future work aims to further refine the dose scheduling and validate the method's performance on finding specific pathologies in larger clinical cohorts. By integrating physics directly into the neural architecture, SinoDiff provides a template for safer and more accurate medical AI.
