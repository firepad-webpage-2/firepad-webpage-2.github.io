In modern acoustic signal processing, tasks such as sound source localization, speech enhancement, and acoustic echo cancellation are fundamental to applications ranging from hearing aids to teleconferencing systems. A central challenge in these fields is the estimation of acoustic transfer functions, which describe how sound travels from a source to a receiver. While the Relative Transfer Function (ReTF) is a standard tool for single-source environments, it often fails in practical scenarios where multiple sources are active simultaneously. This research investigates the Relative Transfer Matrix (ReTM), a generalization of the ReTF designed for multi-source, multi-microphone environments, and explores how deep learning can improve its estimation.

## Background and the Multi-Source Challenge

Most traditional acoustic processing techniques rely on the assumption of W-disjoint orthogonality. This principle suggests that in the time-frequency domain, sound sources do not significantly overlap, allowing a system to treat a multi-source environment as a series of single-source problems. However, in dense acoustic environments—such as a crowded room with multiple people speaking or a space with various background noises like air conditioners and music—this assumption is often violated.

To address this, the Relative Transfer Matrix (ReTM) was introduced to provide a mathematical framework that relates signals received by different groups of microphones in a multi-source context. Until now, ReTM estimation has primarily relied on covariance-based methods that analyze the statistical relationships between microphone signals. While computationally simple, these methods may not capture the complex, non-linear spatial dynamics of reverberant rooms. This paper shifts the paradigm toward data-driven, supervised deep learning frameworks to determine if neural networks can learn more accurate spatial mappings for ReTM estimation.

## The Relative Transfer Matrix (ReTM) Framework

The ReTM framework assumes an environment with $L$ concurrently active sound sources and $Q$ microphones. These microphones are divided into two arbitrary sets: Group A with $Q_A$ microphones and Group B with $Q_B$ microphones. In the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) domain, the received signals at these groups are denoted as $M_A(f, t)$ and $M_B(f, t)$, respectively. These signals are related to the original source signals $S(f, t)$ through acoustic transfer function matrices $H_A(f)$ and $H_B(f)$.

The ReTM, denoted as $R_{AB}(f)$, is formally defined as:

$$
R_{AB}(f) = H_A(f)H_B(f)^\dagger
$$

where $( \cdot )^\dagger$ represents the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. This relationship allows the signals at Group A to be expressed as a linear transformation of the signals at Group B:

$$
M_A(f, t) = R_{AB}(f)M_B(f, t)
$$

In the time domain, this relationship is expressed through convolutions. For the $j$-th microphone in Group A, the signal $m_{j_A}(t)$ is the sum of filtered signals from all microphones in Group B:

$$
m_{j_A}(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{Q_B} r_{ji_{AB}}(t) * m_{i_B}(t)
$$

The research proposes using machine learning functions, such as $F_B(\cdot)$ for the frequency domain and $f_B(\cdot)$ for the time domain, to approximate these relationships and estimate the ReTM coefficients directly from multichannel recordings.

## Architectures for ReTM Estimation

The authors introduce three distinct neural network architectures, each targeting different aspects of the acoustic signal.

### STFT Convolutional Network (SCoNet)

The SCoNet operates in the frequency domain. It takes the STFT of the signals from microphone Group B and processes them to estimate the signals at Group A. To handle the complex-valued nature of STFT data, the real and imaginary components of $M_B(f, t)$ are concatenated along the channel dimension.

![sconet-architecture](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11627v1/Figure1.png)
*{fig:sconet-architecture}: The STFT Convolutional Network (SCoNet) utilizes depthwise convolution to learn frequency-specific spatial parameters.*

The architecture uses depthwise convolution operations across the time and channel axes. By using depthwise convolutions, the network can learn specific spatial parameters for each frequency bin independently, which is crucial because acoustic transfer functions are highly frequency-dependent. The output is then reshaped to provide the estimated STFT representation of the Group A signals.

### Convolutional Filter and Summation Network (FuSNet)

FuSNet (shown in {fig:fusnet-architecture}) is a time-domain model that directly implements the convolutional relationship of the ReTM. It uses $Q_A \times Q_B$ learnable one-dimensional convolutional filters. The weights of these filters are intended to represent the time-domain ReTM coefficients $r_{ji_{AB}}(t)$.

The model processes segments of the time-domain signals from Group B. To ensure the multiplicative transfer function assumption holds—which requires the processing window to be longer than the room's reverberation time—FuSNet utilizes a context window. This architecture is particularly efficient because its structure mirrors the physical process of sound propagation and mixing in the room.

### LSTM-based Autoencoder Network (LAeNet)

The LAeNet utilizes Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) blocks to capture sequential dependencies and narrowband spatial information. Unlike the convolutional approaches, LAeNet processes each frequency bin independently using a shared bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM).

![laenet-architecture](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11627v1/Figure3.png)
*{fig:laenet-architecture}: The LSTM-based Autoencoder Network (LAeNet) uses a shared BiLSTM and a fully connected network to estimate ReTM coefficients.*

After the BiLSTM layer, the temporal sequences are averaged to create a feature vector. A fully connected network $k(\cdot)$ then maps these features to the estimated ReTM coefficients. This model is more parameter-intensive but leverages the sequential modeling strengths of recurrent networks to handle temporal variations in the acoustic scene.

## Optimization and Joint Loss Functions

To train these models effectively, the researchers employ a joint loss function that combines both time-domain and frequency-domain objectives. The total loss $L$ is defined as:

$$
L = \alpha L_{SDR} + \beta L_{RSE}
$$

In this equation, $L_{SDR}$ is the negative Signal-to-Distortion Ratio, which measures the fidelity of the reconstructed time-domain waveform. $L_{RSE}$ is the Relative Spectrum Error, which ensures that the frequency content of the estimated signal matches the ground truth. The weights $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are set to 1 and 10, respectively, balancing the importance of time-domain accuracy with spectral consistency. This dual-domain approach helps the models achieve stability and robustness against noise.

## Experimental Evaluation and Comparative Analysis

The performance of these models was evaluated in a simulated room (6m x 7m x 3m) with a reverberation time $T_{60}$ of 500 ms. The study compared the deep learning models against a standard covariance-based baseline across several scenarios with varying numbers of sources (White Gaussian Noise, speech, air conditioning noise, and music) and microphones.

The evaluation used five metrics: Signal-to-Distortion Ratio (SDR), Mean Square Error (MSE), Log Spectral Distortion (LSD), Average Phase Distortion (APD), and Relative Spectrum Error (RSE).

The results indicated that the deep learning models generally outperformed the traditional covariance-based method, particularly in time-domain metrics like MSE and SDR. FuSNet showed the highest accuracy in scenarios with two sources, likely due to its direct modeling of the convolutional relationship. SCoNet and LAeNet also demonstrated high accuracy, often surpassing the baseline in multi-source, multi-microphone scenarios (e.g., three sources with 12 microphones).

The study also highlighted significant trade-offs in computational efficiency. FuSNet was the most efficient, featuring the lowest inference latency and the smallest parameter count. In contrast, LAeNet was the most complex, requiring more parameters and exhibiting higher latency due to the recurrent nature of the BiLSTM layers.

## Practical Application: Speech Denoising

To demonstrate the real-world utility of accurate ReTM estimation, the models were applied to a multi-source speech denoising task. In this application, a "noise ReTM" $R_{AB}^{(N)}$ was estimated from a noise-only recording. This matrix was then used to estimate the noise component in the microphone signals during active speech, allowing for its subtraction.

Interestingly, although FuSNet was highly accurate in the general ReTM estimation task, it performed poorly in speech denoising. Informal listening revealed that the denoised speech from FuSNet retained significant echo noise, suggesting that the model struggled to separate the primary signal from its reflections in this specific context.

In contrast, LAeNet and SCoNet proved highly effective for denoising. LAeNet achieved the best results, yielding an average SDR improvement of +10.55 dB and an average Short-Time Objective Intelligibility (STOI) improvement of 37% over the noisy baseline. SCoNet followed closely, providing a significant boost in both audio quality and intelligibility. This suggests that while time-domain filters (like those in FuSNet) are good for direct modeling, frequency-domain or sequential models (like SCoNet and LAeNet) may be better suited for applications where echo and reverberation must be carefully managed.

## Conclusion and Future Directions

This research provides evidence that supervised deep learning frameworks can significantly enhance the estimation of the Relative Transfer Matrix in multi-source acoustic environments. By moving away from purely statistical covariance-based methods, the proposed models—SCoNet, FuSNet, and LAeNet—capture more intricate spatial relationships between microphone groups.

The study reveals that the choice of architecture depends heavily on the specific requirements of the application. For tasks requiring low-latency and high raw estimation accuracy, time-domain convolutional models like FuSNet are advantageous. However, for complex enhancement tasks such as speech denoising, architectures that operate in the STFT domain or utilize recurrent structures (SCoNet and LAeNet) offer superior performance by better handling spectral characteristics and reverberation.

Future research could involve integrating these ReTM estimation frameworks with dereverberation techniques to mitigate the echo issues observed in time-domain models. Additionally, exploring how these models perform with moving sources or in real-world hardware implementations remains an important area for further investigation. This work establishes a foundation for applying data-driven spatial modeling to a wide range of multi-microphone audio processing challenges.
