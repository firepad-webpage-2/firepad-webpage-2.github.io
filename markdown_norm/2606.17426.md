## Measuring AI Capability with Dependent Data

The evaluation of large language models often relies on composite benchmarks like the Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) set. These benchmarks aggregate thousands of questions across diverse subjects such as mathematics, history, and law to produce a single performance score. In standard practice, researchers often treat each question as an independent Bernoulli trial, calculating confidence intervals as if the model's performance on one question has no statistical relationship with its performance on another.

However, this assumption of independence is often violated. A model that excels at high school algebra is statistically more likely to perform well on college-level calculus compared to a model that struggles with basic arithmetic. These internal correlations mean that the questions are not independent. Instead, they exhibit a property known as exchangeability, where the joint distribution of the outcomes is invariant to the order of the questions. This paper addresses the limitations of independence-based statistics by establishing new concentration inequalities specifically designed for infinitely exchangeable sequences.

By moving beyond the independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) framework, the authors provide a more robust way to quantify uncertainty. They demonstrate that ignoring these dependencies leads to a significant underestimation of error margins. For instance, in evaluations of modern language models, traditional i.i.d. methods might suggest a highly precise score, while the exchangeable framework reveals that the true uncertainty is several times larger due to the underlying subject-level variability shown in the heatmap above.

## The Principle of Infinite Exchangeability

To handle dependent data rigorously, the authors utilize de Finetti’s Representation Theorem. In probability theory, a sequence of random variables $X_1, \dots, X_n$ is exchangeable if their joint probability distribution does not change when the variables are reordered. This is a weaker, more flexible assumption than independence.

De Finetti’s theorem provides a powerful bridge: it states that any infinitely exchangeable sequence can be viewed as being conditionally i.i.d. given some latent "directing measure" or parameter $\Theta$. Intuitively, if we are evaluating a language model on MMLU, $\Theta$ represents the latent capability of the model in a specific domain. Once this latent capability is fixed, the individual question responses $X_i$ can be treated as independent samples from a distribution governed by $\Theta$.

The challenge in statistical concentration—the study of how much a random sum or function deviates from its expected value—is that $\Theta$ is itself a random variable. This introduces an additional layer of uncertainty. While standard tools like McDiarmid’s inequality provide bounds for independent variables, they do not account for the fluctuations caused by the randomness of the latent measure $\Theta$.

## Decomposing Sources of Fluctuation

The core theoretical contribution of this work is the decomposition of the total deviation of a function $f(X_1, \dots, X_n)$ from its mean into two distinct components. For a function $f$ of exchangeable variables, the deviation from its expectation can be split as:

$$W = f(X) - E[f(X) | \Theta]$$

$$U_f = E[f(X) | \Theta] - Ef(X)$$

The first term, $W$, is the **conditional sampling fluctuation**. This represents the "luck of the draw" at the item level—how much the score varies because we picked specific questions, assuming the model's general capability is fixed. The second term, $U_f$, is the **latent mixture fluctuation**. This captures the uncertainty inherent in the latent directing measure itself—the variability in the model's underlying performance across different domains or subjects.

By applying this decomposition, the authors derive a new concentration inequality for exchangeable sequences. They show that if the function $f$ satisfies a bounded difference condition (where changing one variable $X_i$ changes the output by at most $c_i$), the total variability is governed by an effective variance proxy:

$$\sigma^2_{eff} = \frac{1}{4} \sum_{i=1}^n c_i^2 + \sigma^2_{mix}$$

Here, $\sigma^2_{mix}$ represents the subgaussian parameter of the latent mixture. This formula explicitly shows that the total uncertainty is the sum of the traditional item-level uncertainty and the newly quantified mixture uncertainty. This theoretical result provides a formal mechanism to account for the "hidden" correlations that often plague AI evaluation.

## The Advantage of Zero-Sum Linear Contrasts

A particularly striking result in the paper concerns a specific class of statistics known as zero-sum linear contrasts. These are functions of the form $f(X) = \sum_{i=1}^N a_i X_i$ where the coefficients sum to zero ($\sum_{i=1}^N a_i = 0$). Common examples include comparing the performance of a model on two different subsets of data or comparing a model's score on a small sample to its score on a larger population.

The authors prove that for these statistics, the latent mixture fluctuation $U_f$ vanishes entirely. Because the sum of the weights is zero, the influence of the latent parameter $\Theta$ cancels out. This means that for comparing a subsample mean to a full population mean, the dependency structure of the data becomes irrelevant to the concentration bound.

This discovery has immediate practical utility for "subsample-vs-full" evaluations. If a researcher wants to estimate the full MMLU score of 14,000 questions by only testing a model on a random subset of $n$ questions, they can use a sharp, distribution-free bound:

$$P(|\bar{X}_n - \bar{X}_N| \geq t) \leq 2 \exp\left(-\frac{2t^2 nN}{R^2(N-n)}\right)$$

In this equation, $n$ is the subsample size, $N$ is the total number of questions, and $R$ is the range of the scores (usually 1 for correctness). Remarkably, this bound does not require any knowledge or estimation of the latent mixture $\sigma^2_{mix}$. It provides a rigorous guarantee that the subsample mean will be close to the full mean, even if the questions themselves are highly correlated.

## Refining Uncertainty in AI Benchmarks

To demonstrate the real-world impact of these theories, the authors analyzed response data from several large language models, including the Gemma and Qwen families, on the MMLU benchmark. 

They proposed a **hierarchical exchangeability model** to better reflect how benchmarks are structured. In this model, questions are grouped into subjects, and subjects are grouped into domains (STEM, Humanities, etc.). By treating the performance within these groups as exchangeable, the authors could estimate the different layers of variance.

Their empirical findings highlight three critical points:

1.  **Significant Underestimation:** Traditional binomial confidence intervals (which assume independence) are much too narrow. For several models, the exchangeable confidence intervals were approximately four times wider than the standard ones. This suggests that many reported "significant" differences between models may actually be within the margin of error when subject-level correlations are accounted for.
2.  **Stable Subject Performance:** Through "split-half reliability" tests, the authors found that a model's performance on one half of a subject's questions is a very strong predictor of its performance on the other half. This confirms that subjects possess a latent "difficulty" or "capability" level, justifying the use of exchangeable models over i.i.d. models.
3.  **Cost-Saving Guarantees:** The paper provides a mathematical justification for evaluating models on subsets of benchmarks. For instance, testing on 5,000 questions instead of the full 14,000 in MMLU yields an error margin of roughly 1.5%. Because the mixture term cancels in this specific comparison, this guarantee is "model-agnostic"—it holds true regardless of how biased or specialized the model's knowledge is.

By providing these tools, the research offers a path toward more responsible and statistically sound AI evaluation. It enables researchers to report uncertainty that reflects the true complexity of model behavior across different domains while also providing the theoretical foundation for more efficient, lower-cost benchmarking.
