## Understanding Joint Climate Variability in the South Central U.S.

The characterization of seasonal climate variability is a fundamental task in meteorology and environmental science, particularly for assessing risks associated with hydro-meteorological hazards. Hazards such as flooding, soil erosion, and urban runoff are rarely the result of a single atmospheric variable; rather, they emerge from the complex interplay between temperature and precipitation. Historically, much of the research in this domain has treated these variables as independent univariate time series. While such models are effective for identifying long-term trends or persistence in a single variable, they often fail to capture the co-variability and dependence structures that define compound climate conditions—such as a warm-dry or a cool-wet season.

This study addresses the limitations of univariate analysis by applying a multivariate functional framework to the U.S. South Central region. By focusing on Texas, Oklahoma, Louisiana, Arkansas, Mississippi, Tennessee, and Alabama, the researchers investigate how temperature and precipitation evolve together throughout the winter and summer seasons. The work moves beyond simple averages, treating daily weather observations as continuous functional curves. This allows for a formal statistical comparison of regional climate trajectories and the identification of latent "climate regimes"—distinct groupings of weather patterns that exhibit internal consistency and spatial coherence.

## Functional Representation of Climate Data

At the heart of this research is the treatment of climate observations as functional data. In a traditional statistical setup, one might calculate the average temperature for a month. In a functional setup, the entire sequence of daily temperatures and precipitation amounts over a season is treated as a single, multi-dimensional object.

For each weather station $j$ in a state $i$, the daily observations are represented as a bivariate functional vector:

$$
X_{ij}(t) = (X_{ij1}(t), X_{ij2}(t))'
$$

where $X_{ij1}(t)$ represents the daily average air temperature and $X_{ij2}(t)$ represents the daily total precipitation at time $t$ over a season of length $T$. To make these raw, noisy daily observations mathematically workable, the researchers use B-spline basis functions. This process smooths the data into continuous curves, preserving the underlying seasonal signal while filtering out high-frequency noise. This functional approach is particularly useful for climate data because it respects the temporal ordering and the continuous nature of weather processes, which discrete models often overlook.

## Multivariate Functional Analysis of Variance (FMANOVA)

One of the primary goals of the study is to determine if the different states in the South Central region actually experience statistically distinct climate patterns. To test this, the authors employ Multivariate Functional Analysis of Variance (FMANOVA).

The FMANOVA framework tests the null hypothesis $H_0$ that the mean bivariate functions (the average "shape" of the temperature and precipitation curves) are identical across all seven states. The researchers utilize two robust test statistics: Wilks' lambda and Pillai's trace. Because the distribution of these statistics for functional data is not standard, $p$-values are generated using permutation methods. This involves shuffling the state labels thousands of times to see how often the observed differences occur by chance.

In 2025, the results for both winter and summer led to a clear rejection of the null hypothesis ($p < 0.001$), confirming that state-level climate trajectories are not uniform. However, the drivers of these differences vary by season. In winter, differences are often driven by both temperature and precipitation gradients, particularly between geographically distant states like Oklahoma and Tennessee. In summer, the region becomes more thermally homogeneous, and temperature becomes the dominant factor in distinguishing the subtle climatic variations between states.

## Identifying Latent Regimes with Gaussian Mixture Models

While FMANOVA tells us that states differ, it does not describe the nature of the climate types present across the region. To achieve this, the researchers use Gaussian Mixture Modeling (GMM) to cluster station-level summaries.

A GMM assumes that the observed data is generated from a mixture of several underlying Gaussian (normal) distributions, each representing a "regime." The probability density of a seasonal summary $x^{(q)}$ is modeled as:

$$
p(x^{(q)}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \mathcal{N}(x^{(q)} | \mu_k, \Sigma_k)
$$

In this equation, $K$ is the number of clusters (regimes), $\pi_k$ is the mixing proportion (how common the regime is), $\mu_k$ is the mean vector (the center of the regime in temperature-precipitation space), and $\Sigma_k$ is the covariance matrix (describing the spread and relationship between temperature and precipitation within that regime).

By using the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), the study identifies three primary regimes in both seasons. These regimes are not just mathematical constructs; they correspond to physical climate conditions:
1.  **Regime 1 (Intermediate):** Moderate temperatures and moderate precipitation.
2.  **Regime 2 (Warm/Wet):** Higher temperatures and a broader spread of precipitation, often found near the Gulf Coast.
3.  **Regime 3 (Cold/Dry):** Lower temperatures and concentrated, low precipitation, typically found inland or further north.

## Seasonal Asymmetry and Spatiotemporal Stability

A significant finding of the study is the differing stability of these regimes across time. By extending the GMM analysis from 2020 to 2025, the researchers observed how these regimes shift year-over-year.

In winter, the climate regimes show high spatial persistence. The "Warm/Wet" coastal regime (Cluster 2) consistently stays near the Gulf, while the "Cool/Dry" inland regime (Cluster 1) remains in the north. This suggests that winter climate in the South Central region is dominated by large-scale, stable synoptic processes and fixed geographic influences like latitude and proximity to the ocean.

![winter-cluster-persistence](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13808v1/Figures/Cluster_winter_yearwise_20_25.png)
*{fig:winter-cluster-persistence}: The spatial footprint of winter climate regimes from 2020 to 2025, demonstrating high temporal stability and consistent regional boundaries.*

Summer presents a different story. The regimes are more fluid and show significant interannual reorganization. Because summer weather is often driven by localized convective storms rather than massive frontal systems, the precipitation-heavy clusters move more unpredictably. In some years, a hot-dry regime dominates large parts of Texas and Oklahoma, while in others, it shrinks. This indicates that summer climate is more sensitive to annual variability and local environmental forcing.

## Interpreting Joint Density and Hazard Implications

The value of the multivariate approach is most visible in the joint density contour plots. These plots show the "shape" of the relationship between temperature and precipitation. In winter, the clusters are primarily separated along the horizontal temperature axis. As we move from winter to summer, the clusters begin to overlap significantly in temperature but remain distinct in the "tail" of their precipitation distributions.

This transition from temperature-led variability to precipitation-led variability is a crucial insight for hazard modeling. For instance, an extreme rainfall event in a "Warm/Wet" summer regime has different environmental implications (in terms of evaporation rates and soil moisture) than the same rainfall in a cooler regime. By providing a method to classify these joint conditions, the researchers offer a more nuanced tool for regional climate assessment.

## Conclusion and Future Directions

The study demonstrates that multivariate functional methods provide a much richer description of regional climate than univariate approaches. By rejecting the assumption that temperature and precipitation can be treated in isolation, the authors reveal the asymmetric nature of seasonal variability in the South Central U.S.

The FMANOVA results provide a rigorous statistical foundation for state-level comparisons, while the GMM clustering offers an intuitive way to map and track climate regimes over time. The findings highlight that while winter patterns are geographically "locked" and predictable, summer patterns are highly dynamic.

Looking forward, the researchers suggest expanding this framework to include longer-term historical records to detect how these regimes might be shifting due to climate change. Additionally, incorporating spatial dependence directly into the functional models could further improve the accuracy of regional climate projections, helping communities better prepare for the compound hazards of the future.
