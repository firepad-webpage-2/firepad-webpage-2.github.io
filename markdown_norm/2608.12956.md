## Spatial Transmission and Control of Avian Influenza

Highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) poses a recurring threat to global food security and animal health. The virus, primarily carried by migratory wild birds, frequently spills over into domestic poultry populations, leading to massive economic losses and risks to public health. Managing these outbreaks requires a dual focus: implementing immediate control measures like culling and confinement, and determining the optimal timing for restocking farms once the epidemic subsides. This research presents a stochastic, spatially explicit metapopulation model designed to address these operational challenges, using a synthetic outbreak on the fictional "Jolly Island" as a case study.

![figure-1-spatial-distribution-of-hpai-outbreaks-across-jolly-island-categorized-by-poultry-species-chicken-and-duck](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12956v1/casesSpatial.png)

The study focuses on the complex transmission ecology of HPAI, which operates across multiple scales. Transmission is influenced by local farm density, regional movement of birds and equipment, and the persistence of the virus in the environment. By integrating these factors into a single mathematical framework, the authors provide a tool for evaluating the epidemiological and economic consequences of different management policies. The unique contribution of this work lies in its integration of post-epidemic recovery—specifically the probabilistic risk of "rebound" infections during restocking—with active outbreak control simulations.

## The SEIR Metapopulation Framework

The model utilizes a discrete-time, stochastic SEIR framework, where farms transition through four epidemiological states: Susceptible ($S$), Exposed ($E$), Infectious ($I$), and Removed ($R$). Rather than modeling every farm individually in a single pool, the researchers employed a metapopulation structure. In this setup, the island is divided into patches (counties), and farms within these patches are stratified by production class: Broiler-2 (commercial chickens), organic duck, and "Other" farms.

Transitions between states are governed by probabilities derived from a total transmission pressure $\lambda_{c,p}(t)$ for a farm in county $c$ and production class $p$ on day $t$. This pressure is the sum of four distinct transmission pathways:

$$\lambda_{c,p}(t) = \lambda^{\text{local}}_{c,p}(t) + \lambda^{\text{env}}_{c,p}(t) + \lambda^{\text{move}}_{c,p}(t) + \lambda^{\text{spatial}}_{c,p}(t)$$

1.  **Local Transmission:** Represents the risk from infectious farms within the same county and production class.
2.  **Environmental Transmission:** Accounts for indirect contact through contaminated surroundings. This is modeled via a county-level reservoir $Env_c(t)$ that decays over time but is replenished by shedding from infectious farms.
3.  **Movement-Mediated Transmission:** Captures the risk from the recorded daily movement of birds between counties.
4.  **Spatial Transmission:** Models the spread between neighboring counties using an exponential distance-decay kernel $K_{c,c'} = \exp(-d_{c,c'} / d_0)$, where $d_{c,c'}$ is the distance between county centroids and $d_0$ is the spatial decay parameter.

The environmental component is particularly important for HPAI, as the virus can survive for extended periods in water or soil. The model updates the environmental contamination level daily using the following relation:

$$Env_c(t+1) = Env_c(t)(1 - \delta_{\text{env}}) + \sum_p \kappa^{\text{env}}_p I_{c,p}(t)$$

where $\delta_{\text{env}}$ is the decay rate and $\kappa^{\text{env}}_p$ is the production-specific shedding rate. This allows the model to simulate how confinement measures, which reduce environmental exposure, impact the overall trajectory of the disease.

## Evaluating Control Strategies: Culling and Confinement

The primary tools for controlling HPAI outbreaks are culling (depopulation of birds) and confinement (keeping birds indoors to prevent environmental exposure). The study rigorously compares different implementations of these strategies to determine their effectiveness in reducing the total infectious burden.

Culling is categorized into two types: reactive and preventive. Reactive culling targets farms where the virus has been confirmed, while preventive culling targets nearby farms (within a $1\text{--}3 \text{ km}$ radius) to create a buffer zone. The simulation findings indicate that all recorded preventive culling measures combined reduced the mean cumulative infectious burden by $16.7\%$. Interestingly, the benefits were not uniform across species; chicken-targeted preventive culling was significantly more effective at reducing the regional burden than duck-targeted culling.

![figure-2-spatiotemporal-evolution-of-the-infection-burden-across-jolly-island-over-consecutive-five-week-periods](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12956v1/five_week_maps_all.png)

Confinement measures were also found to be highly sensitive to timing and scope. Delaying the start of confinement from late December to mid-February resulted in a $91.1\%$ increase in the peak number of infectious farms. Furthermore, the "Other" production class was identified as a major driver of the epidemic's persistence. When confinement was only applied to Broiler-2 and organic duck farms, the remaining exposure in the "Other" category allowed the epidemic to plateau rather than decline rapidly. This suggests that comprehensive confinement across all farm types is necessary to effectively suppress HPAI outbreaks.

## Managing Post-Outbreak Recovery and Restocking

A critical challenge for poultry producers is deciding when it is safe to restock farms after they have been culled. Restocking too early risks a "rebound," where the newly introduced susceptible birds are immediately infected by residual environmental contamination or lingering infectious cases, sparking a second wave of the epidemic.

The authors developed a probabilistic risk metric, $\hat{P}_{\text{rebound}}(t_r)$, to evaluate various restocking dates $t_r$. A "rebound" was defined as an event where the infectious farm count significantly increased following restocking. A safety threshold was set at $\hat{P}_{\text{rebound}}(t_r) \leq 0.20$, meaning there is an $80\%$ or greater chance of avoiding a resurgence.

A unique feature of this model is the "capacity-based" restocking formulation. Unlike simpler models that might add a fixed number of farms regardless of the current population, this formulation only adds a fraction $f_{\text{restock}}$ of the *available empty capacity* in each county. This ensures that the total number of farms never exceeds the baseline capacity of the region.

![figure-3-risk-of-epidemic-rebound-across-different-candidate-restocking-dates-highlighting-the-transition-from-risky-to-safe-periods](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12956v1/restocking_rebound_risk.png)

The analysis showed that restocking during the active phase of the epidemic is extremely hazardous. For example, restocking in mid-March with $f_{\text{restock}} = 0.20$ resulted in a rebound probability of $0.533$, far above the safety limit. The simulation identified 24 May 2026 as the first "safe" date, where the rebound probability dropped to $0.180$. By late June, the risk reached zero ($0.000$), indicating the epidemic had fully resolved.

## Spatial and Temporal Dynamics of the Burden

The epidemic burden was not distributed evenly across Jolly Island. Geographical mapping revealed that Berks County suffered the highest mean cumulative burden, followed by Indiana and Susquehanna. These areas acted as the epicenter of the outbreak, with the infection intensifying and expanding in the early months of 2026 before contracting.

![figure-4-spatial-burden-specifically-for-the-broiler-2-production-class-showing-a-concentrated-peak-and-decline](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12956v1/five_week_maps_B2.png)

By breaking down the burden by production class, the researchers observed that Broiler-2 farms experienced a sharp peak and a steady decline, while the "Other" class maintained a lower but more persistent level of infection throughout the spring. Organic duck farms contributed the least to the overall burden. These insights are vital for resource allocation, suggesting that veterinary services and surveillance efforts should be prioritized in high-burden counties like Berks.

## Conclusion and Operational Significance

This research demonstrates that stochastic metapopulation models are powerful tools for evaluating the multi-faceted nature of HPAI management. By accounting for spatial proximity, movement data, and environmental persistence, the model moves beyond simple forecasts to provide actionable risk assessments.

The study’s findings emphasize that the timing of interventions is just as important as their nature. Early confinement and targeted preventive culling are essential for blunting the epidemic peak. Perhaps more importantly, the work provides a rigorous framework for post-outbreak recovery. The use of rebound probabilities and capacity-based restocking allows decision-makers to move away from guesswork and toward evidence-based strategies for rebuilding the poultry industry after a crisis.

While the model was applied to the synthetic Jolly Island, the underlying principles—integrating transmission pathways with logistical control constraints—are directly applicable to real-world HPAI outbreaks. Future work could further refine these models by incorporating more granular data on biosecurity practices and economic trade-offs, helping to create even more robust decision-support tools for global animal health.
