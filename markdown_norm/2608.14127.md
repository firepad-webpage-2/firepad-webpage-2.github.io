## Overview of FastJM

In clinical research and population health studies, researchers often collect two distinct types of data from the same participant: longitudinal biomarkers and time-to-event outcomes. Longitudinal biomarkers are measurements taken repeatedly over time, such as blood glucose levels or blood pressure, while time-to-event outcomes track how long it takes for a specific event to occur, such as disease progression or mortality. Analyzing these two data types in isolation can lead to biased results. For instance, if a patient dies (an event), their longitudinal measurements stop; this is known as informative dropout.

Joint modeling provides a statistical framework to analyze these processes simultaneously by linking them through a shared latent structure, typically subject-specific random effects. However, joint models are computationally expensive, particularly as the number of participants, biomarkers, or event types increases. The R package `FastJM` addresses these scalability issues by implementing efficient frequentist estimation methods for semiparametric joint models. It specifically targets scenarios common in modern biomedical research, such as multiple biomarkers, competing risks, and heterogeneous within-subject variability.

## The Challenge of Scalability in Joint Modeling

As biomedical datasets grow—driven by electronic health records (EHRs) and large-scale biobanks—standard joint modeling implementations encounter significant bottlenecks. There are two primary sources of computational burden:

1.  **Numerical Integration:** Estimating joint models requires integrating over random effects (variables that represent individual-level deviations from the population average). The complexity of this integration grows exponentially with the number of random effects. For example, modeling three biomarkers, each with a random intercept and slope, requires integration in a six-dimensional space. Traditional methods like Gauss-Hermite quadrature become impractical in such high dimensions.
2.  **Risk-Set Calculations:** In semiparametric survival models, the baseline hazard is typically unspecified. Estimating this hazard involves summing across all individuals currently "at risk" for an event at every time an event occurs. For a dataset with $n$ participants, a direct implementation leads to $O(n^2)$ computational complexity. Calculating standard errors using profile likelihood can further escalate this to $O(n^3)$.

`FastJM` overcomes these limitations by utilizing customized linear-scan algorithms that reduce the complexity of baseline hazard and standard error calculations to $O(n)$. It also employs specific approximation techniques for the integration step, such as normal approximations for high-dimensional random effects.

## Core Methodology and Model Classes

`FastJM` implements three distinct classes of joint models through three primary functions: `jmcs`, `mvjmcs`, and `JMMLSM`. All three share a common structure where the longitudinal and survival processes are assumed to be independent once conditioned on the subject-specific random effects.

### Single Biomarker with Competing Risks

The `jmcs` function handles the case of a single longitudinal biomarker and multiple possible event types (competing risks). For a participant $i$, the longitudinal measurements $Y_i(t)$ at visit times $t_{ij}$ are modeled using a linear mixed-effects model:

$$
Y_i(t) = X_i^\top(t)\beta + Z_i^\top(t)b_i + \sigma\epsilon_i(t)
$$

Here, $\beta$ represents fixed effects, $b_i$ represents subject-specific random effects (usually $b_i \sim N(0, \Sigma)$), and $\sigma^2$ is the variance of the measurement error. The survival submodel for the $k$-th event type among $K$ total types is defined by a cause-specific hazard:

$$
\lambda_{ik}(t | W_i, b_i) = \lambda_{0k}(t) \exp(W_i^\top\gamma_k + b_i^\top\alpha_k)
$$

In this equation, $\lambda_{0k}(t)$ is the unspecified baseline hazard for cause $k$, $\gamma_k$ are coefficients for baseline covariates $W_i$, and $\alpha_k$ is the association parameter that links the random effects from the longitudinal process to the risk of that specific event.

### Multiple Longitudinal Biomarkers

When a disease is monitored by several correlated biomarkers, the `mvjmcs` function is used. It extends the previous model to $G$ biomarkers. Each biomarker $g$ follows its own trajectory:

$$
Y_{ig}(t) = X_{ig}^\top(t)\beta_g + Z_{ig}^\top(t)b_{ig} + \sigma_g\epsilon_{ig}(t)
$$

The random effects for all biomarkers are concatenated into a joint vector $b_i = (b_{i1}^\top, \dots, b_{iG}^\top)^\top \sim N(0, \Sigma)$, which allows the model to capture how the biomarkers vary together. The hazard for cause $k$ then becomes:

$$
\lambda_{ik}(t | W_i, b_i) = \lambda_{0k}(t) \exp(W_i^\top\gamma_k + \sum_{g=1}^G b_{ig}^\top\alpha_{gk})
$$

### Heterogeneous Within-Subject Variability

Traditionally, joint models assume that the measurement error $\sigma^2$ is the same for everyone (homogeneous). However, the amount of fluctuation in a biomarker can itself be a predictor of risk. The `JMMLSM` function implements a "location-scale" joint model where the variance is allowed to vary between individuals:

$$
\log\{\sigma_i^2(t)\} = U_i^\top(t)\tau + \omega_i
$$

In this model, $\omega_i$ is a random effect for the variability process. The risk of an event is then linked to both the level of the biomarker and its variability:

$$
\lambda_{ik}(t | W_i, \theta_i) = \lambda_{0k}(t) \exp(W_i^\top\gamma_k + b_i^\top\alpha_k + \omega_i\nu_k)
$$

where $\theta_i = (b_i^\top, \omega_i)^\top \sim N(0, \Sigma_\theta)$ and $\nu_k$ quantifies how the within-subject variability affects the risk of event $k$.

## Computational Efficiency and the EM Algorithm

`FastJM` uses the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm for maximum likelihood estimation. In the E-step, the package calculates the expected values of the random effects given the observed data. In the M-step, it updates the model parameters to maximize this expectation.

To maintain speed, the package employs several strategies:
*   **Linear-Scan Algorithms:** By sorting participants by event time and using cumulative sums, `FastJM` avoids the $O(n^2)$ double loops typically required to update the nonparametric baseline hazards $\lambda_{0k}(t)$.
*   **Approximations for High Dimensions:** For multivariate models where $b_i$ has high dimensions, `FastJM` uses a normal approximation for the posterior distribution $f(\vartheta_i | Y_i, T_i, D_i; \Psi^{(m)})$ of the random effects. This avoids the "curse of dimensionality" associated with grid-based numerical integration.
*   **Profile Likelihood:** The variance-covariance matrix of the parameters is computed using a profile-likelihood approach, which is also optimized using linear-scan techniques to keep the complexity at $O(n)$.

## Integrated Workflow and Prediction

Beyond estimation, `FastJM` provides a complete toolkit for post-estimation analysis and diagnostic checking. 

### Diagnostics and Visualization
The package includes methods to visualize data and check model fit. For example, `timeplot()` allows researchers to see longitudinal trajectories and cumulative incidence rates before fitting a model. After fitting, `plot()` generates diagnostic plots such as residuals vs. fitted values and Q-Q plots to check for normality of residuals.

### Dynamic Prediction
A key application of joint modeling is dynamic prediction. Given a new patient $i^*$ who has been followed up to a landmark time $s$, we want to estimate the probability that they will experience event $k$ within a future window $u > s$. The function `survfitJM()` calculates this cumulative incidence probability $P_{i^*k}(u, s | \Psi)$, updating the prediction as more longitudinal data $Y_i$ becomes available for the patient.

*{fig:dynamic-prediction}: Dynamic prediction of cumulative incidence for a single subject at two different landmark times. As the patient provides more biomarker measurements, the risk prediction is updated.*

### Landmark Multivariate Joint Modeling
`FastJM` also introduces a hybrid approach called landmark multivariate joint modeling. In standard joint models, the association between the biomarker and the hazard is time-dependent, which can be slow for large numbers of biomarkers. The landmarking extension in `mvjmcs()` evaluates the latent biomarker characteristics $A_{ig}(s)$ (like the current latent value $m_{ig}(s)$ or the random effect $Z_{ig}^\top(s)b_{ig}$) at a fixed landmark time $s$ for all individuals still at risk $R(s) = \{i : T_i > s\}$. This simplification maintains the measurement-error correction benefits of joint models while speeding up the prediction process for large-scale datasets.

## Significance and Practical Utility

The development of `FastJM` makes sophisticated joint modeling accessible for datasets where it was previously computationally prohibitive. By providing a frequentist framework that is both flexible and fast, it allows researchers to:
1.  **Analyze Real-World Data:** Process large cohorts from EHRs and biobanks without waiting days for model convergence.
2.  **Capture Nuance:** Explore associations between the variability of a biomarker and clinical outcomes, a factor often ignored in simpler models.
3.  **Perform Rigorous Evaluation:** Use cross-validation and time-dependent accuracy metrics (like AUC and Brier score) to ensure that the models generalize well to new data.

The package's unified interface ensures that moving from a simple single-biomarker model to a complex multivariate or heterogeneous variance model requires minimal changes to the code, facilitating a more thorough exploration of biomedical data.
