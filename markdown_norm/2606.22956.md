## Introduction to Centralized Risk Sharing

Centralized risk sharing describes a market structure where multiple agents, typically policyholders, transfer their individual risks to a single central agent, such as an insurance or reinsurance company. Unlike bilateral insurance, where a single insurer deals with a single policyholder, the centralized model involves a pool of participants whose decisions collectively affect the insurer's total liability. This model is increasingly relevant for modern insurance schemes, including peer-to-peer (P2P) platforms and mutual insurance pools, where the stability of the entire system depends on how risks and costs are distributed among all participants.

A critical challenge in these multilateral systems is ensuring that the final risk allocation is not only efficient but also representative of all agents' interests. In economics, efficiency is often defined by Pareto optimality: a state where no agent can be made better off without making at least one other agent worse off. However, in a centralized setting with many policyholders, the standard definition of Pareto optimality can lead to "imbalanced" outcomes. For example, a solution might be Pareto optimal even if it entirely ignores the preferences of one specific policyholder while maximizing the welfare of others. This paper introduces the concept of "inclusive and fair Pareto optimality" to address these representation issues, ensuring that every participant is considered fairly in the decision-making process.

## The Mathematical Framework of Risk Sharing

The paper formalizes a centralized risk-sharing problem involving $n+1$ agents: $n$ policyholders and $1$ central insurer. Each policyholder $i$ (where $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$) faces an initial random risk $X_i$. To mitigate this, they choose an indemnity function $I_i(X_i)$, which represents the amount of risk they transfer to the central insurer. This function must belong to a set of admissible decisions $\mathcal{I}_0$, typically constrained by $0 \leq I(x) \leq x$ and $I(0)=0$.

A distinguishing feature of this work is the treatment of **endogenous pricing**. In many models, the premium paid for insurance is treated as a separate, independent variable. Here, the premium $\Pi_i$ for each policyholder $i$ is calculated directly from the chosen indemnity $I_i(X_i)$ using a pricing principle. This creates a coupled system where the final risk position of each agent depends on both the risk transfer and the resulting cost.

The post-transfer risk positions for the policyholders are defined as:
$$
Y_i = X_i - I_i(X_i) + \Pi_i(I_i(X_i))
$$
For the central insurer (agent $n+1$), the total risk is the sum of all indemnities paid minus the total premiums received:
$$
Y_{n+1} = \sum_{i=1}^n I_i(X_i) - \sum_{i=1}^n \Pi_i(I_i(X_i))
$$
Each agent $i$ evaluates their risk $Y_i$ using a risk measure $\rho_i(Y_i)$. The goal is to find a vector of ceding decisions $I = (I_1, \dots, I_n) \in \mathcal{I}^n \subseteq \mathcal{I}_0^n$ that is Pareto optimal. Under the technical assumptions $(\dagger)$—which include the convexity of risk measures $\rho_i$, the semi-linearity of premiums $\Pi_i$, and the convexity of the decision set—the overall problem remains mathematically tractable.

## Limitations of Classical Pareto Optimality

While Pareto optimality is a fundamental criterion for economic efficiency, its standard interpretations often fail to guarantee a "fair" representation of all agents in a multilateral setting ($n \ge 2$). The most common method to find Pareto optimal solutions is through **weighted-sum scalarization**, where one minimizes a weighted average of all agents' risk measures: $\sum_{i \in N_n} \lambda_i \rho_i(Y_i)$ for some weight vector $\lambda \in \Delta^n[0,1]$.

However, this approach has significant drawbacks in a centralized context:
1.  **Zero Weights**: In convex optimization, it is possible for some weights $\lambda_i$ to be zero. If a policyholder's weight is zero, their risk preferences are essentially deleted from the optimization, meaning they have no "voice" in determining the optimal indemnity.
2.  **Aggregation Obscures Individuals**: When multiple agents' risks are aggregated into a single group objective, a reduction in one agent's risk can be offset by an increase in another's without changing the group total. This makes it difficult to ensure that each individual is being treated fairly within the group.
3.  **Implicit Nature**: Other methods, like the $\epsilon$-constraint method (which minimizes one agent's risk while keeping others' below a certain threshold), require knowing the target values in advance. This makes the optimization an implicit verification of optimality rather than an explicit constructive process for finding balanced solutions.

These issues motivate a refinement that explicitly requires all agents to be represented in the optimization sequence, preventing any participant from being ignored.

## Balanced Sequential Optimization

To resolve the representation problem, the paper proposes using **sequential optimization**. Instead of optimizing all agents simultaneously in one weighted sum, the process is broken down into stages. In each stage, a subset of agents (or their weighted sum) is prioritized, and the resulting set of optimal solutions serves as the feasible region for the next stage.

The authors formalize this using "sequential matrices" $A_n$. A matrix $\Lambda \in A_n$ is an $(n+1) \times (n+1)$ matrix where each non-zero column represents the weights for an optimization stage. A sequence of optimizations is defined as $K^{*(j)}(\Lambda[\cdot,1:j])$, where stage $j$ minimizes the weighted sum using weights $\Lambda[\cdot, j]$ over the optimal solutions of the previous $j-1$ stages.

A sequential process is considered **balanced** if:
*   Each agent $i$ is assigned a non-zero weight exactly once across all stages.
*   This ensures "inclusivity," as no agent is left out (all agents appear in some stage).
*   This ensures "fairness of representation," as no agent's objective is repeated or over-emphasized relative to others in the sequential hierarchy.

By requiring this specific structure, the researchers define a subset of Pareto optimal solutions that are inherently "well-behaved" regarding agent participation.

## Inclusive and Fair Pareto Optimality

Building on the sequential approach, the paper introduces the formal definition of **inclusive and fair Pareto optimality**. This definition relies on three components:

1.  **Convex-group risk measure**: For a group of agents $G \subseteq N_n$, the group risk is $\rho(Y; \lambda_G) = \sum_{i \in G} \lambda_{G,i} \rho_i(Y_i)$.
2.  **Ordered set partition**: The $n+1$ agents are divided into a sequence of disjoint groups $P = (N_1, \dots, N_m)$.
3.  **Recursive Pareto Optimality**: A decision $I^*$ is inclusive and fair Pareto optimal if it minimizes the risk for the first group $N_1$, and then, for every subsequent group $N_l$ ($l=2, \dots, m$), it is Pareto optimal between the accumulated prior agents ($N_1 \cup \dots \cup N_{l-1}$) and the new group $N_l$.

This recursive structure ensures that at every step, the new group of agents is brought into the decision process without overriding the optimality already achieved for previous participants. The central result of the paper is the proof of equivalence: a decision vector is inclusive and fair Pareto optimal if and only if it can be obtained through a balanced sequential optimization process.

Crucially, this equivalence (Theorems 4.1 and 4.2) does not strictly require the convexity assumptions $(\dagger)$ for the mapping from set partitions to sequential matrices, though these assumptions are helpful for the broader existence of Pareto optima. This makes the framework robust across different types of risk measures and premium principles.

## The Hierarchy of Efficiency

The paper places this new concept within the existing hierarchy of multi-objective optimization. In the multilateral case, the relationship between these efficiency notions is as follows:

*   **Properly Efficient Solutions**: These are Pareto optimal solutions where the trade-offs between agents are bounded (i.e., a small gain for one agent doesn't require an infinitely large loss for another).
*   **Inclusive and Fair Pareto Optimal Solutions**: These are the solutions identified by this work, where every agent is represented exactly once in a balanced sequence.
*   **Classical Pareto Optimal Solutions**: The broadest set, including solutions where some agents might have zero influence (zero weights).

The study shows that every inclusive and fair solution is a classical Pareto optimal solution. Furthermore, every properly efficient solution (in the sense of Geoffrion) is also an inclusive and fair solution. This means that by focusing on inclusive and fair optimality, researchers and practitioners can identify a subset of efficient solutions that are both representative and avoid the extreme "imbalanced" trade-offs often found at the edges of the Pareto frontier.

## Significance for Modern Insurance

The implications of this work are particularly significant for the design of fair risk-sharing mechanisms. In traditional insurance, the power dynamic is often tilted towards the insurer. However, in centralized risk pools or P2P insurance platforms, policyholders often expect a more equitable and transparent allocation of risks and costs.

By applying the principles of inclusive and fair Pareto optimality, platform designers can:
*   **Ensure Participation**: Guarantee that every participant's risk preferences (modeled by $\rho_i$) are reflected in the final contract design.
*   **Resolve Conflicts**: Provide a structured sequence for prioritization. For example, a pool might prioritize the stability of the central insurer first ($N_1 = \{n+1\}$), and then optimize the welfare of different subgroups of policyholders.
*   **Improve Transparency**: The use of sequential matrices and set partitions provides a clear "roadmap" of how decisions are made, which can be audited for fairness.

In conclusion, this research moves beyond simple aggregate efficiency to consider the procedural fairness of multilateral risk sharing. By characterizing inclusive and fair Pareto optimality through balanced sequential optimization, the authors provide a rigorous foundation for building insurance systems that are both efficient and equitably representative of all participants. Future extensions of this work may include exploring how these optimal solutions change when agents behave strategically (as in Stackelberg games) or when the number of agents becomes very large.
