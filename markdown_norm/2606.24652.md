## Introduction to High-Dimensional Factor Models

In modern economics and finance, researchers often work with datasets where the number of variables $p$ (e.g., hundreds of stock returns or macroeconomic indicators) is much larger than the number of observations $n$ (the sample size). Despite this high dimensionality, these variables frequently move together, driven by a few underlying latent factors. The standard framework to describe this behavior is the factor model, which decomposes an observation $Y_i$ into a common component and an idiosyncratic error:

$$
Y_i = B\eta_i + \epsilon_i
$$

where $B$ is a $p \times k$ matrix of factor loadings, $\eta_i \sim N(0, I_k)$ represents the latent factors, and $\epsilon_i \sim N(0, \Sigma_e)$ is the idiosyncratic error. This implies that the population covariance matrix $\Sigma_0$ can be represented as $\Sigma_0 = BB^\top + \Sigma_e$.

Traditionally, "strict" factor models assume that error terms are uncorrelated (i.e., $\Sigma_e$ is diagonal). However, real-world data often exhibit weak dependencies even after common factors are removed. "Approximate" factor models (AFM) allow for this by letting $\Sigma_e$ have non-zero off-diagonal elements, provided the dependence remains "weak" relative to the signal from the factors. 

Estimation in high-dimensional AFM settings is typically performed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). However, as $p/n$ grows, the sample covariance matrix becomes a poor estimator of $\Sigma_0$, leading to biased estimates of the underlying factor structure. While Bayesian methods offer a way to incorporate prior knowledge and quantify uncertainty, existing Bayesian factor models often struggle with "rotational ambiguity"—the fact that $B$ and $BR$ (for any orthogonal matrix $R$) yield the same covariance matrix—and the specific theoretical requirements of the approximate factor structure.

## Addressing Rotational Ambiguity via Eigenstructure

A core challenge in factor analysis is that the loading matrix $B$ is not uniquely identifiable without arbitrary constraints. To circumvent this, the researchers focus on the eigenstructure of the common component $BB^\top$. By decomposing the common component as $BB^\top = U\Lambda U^\top$, where $U$ is a $p \times k$ matrix with orthonormal columns and $\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k)$ contains the eigenvalues, the model targets parameters that are invariant to rotation. 

This approach aligns with the "spiked covariance" framework. In this view, the $k$ largest eigenvalues (the spikes) are assumed to grow with $p$, representing the strength of the common factors, while the remaining eigenvalues (the noise) remain bounded. By placing priors directly on $U$ and $\Lambda$, the Bayesian AFM enables direct inference on the factor subspace and factor strengths without requiring the identification of individual loadings in $B$.

## Bayesian Framework and Prior Specifications

The methodology specifies a prior distribution on the trio $(U, \Lambda, \Sigma_e)$ to reflect the structural assumptions of high-dimensional approximate factor models.

**The Factor Subspace ($U$):** 
A uniform prior is placed on the Stiefel manifold $V_{p,k}$, the set of all $p \times k$ matrices with orthonormal columns. This prior ensures that the model is rotationally invariant, meaning no specific direction in the factor subspace is favored a priori.

**The Factor Strengths ($\Lambda$):** 
The eigenvalues $\lambda_1 > \dots > \lambda_k > 0$ represent the variance explained by each factor. The researchers use independent inverse-gamma priors for each $\lambda_i$. Crucially, the hyperparameters are set adaptively based on the sample eigenvalues of the data:

$$
a_i = \frac{nt}{2(\hat{\lambda}_i - t)} + 2
$$

where $\hat{\lambda}_i$ is the $i$-th sample eigenvalue. This choice facilitates shrinkage, helping the model distinguish between the "spiked" eigenvalues of the common component and the smaller eigenvalues of the error process.

**The Idiosyncratic Covariance ($\Sigma_e$):** 
To maintain the "approximate" nature of the model, $\Sigma_e$ is assigned an inverse-Wishart prior, but with a critical modification: it is truncated to ensure its eigenvalues remain within specific bounds $b_0 I_p \preceq \Sigma_e \preceq b_1 I_p$. This truncation prevents the idiosyncratic noise from becoming too large and overwhelming the factor signal, a necessary condition for the factors to be identifiable in high dimensions.

## Computational Strategy: The Gibbs Sampler

The posterior distribution is complex and high-dimensional, requiring a specialized Gibbs sampler for computation. The algorithm iterates through several key steps:

1.  **Latent Factors:** Given the current parameters, the latent factors are sampled using standard multivariate normal updates.
2.  **Factor Strengths:** The diagonal elements of $\Lambda$ are updated using inverse-gamma distributions.
3.  **Factor Subspace ($U$):** Sampling from the Stiefel manifold is non-trivial. The algorithm employs a technique involving random Givens rotations. By iteratively rotating pairs of columns in the orthogonal matrix, the sampler explores the space of factor subspaces effectively while maintaining the orthonormality constraint.
4.  **Error Covariance ($\Sigma_e$):** Because of the eigenvalue truncation, $\Sigma_e$ is sampled through its own spectral decomposition. The eigenvectors and eigenvalues of $\Sigma_e$ are updated using slice sampling to respect the bounds $b_0$ and $b_1$.

## Theoretical Consistency in High Dimensions

A major contribution of this work is establishing that the Bayesian posterior actually converges to the true population values as the data dimensions grow. The paper provides theoretical guarantees for the convergence rates of the estimated eigenstructure under the regime where $p/n \to \infty$.

For the factor strengths, Theorem 4.2 states that the posterior expectation $E[\lambda_{(i)} | Y]$ converges to the true eigenvalue $\lambda_{0,i}$ at a rate:

$$
O(\lambda_{0,i}^{-1}\sqrt{p/n} + \beta_i)
$$

where $\beta_i$ is a smaller-order term (approximately $n^{-1/2+\delta}$). 

For the factor directions, Theorem 4.3 establishes that the posterior distribution of the eigenvectors $\xi_{(j)}$ concentrates around the true directions $\xi_{0,j}$. The error, measured by the squared sine of the angle between the estimated and true eigenvectors, $E[1 - |\xi_{0,j}^\top \xi_{(j)}|^2 | Y]$, converges at a rate of:

$$
O(p/(n\lambda_{0,j}) + \zeta_j)
$$

These rates are significant because they match the optimal minimax rates found in frequentist literature for spiked covariance models, demonstrating that the Bayesian AFM is theoretically robust in high-dimensional settings.

## Empirical Performance and Applications

The paper validates the AFM through simulations and real-world datasets, illustrating its advantages over traditional PCA and other high-dimensional covariance estimators like S-POET or Penalized Maximum Likelihood (PML).

### S&P 500 Factor Analysis
When applied to S&P 500 returns, the AFM provides a more structured view of market dynamics. While PCA often produces diffuse loadings, the AFM identifies a clear market-wide factor and higher-order factors that concentrate on specific sectors like Utilities or Energy (as shown in {fig:afm-factor-loadings}). This is visually contrasted with PCA in the paper's comparison plots.

### Macroeconomic Forecasting (FAVAR)
The researchers integrated the AFM into a Factor-Augmented Vector Autoregression (FAVAR) framework to forecast returns for 12 industry portfolios. By extracting factors from a large panel of macroeconomic variables (FRED-MD), the AFM-FAVAR model achieved lower Root Mean Squared Errors (RMSE) than baseline VAR models and performed competitively against other factor-based methods.

### Korean Macroeconomic Dynamics
In an application to Korean economic data, the model identified two primary factors. Factor 2 was strongly linked to interest rates (KORIBOR, bond yields), while Factor 1 captured broader domestic conditions, including labor and construction indices. The latent paths of these factors ({fig:latent-factor-paths}) reveal significant economic disturbances, particularly the volatility observed during the 2022-2023 period.

## Significance and Future Impact

The Bayesian estimation of the eigenstructure in approximate factor models represents a shift toward more interpretable and theoretically grounded high-dimensional analysis. By focusing on rotation-invariant parameters $(U, \Lambda)$ rather than the ambiguous loading matrix $B$, the authors provide a framework that is both computationally efficient and statistically consistent.

The unique combination of a Bayesian approach with the spiked covariance framework allows for natural uncertainty quantification—something frequently missing in PCA-based methods—while maintaining the flexibility required for "approximate" error structures. This work lays the foundation for future extensions into dynamic factor models, where the subspace $U$ might evolve over time, potentially offering even deeper insights into the changing correlations within complex economic systems.
