## Understanding the Learning Assistant Model and Observational Needs

The Learning Assistant (LA) model has become a staple in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) education, particularly in large enrollment courses at universities. Under this model, undergraduate students who have previously succeeded in a course are hired as peer educators to facilitate group work and encourage student engagement. Research indicates that the presence of LAs can lead to improved student learning outcomes, increased motivation, and higher graduation rates. However, despite these broad successes, the way the LA model is implemented varies significantly across different institutions and even within different departments at the same institution.

![the-original-riot-interface-designed-for-a-single-instructor](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13740v1/riotinterface.png)
*{fig:the-original-riot-interface-designed-for-a-single-instructor}: The original RIOT interface designed for characterizing the behaviors of a single instructor. It includes categories for talking at students, dialoguing, observing, and not interacting.*

To understand how these implementation differences impact the classroom, researchers use observational protocols. These are systematic methods for recording exactly what happens during a class period. One such tool is the Real-time Instructor Observational Tool (RIOT), which was originally developed at UC Davis. The RIOT allows an observer to track an instructor's behavior in real-time, categorizing their actions into specific types like "explaining content," "listening to a student's question," or "passively observing." This provides a quantitative breakdown of how an instructor spends their time.

While the original RIOT is effective for single-instructor classrooms, it faces a significant challenge in LA-supported environments. In these settings, the instructional team consists of multiple people—usually one primary faculty member and several LAs. The original RIOT lacked the ability to document the interactions between these team members. For instance, if an LA asks the instructor a question, or if the instructor gives logistical directions to the LAs, the original tool had no way to categorize these moments. To address this limitation, researchers at San José State University developed the RIOT 2, a modified version of the protocol designed specifically for classrooms with multiple instructional team members.

## The Evolution to RIOT 2: Addressing Multi-Member Dynamics

The motivation for creating RIOT 2 stemmed from the observation that the "instructional team" is not a monolith; rather, it is a dynamic group of individuals with different roles and responsibilities. The researchers identified that the original RIOT could not capture instances where an LA was observing an instructor lecture, asking an instructor for clarification, or when an instructor was coordinating with LAs. These interactions are critical because they define the partnership between faculty and LAs, which in turn influences student learning.

The development of RIOT 2 involved an iterative process. Observers initially used the original RIOT to monitor LA-supported classes but kept detailed field notes whenever they encountered a behavior that didn't fit the existing categories. These gaps almost always involved interactions within the instructional team. Consequently, the team re-implemented the RIOT software using a modern web-based framework (Python on Google Cloud Platform) to allow for the flexible addition of new features.

The primary structural change in RIOT 2 is the addition of "Instructional Team" (INST) columns. In the original interface, behaviors were categorized by whom the instructor was interacting with: the whole class (WC), an individual student (Ind), or a specific group (e.g., $G_1, \dots, G_6$). The RIOT 2 adds the INST column to both the small-group and whole-class discussion sections. This allows an observer to record when the person being observed—whether they are an LA or a faculty member—is interacting with another member of the instructional team.

![the-riot-2-interface-with-newly-added-inst-columns](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13740v1/riot2interface2.png)
*{fig:the-riot-2-interface-with-newly-added-inst-columns}: The RIOT 2 interface featuring the added "INST" columns, which allow observers to categorize interactions between members of the instructional team.*

In addition to the new columns, the RIOT 2 categorized "Chatting" as its own row rather than grouping it under "Not Interacting." This change reflects the pedagogical value of informal chatting between LAs and students, which can help build rapport and a sense of community in the classroom. When visualizing the data, interactions within the instructional team are typically aggregated into a single color (purple) due to their relatively small duration compared to student interactions, yet their presence provides vital context for how the team functions.

## Implementation and Behavioral Characterization

The RIOT 2 tracks behaviors across four main types: Talking at students, Shared Dialogue, Observing, and Not Interacting. Within these types, there are specific sub-behaviors. For example, "Talking at students" includes "clarifying instructions" and "explaining content." "Shared Dialogue" includes "listening to questions," "closed dialogue" (questions with one correct answer), and "open-ended dialogue" (exploratory questions).

When using the tool, an observer focuses on one individual (the observee) at a time. If the class has one instructor and three LAs, four observers might be needed to capture the entire team's activity simultaneously, or a single observer might focus on different individuals across different class sessions. The tool records the duration of each behavior, allowing for a total time calculation:
$$T_{total} = \sum t_i$$
where $t_i$ is the time spent on a specific interaction $i$. This quantitative approach allows researchers to see not just *what* happened, but for *how long* it happened.

The tool’s interface is designed for rapid entry. For instance, if an LA is watching an instructor give a presentation, the observer selects the "Presentation" button under the "INST" column. If the LA then moves to help a specific student group $G_i$, the observer clicks the appropriate button for that group, such as "Open ended dialogue." This real-time tracking generates a timeline that shows the flow of the class and the distribution of effort.

## Comparative Insights: LA vs. Faculty Roles

The utility of RIOT 2 is best demonstrated through the preliminary data collected by the research team. One significant application is the simultaneous comparison of an LA and a faculty member in the same classroom environment.

![comparison-of-instructor-and-la-behaviors-during-small-group-work](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13740v1/lafacultycomparison.png)
*{fig:comparison-of-instructor-and-la-behaviors-during-small-group-work}: A timeline comparison of an instructor (top) and an LA (bottom) during a 30-minute segment of small-group work. The instructor spent more time clarifying instructions and observing, while the LA spent more time dialoguing and explaining content.*

In one case study involving a physics class engaged in small-group work, the RIOT 2 data revealed distinct behavioral patterns for different team members. The primary instructor spent a majority of the time "clarifying instructions" and "passively observing." This suggests the instructor was acting in a managerial or oversight role, ensuring the students understood the task and monitoring the overall room environment.

In contrast, the LA in the same class spent significantly more time on "explaining content" and engaging in "open and closed dialogue" with students. This indicates that while the instructor managed the logistical flow, the LA was the primary facilitator of conceptual understanding at the group level. The data showed that both the instructor and the LA were highly mobile, interacting with all six student groups ($G_1$ through $G_6$) within the 30-minute observation window. This specific characterization is only possible because RIOT 2 can distinguish between the different ways two educators might approach the same group of students.

## Adapting to Different Classroom Structures

Another strength of the RIOT 2 is its ability to highlight how classroom structure influences behavior. The researchers compared the behaviors of LAs in two different settings: an extended small-group session and an interactive lecture.

In the interactive lecture setting, the timeline shows that the LA spent a large portion of time in the "INST" category, specifically listening to the instructor lecture to the whole class. However, these lecture periods were interspersed with short, high-intensity bursts of interaction with small groups.

![la-behavior-in-an-interactive-lecture-setting](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13740v1/lainteractivelecture.png)
*{fig:la-behavior-in-an-interactive-lecture-setting}: A 70-minute observation of an LA in an interactive lecture. The large blocks of blue in the "Instructor" row show the LA listening to the instructor, while the vertical stripes show brief interactions with students during active learning segments.*

Despite the differences in structure, a common theme emerged: LAs in both settings prioritized dialogue. In the interactive lecture, almost all of the LA's active speaking time was spent in dialogue rather than just "talking at" students. This suggests that the pedagogical training LAs receive—which typically emphasizes asking questions and facilitating discussion—is being successfully translated into classroom practice, even when the time available for interaction is limited.

## Broader Significance and Research Potential

The development of RIOT 2 provides a more nuanced way to study the effectiveness of the LA model. By capturing the interactions within the instructional team, researchers can begin to answer questions about how different partnership styles affect the classroom experience. For example, some faculty treat LAs as "co-instructors" with significant autonomy, while others treat them as "informants" who mainly provide feedback on student progress. RIOT 2 can quantify whether these different power dynamics result in different observable behaviors in the classroom.

Beyond research, RIOT 2 serves as a powerful tool for professional development. Instructors and LAs can use the generated timelines to reflect on their own teaching. An instructor might believe they are facilitating active dialogue but find, upon reviewing RIOT 2 data, that they are actually spending most of their time "talking at" students. This objective data can be a catalyst for pedagogical change.

Furthermore, RIOT 2 offers practical advantages over traditional video analysis. Video analysis is incredibly time-consuming and often requires complex ethical approvals because it records identifiable student data. Because RIOT 2 focuses strictly on the behavior of the educators and uses a real-time coding system, it is much faster to implement and often bypasses the need for individual student consent, making it a more scalable solution for large-scale educational studies.

In summary, RIOT 2 addresses the complexities of modern, multi-instructor STEM classrooms. By providing a structured way to record interactions within instructional teams, it opens new avenues for understanding how collaborative teaching can best support student success.
