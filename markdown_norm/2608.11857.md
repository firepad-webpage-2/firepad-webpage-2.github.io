## Grid-Forming Control in the Modern Power System

As the global energy landscape transitions from synchronous machine-based generation to converter-interfaced renewable energy sources, the requirements for grid stability have become increasingly stringent. Traditional grid-following converters rely on the existence of a stable grid voltage to synchronize and inject power. However, as the penetration of renewables increases, the grid becomes "weak," meaning its voltage and frequency are more susceptible to disturbances. This has led to the adoption of grid-forming (GFM) control, where the converter acts as a controlled voltage source behind a virtual or physical impedance, effectively contributing to the grid's strength and stability.

A significant challenge for GFM converters is operation under unbalanced grid conditions, such as single-phase-to-ground faults. In such scenarios, the grid voltage contains both positive-sequence components (rotating in the normal direction) and negative-sequence components (rotating in the opposite direction). Standard GFM controllers designed for balanced conditions may fail to maintain a stable voltage or may inadvertently inject harmonic currents. Furthermore, many existing solutions require direct AC-side voltage measurements, which adds cost, increases the number of sensors, and complicates the control architecture. The research presented here addresses these issues by proposing a GFM control method that uses a disturbance observer to synchronize with the grid and estimate voltage sequences without needing AC voltage sensors.

## System Modeling and Sequence Separation

To manage unbalanced conditions, the control system must differentiate between the positive- and negative-sequence components of the electrical quantities. The system is modeled using complex space vectors in a general rotating coordinate frame defined by the angle $\vartheta_c$ and angular speed $\omega_c$. In this framework, the converter is connected to the grid through a total inductance $L$.

The dynamics of the system are governed by the converter current $i_c$ and the grid voltage $u_g$, which is decomposed into its positive-sequence $u_g^+$ and negative-sequence $u_g^-$ parts. A central component of this method is the concept of dynamic sequence separation. Instead of using complex sequence extraction filters that introduce delays, the method defines quasi-static positive-sequence current $i_c^+$ and negative-sequence current $i_c^-$. These are derived using a complex low-pass filter applied to the measured converter current. This allows the controller to determine the quasi-static converter voltage components $v_c^+$ and $v_c^-$, as well as the positive-sequence active power $p_g^+$. These quantities are essential because they represent the fundamental components the controller aims to regulate, even when the grid is distorted.

## The Role of the Disturbance Observer

The core of the proposed architecture is a disturbance observer (DOB). In control theory, a disturbance observer is used to estimate unknown inputs or internal states of a system by comparing the actual measured output with the output predicted by a model. In this specific application, the DOB serves three unified purposes: synchronization, sequence estimation, and the provision of integral control action.

The observer takes the converter current $i_c$ and the reference converter voltage $u_{c,ref}$ as its primary inputs. Based on an internal model of the inductive grid, it estimates the grid voltage components $\hat{u}_g^+$ and $\hat{u}_g^-$. By doing so, it eliminates the need for physical AC voltage sensors at the converter terminals. This estimation process is implemented as a direct discrete-time algorithm, which is optimized for performance at the lower sampling frequencies often found in high-power industrial converters.

The use of a DOB reduces the control system's complexity. While some sequence separation methods require high-order filters or multiple cascaded control loops, this observer-based approach results in a system with only seven real-valued state variables. This streamlined structure makes the control logic easier to implement and analyze for stability.

## Grid-Forming Control Law and Negative-Sequence Suppression

The grid-forming control objective is twofold: to regulate the positive-sequence active power $p_g^+$ to a desired reference and to maintain the positive-sequence converter-voltage magnitude $v_c^+$ at a constant level. The converter voltage reference $u_{c,ref}$ is constructed from an estimate of the internal converter voltage $\hat{v}_c$ and a feedback correction term $e_c$:

$$
u_{c,ref} = \hat{v}_c + e_c
$$

The correction term $e_c$ is divided into two parts: $e_c^+$ and $e_c^-$. The positive-sequence term $e_c^+$ uses proportional gains $k_p$ and $k_v$ to handle power and voltage regulation. It also includes decoupling terms to ensure that changes in active power do not cause unwanted fluctuations in the voltage magnitude.

The treatment of the negative-sequence component $e_c^-$ is what enables robust operation in unbalanced grids. The goal is to make the converter appear as a purely positive-sequence voltage source. To achieve this, the controller must actively suppress the negative-sequence voltage at the converter terminals. This is done by applying a feedback gain $k_n$ to the estimated negative-sequence voltage $\hat{v}_c^-$. A unique finding of this research is that $k_n$ should be a purely imaginary gain ($k_n = jk_n$ where $k_n > 0$). This specific configuration provides the necessary damping to stabilize the negative-sequence dynamics, which would otherwise oscillate at $2\omega_g$ (twice the grid frequency).

## Robust Fault Ride-Through and Current Limitation

During grid faults, the voltage can drop significantly, potentially causing the converter to inject currents that exceed its hardware limits. A robust grid-forming controller must include a fault ride-through (FRT) strategy that protects the converter while continuing to support the grid.

The proposed method integrates a transparent current control mode that activates when current limits are reached. Unlike simple saturation blocks that can clip the current waveforms and introduce harmonics, this method uses a sophisticated reference limitation strategy. 

1.  **Active-Power Reference Limitation:** The controller dynamically calculates the maximum allowable active power $p_{max}$ based on the remaining current capacity after prioritizing reactive power and the necessary negative-sequence current. The power reference $p_{g,ref}$ is capped at this value.
2.  **Elliptical Current Limiter:** When the grid is unbalanced, the current vector follows an elliptical path rather than a circular one. To limit this current without distorting its shape, the controller scales both the positive- and negative-sequence current references by a common factor. This preserves the elliptical trajectory and prevents the injection of unwanted current harmonics into the grid.

This strategy allows the converter to transition smoothly between grid-forming mode (where it regulates voltage) and current-control mode (where it protects itself). Once the fault is cleared and the current drops below the limit $i_{max}$, the grid-forming characteristics are automatically restored.

## Stability Analysis and Tuning

Ensuring the stability of a GFM controller across varying grid conditions is critical. The authors performed a detailed small-signal stability analysis by linearizing the system equations around an operating point.

A key discovery was the interaction between the positive and negative sequences. The analysis showed that without the imaginary feedback gain $k_n$, the system contains poles on the imaginary axis at $\pm j2\omega_g$. This means any disturbance would result in undamped oscillations at twice the grid frequency. By introducing $k_n = jk_n$, these poles are shifted into the stable left half-plane.

The stability was verified using the Routh-Hurwitz criterion and numerical pole-zero maps. The system remained stable across a wide range of grid inductances $L$ (from strong grids to very weak grids near the power transfer limit) and power levels. Furthermore, the observer's estimation error was shown to be decoupled from the control dynamics, meaning that errors in estimating the grid voltage do not inherently destabilize the power and voltage regulation loops.

## Experimental Validation

The effectiveness of the disturbance-observer-based control was validated using a 12.5-kVA three-phase converter. The experiments covered a variety of challenging scenarios to test the robustness of the method.

### Strong vs. Weak Grids
The controller was tested in both strong grids (low inductance) and weak grids (high inductance). In weak grids, the converter's ability to support the voltage is more pronounced. The experimental results showed that the DOB correctly estimated the grid state and maintained stability even when the grid inductance was high, which is traditionally a difficult condition for converter control.

### L and LCL Filters
Converters use output filters (either a simple inductor $L$ or an inductor-capacitor-inductor $LCL$ filter) to smooth the output current. A common problem in control design is that a controller tuned for an L filter may become unstable when used with an LCL filter due to resonance. However, the proposed DOB-based method demonstrated high robustness, performing almost identically with both filter types without requiring specific retuning for the filter's resonant frequencies.

### Fault Response
Under balanced three-phase faults, the converter successfully limited its current and supported the grid voltage. Under unbalanced single-phase faults, the controller demonstrated its unique ability to maintain a constant positive-sequence voltage magnitude while allowing the negative-sequence current to flow as required by the grid imbalance.

## Comparison with Current Approaches

The proposed method differs from existing grid-forming strategies in several key ways:
*   **Sensorless Synchronization:** Unlike many GFM methods that require AC voltage sensors at the output to synchronize via a Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) or to implement virtual impedance, this method uses only current measurements and the converter's own voltage reference.
*   **Unified Observer Design:** Instead of separate modules for synchronization, sequence separation, and integral control, a single disturbance observer handles all these tasks. This reduces the mathematical order of the system and simplifies tuning.
*   **Damping of $2\omega_g$ Oscillations:** By identifying the need for a purely imaginary gain $k_n$ to damp negative-sequence oscillations, the method provides a theoretically grounded solution for unbalanced grid stability that is often handled heuristically in other designs.
*   **Harmonic-Free Limitation:** The elliptical current limiting strategy is specifically designed for unbalanced conditions, ensuring that phase currents are limited safely without creating the waveform distortion common in simpler saturation-based limiters.

In conclusion, this disturbance-observer-based approach provides a robust and simplified framework for grid-forming converters. By eliminating the need for AC voltage sensors and providing a clear path for stabilizing unbalanced grid dynamics, it offers a practical solution for the next generation of power electronics-dominated electrical grids.
