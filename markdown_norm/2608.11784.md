## The Practice of Coarsening Latent Variables

In empirical research, analysts often work with categories that cannot be directly observed. For instance, a researcher might study the effect of race on income disparities, the impact of specific disease subtypes on patient outcomes, or the relationship between poverty status and educational attainment. In many of these cases, the "ground truth" group membership—the latent class—is unknown. Instead, analysts rely on proxies, such as probability vectors generated by machine learning classifiers.

A common but often overlooked practice in these scenarios is "coarsening." This occurs when an analyst takes a continuous, calibrated probability vector $p$ (e.g., a $70\%$ probability of belonging to Class A and $30\%$ to Class B) and simplifies it into a discrete "hard" label $h$. This might involve taking the class with the highest probability (the $\text{argmax}$ assignment) or rounding the probabilities to the nearest integer. While this simplification is often done for convenience or to treat the latent variable as a categorical one, it introduces significant statistical complications.

This research investigates the consequences of this coarsening when the resulting hard labels are used as regressors in downstream analysis. The study moves beyond acknowledging that measurement error exists, providing a framework to quantify two specific costs: directional distortion of the point estimates and the loss of coverage for confidence intervals. By treating the problem as a diagnostic challenge, the work allows researchers to assess the validity of their inferences without requiring the very ground-truth labels they lack.

## A Framework for Latent-Class Inference

To understand the impact of coarsening, the paper establishes a foundational model centered on the relationship between an outcome $Y$, observable covariates $X$, and a probability vector $p$ representing membership in one of $K$ latent classes. The actual, unobserved membership is represented by a one-hot vector $g$.

The analysis relies on two primary structural assumptions. First, the **Structural Conditional Mean** assumption posits that the latent group effects are constant relative to $X$:

$$
E[Y | G, p, X] = \mu(X) + \tau^\top g
$$

Here, $\tau$ represents the contrast between different classes, and $\mu(X)$ captures the effect of other covariates. Crucially, this assumption includes an exclusion restriction: once we know the actual group $g$, the probability vector $p$ provides no additional information about the outcome $Y$.

The second pillar is **Conditional Calibration**, which requires that $E[g | p, X] = p$. This means the probability vector is an unbiased predictor of the true group membership. Under these conditions, the paper demonstrates that the complex latent-variable problem reduces to a more manageable partially linear regression on observables:

$$
E[Y | p, X] = \mu(X) + \tau^\top p
$$

This reduction is significant because it allows researchers to identify the parameters of interest $\tau$ using only the observed data $(Y, X, p)$, bypassing the need for unobserved labels $g$.

## Spectral Geometry and Identification Strength

A key contribution of the work is characterizing the "strength" of identification through the spectral decomposition of the residual-score covariance matrix $M$. This matrix is defined as $M = E[(p - r(X))(p - r(X))^\top]$, where $r(X) = E[p | X]$ is the conditional mean of the probability vector.

The paper proves that the parameter vector $\tau$ is point-identified from the joint law of observables by the moment equation:

$$
E[aR] = M\tau
$$

In this equation, $a = p - r(X)$ represents the score residual, and $R = Y - m(X)$ represents the outcome residual. The eigenvalues $\lambda_j$ of the matrix $M$ determine how well each direction in the parameter space is identified. If all eigenvalues are greater than zero ($\lambda_j > 0$), $\tau$ is fully identified. However, as an eigenvalue approaches zero, the identification in the direction of the corresponding eigenvector $v_j$ becomes weak. This spectral perspective reveals that the precision of our estimates is not uniform; some group contrasts may be estimated with high confidence, while others are highly sensitive to noise or small violations of assumptions.

## Quantifying the Distortion of Point Estimates

When an analyst replaces the continuous vector $p$ with a coarsened label $h = h(p, X)$, the resulting estimator $\hat{\tau}_h$ no longer converges to the true value $\tau$. Instead, it converges to a distorted value $A_h \tau$. The research defines the **coarsening operator** $A_h$ as:

$$
A_h = D_h^{-1} C_h
$$

where $D_h = E[a_h a_h^\top]$ is the covariance of the coarsened score residual and $C_h = E[a_h a^\top]$ is the cross-covariance between the coarsened and original score residuals. 

The paper shows that this distortion is generally **anisotropic**, meaning it affects different directions in different ways. In a multi-class setting, coarsening does not simply "shrink" all coefficients toward zero by a uniform factor. Instead, the relationship between classes is warped. The distortion can be expressed as:

$$
A_h = I + D_h^{-1} E[a_h u^\top]
$$

where $u$ is the signal discarded during the coarsening process. This highlights that coarsening is only "cost-free" if the retained signal is entirely uncorrelated with the discarded information—a condition rarely met by common practices like taking the $\text{argmax}$.

## The Cost of Silent Invalidity: Coverage Loss

Beyond point estimates, the research focuses on the validity of confidence intervals. An analyst using coarsened data typically reports a standard Wald interval. Because the coarsening introduces a bias that the standard error calculation does not account for, these intervals suffer from coverage loss.

The paper provides a formula for the limiting coverage probability of these intervals:

$$
\Phi(z - \lambda_v) - \Phi(-z - \lambda_v)
$$

The term $\lambda_v$ is a noncentrality parameter representing the ratio of the directional coarsening bias to the reported standard error. This finding reveals a phenomenon of "silent invalidity": an interval might appear narrow and precise, but its actual coverage (the probability that it contains the true $\tau$) could be near zero because it is centered far from the truth.

Crucially, the paper demonstrates that researchers can approximate this coverage loss **before** reporting their results. Because the coarsening operator $A_h$ and the parameters needed for $\lambda_v$ depend on observables, one can compare the results of the "soft" (uncoarsened) estimator with the "hard" (coarsened) estimator to gauge how much the interval has been compromised.

## Diagnosing Assumption Failures

The reliability of these diagnostics depends on the core assumptions of calibration and exclusion. The paper introduces a sensitivity analysis to determine which assumption might be failing, especially when a small "gold-standard" labeled subsample is available.

If conditional calibration fails—meaning $E[g | p, X] = p + \eta(p, X)$—the resulting bias is amplified along the weakly identified directions (those with small eigenvalues $\lambda_j$). This makes the spectrum of the covariance matrix $M$ a critical diagnostic tool.

The author proposes a **recalibration experiment**. By using a labeled subsample to estimate a mapping that corrects the probabilities and then re-running the analysis, researchers can distinguish between two problems. If the recalibration closes the gap between estimates, the issue was likely miscalibration. If the gap widens or the results become more unstable, it suggests a violation of the exclusion restriction—meaning the features used to generate the probability $p$ have a direct effect on the outcome $Y$ that is not captured by the group membership alone.

## Empirical Evidence and Applications

The framework's utility is demonstrated through several real-world audits:

1.  **UCI Adult Disparity Audit:** In auditing income disparities using a proxied race attribute, the study found that attenuation was anisotropic. Along well-identified directions, the soft estimator matched the full-information benchmark. In weak directions, deviations were found to be caused by a violation of the exclusion restriction rather than simple miscalibration.
2.  **North Carolina Voter Turnout:** Using a race proxy based on surnames, this audit provided a successful case for the framework. Because the classifier was well-conditioned and there was a negligible direct channel between surnames and turnout, the uncoarsened ("soft") estimator recovered turnout disparities almost exactly, significantly outperforming hard-labeling approaches.

## Conclusion: A Diagnostic Path Forward

This research establishes that coarsening latent-class probabilities is not a neutral simplification but a source of significant statistical distortion. By providing a mathematical description of the coarsening operator and the noncentrality parameter, the paper offers practitioners a way to look "under the hood" of their inferences. 

Rather than prescribing a single "correct" way to handle latent variables, the work provides a diagnostic toolkit. It enables researchers to quantify the cost of their choices, identify when their confidence intervals are likely to be invalid, and determine whether their models suffer from miscalibration or deeper structural violations. As machine-learned scores are increasingly used as proxies in social science, medicine, and policy, these tools provide a necessary foundation for robust and transparent empirical analysis.
