## Interpreting Wastewater Microbiomes through Archetypal Analysis

Water resource recovery facilities (WRRFs) play a critical role in public health and environmental protection, but they are also significant sources of nitrous oxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}$), a greenhouse gas with a global warming potential approximately 300 times that of carbon dioxide. Because $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ emissions from activated sludge processes fluctuate significantly based on seasonal temperatures, influent loads, and operational configurations, identifying the microbial drivers of these emissions is essential for mitigation. However, the microbial communities in these systems are exceptionally complex, often containing thousands of different taxa interacting in dynamic ways.

Traditional methods for analyzing these communities, such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) or Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS), reduce this high-dimensional data into abstract axes. While useful for visualization, these axes are linear combinations of taxa that do not always correspond to biologically feasible community states. This study addresses this limitation by applying Archetypal Analysis (AA) to microbiome data from two full-scale WRRFs in Switzerland: Zürich-Werdhölzli and Jungholz Uster. By summarizing complex microbial profiles as mixtures of "archetypes"—extreme but realizable community configurations—the researchers established a framework for linking specific microbiome states to high and low $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ emission regimes.

*{fig:rescaled-rss}: The rescaled residual sum of squares ($\text{RSS}$) as a function of the number of archetypes $p$. The "elbow" at $p=3$ for both Werdhölzli (blue) and Uster (green) suggests that three archetypal profiles are sufficient to capture the dominant variations in the microbial community.*

## The Mechanics of Archetypal Analysis

At its core, Archetypal Analysis seeks to represent a dataset as a convex combination of "pure" types, or archetypes, which are themselves representative of the data's extremes. Unlike centroids in cluster analysis, which represent the average of a group, archetypes represent the corners of the data's multi-dimensional convex hull. This means that any individual sample $x_i$ (a genus-level microbial profile) can be approximated as:

$$
x_i \approx \sum_{k=1}^p \alpha_{ik} z_k
$$

where $z_k$ represents the $k$-th archetype, and $\alpha_{ik}$ is the weight or proportion of that archetype in sample $i$. Importantly, these weights are constrained such that they are non-negative and sum to one ($\sum \alpha_{ik} = 1$, $\alpha_{ik} \geq 0$). Furthermore, the archetypes themselves are constrained to be mixtures of the observed samples:

$$
z_k = \sum_{j=1}^n \beta_{kj} x_j
$$

where $\beta_{kj}$ follows similar sum-to-one and non-negativity constraints. These constraints ensure that every archetype is a "feasible" microbiome—a configuration that could realistically exist in the reactor. In this study, the researchers found that $p=3$ archetypes provided an optimal balance between simplicity and accuracy, as shown by the elbow in the $\text{RSS}$ plot ({fig:rescaled-rss}). This reduction from thousands of variables (genera) to just three archetype coefficients enables a much clearer view of system behavior.

## Mapping the Simplex of Emission States

By using three archetypes, the state of the microbiome at any given time can be visualized in a 2D triangle known as a simplex. Each vertex of the triangle represents a pure archetype (100% $A1$, $A2$, or $A3$), while points inside the triangle represent different mixtures. When the researchers mapped samples from the two WRRFs into this space, they observed a striking alignment with $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ emission states, despite the archetypes being derived without any prior knowledge of emissions.

Red circles represent high $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ emission samples ($EF \geq 0.016$), while blue circles represent low emission samples ($EF < 0.016$). High emission states are notably concentrated near Archetype 3 ($A3$).*

![simplex-uster](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.18295v1/Figures/Simplex_U2_3A.png)
*{fig:simplex-uster}: Archetypal state space for the Uster facility. High emission samples from multiple parallel reactors converge toward Archetype 3 ($A3$), indicating a common "high-emission" microbial configuration.*

In both facilities, Archetype 1 ($A1$) was strongly associated with low-emission states, whereas Archetype 3 ($A3$) served as an indicator for high-emission regimes. In the Uster WRRF, which operates with six parallel sequencing batch reactors (SBRs), the high-emission samples from different reactors converged toward the same $A3$ vertex. This suggests that while different reactors may follow unique paths, they eventually arrive at a similar problematic microbial state when emissions peak.

## Temporal Trajectories and Early Warning

One of the most practical applications of this framework is the ability to track the microbiome's movement through the state space over time. By monitoring the "mixture weights" ($\alpha$ values), plant operators can see when a community is drifting away from a stable, low-emission $A1$ state toward a high-emission $A3$ state.

![temporal-bar-werdh-lzli](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.18295v1/Figures/Bar_W.png)
*{fig:temporal-bar-werdh-lzli}: Temporal dynamics of archetype weights at Werdhölzli. The line represents the $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ emission factor. Sustained periods of high emissions in spring 2022 correspond clearly with an increase in the $A3$ (red) weight.*

![temporal-bar-uster](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.18295v1/Figures/Bar_U2_3A.png)
*{fig:temporal-bar-uster}: Temporal archetype composition for the six SBRs at Uster. High emission peaks consistently align with periods of $A3$ dominance.*

As shown in {fig:temporal-bar-werdh-lzli} and {fig:temporal-bar-uster}, the transition between emission states is rarely instantaneous. Instead, the microbiome often exhibits a gradual shift in composition weeks before an emission peak reaches its maximum. This provides a potential window for intervention. If the $A3$ weight begins to climb, operators might adjust parameters like aeration intensity or sludge age to steer the community back toward the $A1$ regime.

## Functional Signatures of High-Emission Archetypes

To understand *why* certain archetypes are linked to high emissions, the researchers analyzed the taxonomic and functional markers of each configuration. $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ is primarily produced through two pathways in activated sludge: ammonia-oxidizing bacteria (AOB) activity (during nitrification) and incomplete denitrification by heterotrophs.

![heatmap-werdh-lzli](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.18295v1/Figures/Heat_W.png)
*{fig:heatmap-werdh-lzli}: Log-abundance heatmap of the most defining genera for the three archetypes at Werdhölzli. $A3$ shows distinct enrichment in certain heterotrophic taxa.*

![heatmap-uster](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.18295v1/Figures/Heat_U2_3A.png)
*{fig:heatmap-uster}: Log-abundance heatmap of defining genera at Uster. The high-emission archetype $A3$ is characterized by a specific set of microbial indicators.*

The analysis revealed that the high-emission archetypes were site-specific in their functional makeup:
1.  **Werdhölzli ($A3$):** This archetype showed an enrichment of heterotrophic denitrifiers ($\text{DEN}$), but a relative decrease in the abundance of genera carrying the $nosZ$ gene (which encodes the enzyme responsible for reducing $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ to nitrogen gas). The $nosZ/\text{DEN}$ ratio dropped significantly in $A3$ compared to $A1$. This suggests that high emissions at this site are likely driven by an imbalance where the capacity to produce $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ via denitrification exceeds the capacity to consume it.
2.  **Uster ($A3$):** Here, the high-emission archetype was characterized by an increased $\text{AOB}/\text{NOB}$ ratio. Ammonia-oxidizing bacteria ($\text{AOB}$) were present, but nitrite-oxidizing bacteria ($\text{NOB}$) like *Nitrospira* were significantly reduced. This leads to the accumulation of nitrite, a known precursor that stimulates $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ production via the nitrifier-denitrification pathway.

## Temperature as a Driver of Archetypal Shifts

A key finding of the study was the strong relationship between wastewater temperature and archetype weights. Microbiome composition is inherently seasonal, and temperature acts as a primary environmental filter that reshapes the community.

![temperature-weight-werdh-lzli](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.18295v1/Figures/Temp_weight_W.png)
*{fig:temperature-weight-werdh-lzli}: Relationship between influent temperature and archetype coefficients at Werdhölzli. Warm conditions favor the low-emission $A1$ state, while colder temperatures correlate with the high-emission $A3$ state.*

![temperature-weight-uster](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.18295v1/Figures/Temp_weight_U2_3A.png)
*{fig:temperature-weight-uster}: Relationship between temperature and archetype coefficients at Uster. Similar to Werdhölzli, temperature appears to regulate the movement of the microbiome through the simplex state space.*

At both sites, Archetype 1 weights increased as temperatures rose, while Archetype 3 weights increased as temperatures fell ({fig:temperature-weight-werdh-lzli} and {fig:temperature-weight-uster}). This suggests that cold weather doesn't just slow down kinetics; it fundamentally reorganizes the microbial community into a configuration that is more prone to $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ accumulation. The archetypal framework effectively captures this seasonal reorganization, providing a quantitative link between environmental forcing and functional outcomes.

## Towards Predictive Control and Mitigation

The significance of this work lies in its transition from abstract microbial ecology to actionable environmental engineering. By demonstrating that a low-dimensional "state" (the archetype weights) can accurately categorize emission regimes across different plant scales and configurations, the study provides a roadmap for future monitoring systems.

Unlike previous attempts to correlate individual "bioindicator" species with $\text{N}_2\text{O}$—which often fail because microbial functions are redundant and spread across many taxa—Archetypal Analysis looks at the community as a holistic, integrated unit. The findings suggest that:
*   Interpretable models can be built using unsupervised methods, meaning we don't necessarily need labeled emission data to discover the underlying states of a microbiome.
*   The $A3$ archetype serves as a common destination for communities undergoing stress or seasonal shifts that lead to high $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ output.
*   Integrating these microbial "trajectories" with standard operational sensors (measuring temperature, dissolved oxygen, and nitrogen species) could lead to more robust predictive models for greenhouse gas emissions.

In conclusion, Archetypal Analysis provides a bridge between the high-dimensional complexity of the wastewater microbiome and the practical need for stable, low-emission reactor performance. By viewing the activated sludge as a mixture of realizable archetypes, researchers and practitioners can better understand the microbial transitions that lead to $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ hotspots, ultimately supporting more effective climate mitigation strategies in the water sector.
