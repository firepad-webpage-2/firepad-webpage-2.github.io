## The Challenge of Inverse Optimal Transport

Optimal Transport (OT) is a mathematical framework traditionally used to find the most efficient way to transform one probability distribution into another given a specific cost function. However, in many real-world scenarios—ranging from biological lineage tracing to economic matching—researchers face the inverse problem. They observe the "transport" or movement of entities and must infer the underlying rules or costs that governed those movements. This is known as Inverse Optimal Transport (IOT).

Inferring these costs is difficult because the relationship between the cost parameters and the observed movement is non-linear and often involves hidden constraints. For instance, in biology, we might observe how a population of cells transitions between different states over time. We assume these cells follow an optimal path that minimizes some energy expenditure, but the specific "energy cost" (parameterized by biological features like gene expression) is unknown.

Current approaches to IOT often struggle with theoretical guarantees. While researchers can fit models to data, it is frequently unclear whether the parameters are uniquely identifiable, how much data is needed to recover the true underlying "features" of the cost, or if the optimization algorithms will reliably converge to the correct solution. This paper introduces a unified framework called "Sinkhorn Linearization and the Spectral Proxy" to provide these missing theoretical foundations. By treating the IOT problem as a statistical inference task on a feature-parameterized cost, the authors derive explicit bounds for identifiability, stability, and convergence.

## Feature-Parameterized Entropic Optimal Transport

The paper focuses on a setting where the cost of moving from state $i$ to state $j$ is not an arbitrary number, but a linear combination of interpretable features. Let $\phi(i,j) \in \mathbb{R}^F$ be a vector of features describing the relationship between $i$ and $j$. The cost function is defined as:

$$C_\theta(i,j) = -\theta^\top \phi(i,j)$$

where $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^F$ represents the weight or importance of each feature. The goal is to recover $\theta$ from observed data. In practical applications, the number of potential features $F$ can be large, but only a few might actually matter. This motivates the use of sparse recovery techniques, where we aim to identify the small subset of relevant features.

To make the problem computationally tractable and to model natural noise, the authors use "Entropic" Optimal Transport. This adds a regularization term (scaled by $\epsilon$) to the transport problem, which smooths the transport plan and leads to the well-known Sinkhorn algorithm. The observed data in this framework are conditional transition operators (probabilities of moving to $j$ given starting point $i$), which are derived from the optimal transport plan $\pi$.

The IOT estimation is then formulated as a cross-entropy maximum-likelihood problem. Given observed transitions $\hat{Q}$, we seek the parameters $\theta$ that minimize the difference between our model and the data, while penalizing the number of active features using an $\ell_1$ penalty:

$$\min_{\theta \in \mathbb{R}^F} \sum_{l=1}^L \text{CE}(\hat{Q}_l \Vert Q_\theta(a_l, b_l)) + \lambda_n \Vert \theta \Vert_1$$

In this equation, $\text{CE}(\hat{Q} \Vert Q) = - \sum_i \rho_i \sum_j \hat{Q}_{ij} \log Q_{ij}$ is the cross-entropy loss, and $\lambda_n$ controls the strength of the sparsity penalty.

## The Spectral Sandwich: A Unified Technical Core

The central technical contribution of the paper is the "Sinkhorn Linearization." To understand how the inferred parameters $\theta$ respond to changes in the observed data, one must understand how the optimal transport plan $\pi$ changes when the cost function $C$ is perturbed. The authors achieve this by differentiating the Karush-Kuhn-Ticker (KKT) conditions of the entropic OT problem.

The KKT conditions for entropic OT, which ensure the plan is optimal and satisfies marginal constraints, are given by:

$$C_{ij} + \epsilon \log \pi_{ij} + \alpha_i + \beta_j = 0$$

By differentiating these conditions, the authors show that a small change in the cost $\delta c$ results in a change in the plan $\delta x$ described by a linear relationship: $\delta x = -BH_T^{-1} B^\top \delta c$. Here, $H_T$ is the restricted Hessian of the entropic objective, and $B$ is a basis for the space of allowed plan variations.

The "Spectral Sandwich" refers to the bounds placed on the eigenvalues of this restricted Hessian:

$$(\pi_{\min}/\epsilon)I \preceq H_T^{-1} \preceq (\pi_{\max}/\epsilon)I$$

where $\pi_{\min}$ and $\pi_{\max}$ are the smallest and largest entries in the transport plan. This "sandwich" is significant because it provides a way to control the sensitivity of the entire IOT system. If $\epsilon$ is very small (low noise) or $\pi_{\min}$ is very small (highly sparse movement), the system becomes ill-conditioned, meaning small errors in the data lead to large errors in the inferred parameters. The authors use this single mathematical tool to derive all their main theorems, ensuring a unified theory for IOT.

## Identifiability and Sparsistency

A fundamental question in any inverse problem is whether the parameters can be uniquely recovered. The paper addresses this through the lens of "Identifiability" (Theorem 1). They discover that parameters are identifiable on a "quotient space." This is due to a "gauge subspace"—certain changes in the cost function (like adding a constant to a whole row) do not change the optimal transport plan at all.

The authors show that for full recovery of $\theta$ in the original parameter space, the number of features $F$ must be bounded by the dimensions of the state space: $F \leq (K-1)^2$. They also demonstrate that while having more data (multiple marginal pairs) improves the numerical stability of the problem, it does not change this fundamental rank limit.

Beyond simple identification, the paper establishes "Sparsistency" (Theorem 2). This provides the conditions under which the $\ell_1$-penalized estimator will correctly identify the "true" set of relevant features as the sample size grows. They provide an explicit exponential rate constant for this recovery:

$$C_2 = 2/\Delta_{\max}^2$$

This constant $C_2$ tells researchers exactly how quickly the probability of failure vanishes as more data is collected. Using the spectral sandwich, they show that this rate depends critically on the ratio $\pi_{\max}/(\epsilon \pi_{\min})$.

## Stability, Convergence, and Misspecification

In addition to statistical guarantees, the paper explores the algorithmic behavior of IOT. Theorem 3 (Well-Posedness) establishes that the map from parameters to observed transitions is "strongly monotone." This leads to Lipschitz bounds, which essentially guarantee that the inverse map is stable: small noises in the observed transition probabilities will not cause the inferred cost parameters to jump wildly.

For practitioners using gradient-based optimization, Theorem 4 (Convergence) is particularly relevant. The authors prove that the cross-entropy objective is locally strongly convex. The smallest eigenvalue of the Hessian of the loss is bounded by:

$$\mu \ge \left( \frac{\pi_{\min,*}^2}{\epsilon^2} \right) \lambda_{\min}(\Sigma) > 0$$

This bound ensures that gradient descent will converge monotonically to a local minimum, provided the algorithm is initialized within a reasonable neighborhood.

Finally, the paper acknowledges that real-world data might not perfectly fit an entropic OT model. In such cases of "Misspecification" (Observation 5), they show that the IOT estimator converges to a "pseudo-true" parameter $\theta^\dagger$. This parameter represents the best possible approximation—the projection of the true data-generating process $Q^*$ onto the manifold of all possible optimal transport models.

## Experimental Evidence and Practical Implications

The theoretical findings are backed by extensive numerical simulations. The authors tested their bounds across varying state space sizes $K$, feature counts $F$, and regularization levels $\epsilon$. 

One striking result is the "V-shape" in entropic bias. If data is generated with a certain $\epsilon$ but fitted using a different $\epsilon'$, the bias in the recovered parameters is minimized exactly when $\epsilon' = \epsilon$. This confirms the theoretical sensitivity of the model to the level of entropic smoothing.

The research also highlights the importance of initialization. In their experiments, a simple random initialization for the optimization led to success in only 25% of cases. However, using a "multiscale" warm-start strategy—where the model is first solved on a coarser, simpler version of the problem—increased the success rate to 95%. This demonstrates that while local strong convexity exists, finding the right "basin of attraction" is crucial for practical IOT applications.

By providing the first unified theory that links the statistical difficulty of IOT to the spectral properties of the Sinkhorn algorithm, this work offers a rigorous roadmap for researchers using transport-based models to uncover the hidden mechanisms driving complex systems.
