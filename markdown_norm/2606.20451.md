## Understanding Competing Risks in Complex Systems

Reliability analysis and survival modeling are fundamental tools in engineering and medicine, used to estimate the time until an event of interest occurs. In many real-world scenarios, however, a system or subject is at risk of failing due to multiple distinct causes. This is known as the "competing risks" framework, where the occurrence of one type of event precludes the observation of others. For example, a Graphics Processing Unit (GPU) in a supercomputer might fail due to a memory error or a connection error; once it fails for one reason, it cannot fail for the other.

Predicting these failure times accurately is challenging because traditional statistical models often rely on strict parametric assumptions—presuming the data follows a specific distribution like a Weibull or Gamma curve. While Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) offer a more flexible, data-driven alternative, they frequently operate as "black boxes." This makes it difficult to understand how specific factors influence failure and leads to a reliance on exhaustive, trial-and-error hyperparameter tuning. Furthermore, standard DNNs often struggle to handle complex data structures, such as the hierarchical arrangement of components within a supercomputer.

The Structured Segmented Hazard Deep Neural Network (SSH-Net) is designed to bridge these gaps. It provides a structured approach to modeling time-to-event data under competing risks by explicitly linking the architecture of the neural network to the physical or logical structure of the data. By doing so, it improves both the interpretability of the model and the efficiency of the training process, particularly for large-scale engineering systems like the Titan supercomputer.

## The Framework: Cause-Specific Hazards and Piecewise Continuity

At the heart of SSH-Net is the cause-specific hazard function. In a system with $K$ possible failure types, the hazard function for cause $k$ at time $t$ is defined as the instantaneous rate of failure due to that specific cause, given that the unit has survived up to time $t$:

$$h(t, k | x) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{P(t \leq T < t + \Delta t, G = k | T \geq t, x)}{\Delta t}$$

where $T$ is the failure time, $G$ is the failure type, and $x$ represents the input covariates. From this, we can derive the Cumulative Incidence Function (CIF), which represents the probability of failing due to cause $k$ by time $t$:

$$F(t, k | x) = P(T \leq t, G = k | x) = \int_{0}^{t} h(u, k | x) S(u | x) du$$

Here, $S(t | x)$ is the overall survival function, representing the probability that the unit survives all possible failure causes up to time $t$:

$$S(t | x) = \exp\left( -\sum_{k=1}^{K} \int_{0}^{t} h(u, k | x) du \right)$$

Rather than assuming a global parametric form for these hazards, SSH-Net adopts a piecewise constant hazard assumption. The study interval $(0, \tau]$ is divided into $J_k$ discrete time bins for each failure type $k$. Within each bin, the hazard rate is assumed to be constant. This allows the model to handle continuous failure times without the information loss associated with simple time discretization, while maintaining enough flexibility to capture complex, non-linear failure trends. The network's primary outputs are the logarithms of these constant hazard rates, denoted as $o_{jk|x_i} = \log(h_{jk|x_i})$.

## Architectural Innovation: Aligning Network Structure with Data Structure

The most distinctive feature of SSH-Net is its "structured" architecture. In systems like supercomputers, covariates naturally fall into different groups. For a GPU, there are "global" spatial covariates (like the cabinet's row and column in the server room) and "local" hierarchical covariates (like the cage, slot, and node position within a cabinet).

### Specialized Sub-networks
SSH-Net processes these groups separately using dedicated sub-networks:
*   **Sub-network E:** This handles the lower-level hierarchical covariates $x_{ic}$. The number of nodes in this sub-network is directly associated with the dimension of the hierarchical data.
*   **Sub-network L:** This handles the global spatial covariates $x_{il}$. 

By separating these inputs, the model can learn specific representations for different levels of the system's architecture before synthesising them. Both E and L typically use ReLU activation functions to learn non-linear interactions while avoiding the vanishing gradient problem.

### Information Synthesis and Failure Differentiation
After initial processing, the outputs are concatenated and passed through a **Shared Layer S**, which performs data reduction and extracts the most relevant features using a Tanh activation. 

Finally, the model branches into $K$ separate **Failure-Specific Sub-networks ($M_k$)**. Each sub-network $M_k$ is responsible for predicting the hazards of one specific failure type. A crucial design choice in SSH-Net is linking the number of nodes in $M_k$ to the number of time bins $J_k$. This heuristic provides a principled way to set the network's capacity based on the complexity of the temporal failure pattern it needs to learn.

## Training and Regularization: The Penalized Loss Function

Training a neural network to predict hazard functions can sometimes lead to "wiggly" or erratic predictions that overfit the noise in the training data. To ensure that the predicted hazard curves are physically plausible and smooth, SSH-Net employs a specialized loss function based on a penalized log-likelihood.

The loss function consists of two primary parts:
1.  **Likelihood Term:** This measures how well the model explains the observed failures and censoring times.
2.  **Smoothness Penalty:** This term penalizes large differences between the constant hazard rates of adjacent time bins.

The overall objective function to be minimized is:

$$L = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left[ \sum_{k=1}^{K} \delta_{ik} \log h(t_i, k | x_i) - \sum_{k=1}^{K} H(t_i, k | x_i) \right] - \sum_{k=1}^{K} \lambda_k \sum_{j=2}^{J_k} (h_{jk} - h_{j-1,k})^2$$

where $H(t_i, k | x_i)$ is the cumulative hazard and $\lambda_k$ is the penalty parameter. The researchers suggest an adaptive approach for setting $\lambda_k$ based on the number of observed failures for that specific type:

$$\lambda_k = \lambda_k^* \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} \delta_{ik} \right)^{1/3}$$

This ensures that the model allows for more flexibility when there is more data available to support complex trends, while enforcing stricter smoothness when data is scarce.

## Experimental Validation: Simulations and Comparative Analysis

The performance of SSH-Net was rigorously tested against two established deep learning models for survival analysis: **DeepHit**, which uses a discrete-time approach, and **Neural Fine-Gray (NFG)**, which models the cumulative incidence function directly.

![rmse-ratios](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.20451v1/img-6.jpeg)
*{fig:rmse-ratios}: Ratio of RMSE for Failure Type 1. Ratios below 1.0 indicate that SSH-Net outperforms the comparison models (DeepHit and NFG).*

In simulation studies mimicking the Titan GPU data, SSH-Net consistently demonstrated superior predictive accuracy. Key metrics included the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) of the CIFs, the Brier Score (a measure of probability calibration), and the Area Under the Curve (AUC) for discrimination. As shown in {fig:rmse-ratios}, the RMSE ratios were almost universally below 1.0, indicating that SSH-Net's predicted probability of failure was closer to the ground truth than its competitors. 

A notable finding was that DeepHit struggled to capture the continuous nature of the hazard trends, while NFG, despite modeling continuous time, was less efficient at capturing nuanced hazard modes compared to the piecewise constant approach of SSH-Net.

## Real-World Application: Reliability of Titan GPUs

The practical utility of SSH-Net was demonstrated using failure data from 19,319 GPUs in the Titan supercomputer. The analysis focused on two main failure causes: **Double Bit Errors (DBE)** and **Off the Bus (OTB)** errors.

![titan-hierarchical-structure](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.20451v1/img-16.jpeg)
*{fig:titan-hierarchical-structure}: The hierarchical layout of GPUs within the Titan supercomputer, showing node, slot, and cage positions.*

The model revealed that the physical location of a GPU significantly impacted its risk profile. For instance, GPUs located in "Cage 2" (the top cage in a cabinet) consistently showed the highest risk for both OTB and DBE failures. This aligns with engineering intuition regarding heat accumulation, as heat typically rises, and the cooling efficiency may vary by height.

![relative-risk](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.20451v1/img-25.jpeg)
*{fig:relative-risk}: The relative risk of OTB failures compared to DBE failures over time for three example units, including 95% confidence intervals derived from Monte-Carlo dropout.*

By analyzing the "Relative Risk"—the ratio of the probability of failing from OTB versus DBE—SSH-Net identified specific operational periods (between 2.5 and 3.5 years) where OTB failures became significantly more likely. Such insights are invaluable for supercomputer operators, allowing them to tailor maintenance schedules and spare-part inventories to the specific risks faced by different components at different stages of their lifecycle.

## Significance and Future Directions

SSH-Net offers a framework for reliability analysis that is both high-performing and interpretable. By moving away from "black-box" architectures and instead building networks that mirror the data's structure, it provides a more systematic way to perform deep learning for time-to-event data.

Its ability to handle structured covariates (spatial and hierarchical) and provide smooth, continuous-time hazard estimates makes it particularly suited for modern engineering applications. Beyond supercomputers, this approach could be adapted for industrial manufacturing lines, aerospace systems, or even clinical settings where patients are treated within a hierarchical healthcare system.

Future developments may involve integrating Gaussian Process models to better capture continuous spatial correlations or extending the network to handle time-varying covariates—such as real-time temperature or voltage readings—to enable truly dynamic failure forecasting.
