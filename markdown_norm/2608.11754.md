## Introduction to Nonlinearities in IM-DD Systems

In the landscape of modern data centers and short-reach optical interconnects, Intensity Modulation and Direct Detection (IM-DD) remains a dominant technology. Its enduring popularity stems from its relative simplicity, low cost, and high power efficiency compared to coherent optical systems. However, as the demand for data bandwidth continues to skyrocket, these systems are being pushed to operate at ever-higher baud rates, such as 112 GBd and beyond. At these speeds, the physical limitations of hardware components become increasingly pronounced.

One of the most significant hurdles in high-speed IM-DD links is nonlinear distortion. This distortion primarily originates from the electro-optical (EO) components, particularly the Mach-Zehnder Modulator (MZM), which converts electrical signals into optical intensity. While traditional methods for measuring nonlinearity often rely on analog radio frequency (RF) metrics like third-order intercept points, these metrics are frequently inadequate for digital communication links. Digital waveforms, such as Pulse Amplitude Modulation (PAM-4), possess statistical properties that differ significantly from the simple sine waves used in RF testing. Consequently, the nonlinear impact on a digital signal depends not just on the hardware, but on the specific modulation format and signal statistics.

To address this, researchers have turned to the Best Linear Approximation (BLA). The BLA is a mathematical framework that describes a nonlinear system by finding the "best" linear model that mimics its behavior for a given input. This study utilizes the BLA to provide a deep characterization of nonlinear distortions in MZM-based IM-DD links, offering new insights into the statistical nature of these distortions and providing a methodology for optimizing system parameters to maximize performance.

## The Best Linear Approximation (BLA) Framework

The core principle of the BLA is to decompose the output of a nonlinear system into three distinct parts: a linear signal component, additive noise, and a residual component that represents the nonlinear distortion. For a sequence of received samples $y_n$, the BLA model provides the following decomposition:

$$y_n = \hat{y}_n + w_n + d_n$$

In this expression, $\hat{y}_n$ is the output of a linear time-invariant (LTI) filter that best approximates the system's behavior in a mean-squared error (MSE) sense. The term $w_n$ represents the additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) typically introduced by the receiver electronics, such as the transimpedance amplifier (TIA). The final term, $d_n$, is the orthogonal nonlinear distortion (ONL).

The term "orthogonal" is crucial here. It means that the nonlinear distortion $d_n$ is uncorrelated with the input symbols $x_n$. Mathematically, this is expressed as $E\{e_n x_{n-m}\} = 0$, where $e_n$ is the total error. This decomposition allows researchers to treat the nonlinear distortion as an additional noise source that effectively degrades the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). By isolating $d_n$, it becomes possible to analyze how much of the system's performance loss is due to linear effects (like bandwidth limitations), how much is due to thermal noise, and how much is strictly due to the nonlinear response of the modulator.

## System Architecture and Modeling

The researchers analyzed an IM-DD link operating at 112 GBd using PAM-4 modulation. The system begins with a transmitter where bits are mapped to symbols and converted to a continuous-time waveform. This waveform is then processed by a modulator driver and a low-pass filter before reaching the Mach-Zehnder Modulator.

The MZM is the primary source of nonlinearity in this study. Its operation is governed by an electro-optic transfer function that relates the applied voltage $v(t)$ to the transmitted optical power $P_{Tx}(t)$. When biased at the quadrature point (specifically $-V_{\pi}/2$), the relationship is:

$$P_{Tx}(t) = P_{cw} \cos^2\left(\frac{\pi v(t)}{2 V_{\pi}} - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)$$

where $P_{cw}$ is the continuous-wave laser power and $V_{\pi}$ is the voltage required to induce a $\pi$ phase shift. This sinusoidal relationship is fundamentally nonlinear; for small voltage swings, it appears linear, but as the signal amplitude increases, the output "compresses" as it follows the curve of the cosine function.

The receiver consists of a photodiode that converts light back into current $i(t)$, followed by a TIA modeled as a fourth-order Bessel filter with a bandwidth $BW_3 = 56 \text{ GHz}$. The thermal noise at the receiver is characterized by an input-referred noise current $I$. After the TIA, the signal is sampled and processed by digital equalizers—either a standard linear Feed-Forward Equalizer (FFE) or a more complex Volterra Nonlinear Equalizer (VNLE).

## Statistical Characterization of Distortion

A common assumption in communication theory is that any sufficiently complex noise source, including nonlinear distortion, can be approximated as Gaussian noise. However, this study challenges that assumption for MZM-based links. By analyzing the probability density function (PDF) of the orthogonal nonlinear distortion $d_n$, the researchers found that it is decidedly non-Gaussian.

When looking at the overall distribution of $d_n$, it might appear zero-mean. However, the study reveals that if we condition the distortion on specific transmitted PAM-4 symbols $x_{n-q}$, the story changes. The conditional PDFs show significant "mean shifts" ($\mu$). For example, when a symbol at the highest level ($x_{n-q} = 1$) is transmitted, the distortion $d_n$ has a negative mean. Conversely, for the lowest level ($x_{n-q} = -1$), the mean shift is positive.

This phenomenon occurs because the BLA filter is optimized to minimize the total error across *all* levels. Because the MZM transfer function compresses the signal more at the extreme voltage levels (the "top" and "bottom" of the PAM-4 eye), the best linear fit inevitably "overshoots" or "undershoots" those specific levels. This creates a deterministic residual at each symbol level. While these shifts cancel out across the entire sequence due to the symmetry of the PAM-4 constellation and the MZM curve, they represent a structured, non-Gaussian impairment that can impact the effectiveness of standard receiver algorithms.

## Optimizing Modulation Depth

One of the most practical applications of the BLA framework demonstrated in this work is the optimization of the modulation depth, denoted as $k$. The modulation depth represents the "swing" of the electrical signal relative to the MZM's $V_{\pi}$:

$$k = \frac{\max|u(t)|}{V_{\pi}}$$

Increasing $k$ has two competing effects. First, it increases the Optical Modulation Amplitude (OMA), which effectively boosts the signal strength against thermal noise at the receiver. This improves the SNR. Second, as $k$ grows, the signal travels further into the nonlinear regions of the MZM's sinusoidal curve, dramatically increasing the power of the nonlinear distortion $d_n$.

To find the optimal operating point, the researchers used the BLA to track the powers of different signal components. The total error power $J$ is the sum of the distortion power and the noise power:

$$J = E\{d_n^2\} + E\{w_n^2\}$$

The study identifies a critical modulation depth, $k^*$, where the power of the orthogonal nonlinear distortion $E\{d_n^2\}$ exactly matches the power of the receiver noise $E\{w_n^2\}$. For values of $k$ smaller than $k^*$, the system is "noise-limited," and increasing $k$ improves performance. For values of $k$ larger than $k^*$, the system becomes "nonlinearity-limited," where the gains in signal power are outweighed by the rapid growth of distortion. The results show that the minimum Bit Error Rate (BER) for the system occurs very close to this $k^*$ point, providing a clear design rule for setting the optimal drive voltage.

## Equalization and System Performance

The effectiveness of different equalization strategies depends heavily on which regime the system is operating in. The researchers compared a 112 GBd PAM-4 link using a standard 8-tap FFE versus a third-order VNLE.

In the noise-limited regime (low $k$), both equalizers perform similarly. This is because the primary impairment is additive noise $w_n$, which neither equalizer can "remove," only mitigate through linear filtering. However, as $k$ increases and the system enters the nonlinearity-limited regime, the VNLE demonstrates a clear advantage. Because the VNLE is designed to model and compensate for nonlinear kernels, it can partially undo the compression and distortion introduced by the MZM.

The BLA analysis helps quantify exactly how much "headroom" a nonlinear equalizer provides. While the VNLE can shift the optimal $k$ to slightly higher values, the ultimate performance limit is still dictated by the residual distortion that cannot be captured even by the nonlinear equalizer. The study shows that the BLA is a robust tool for predicting these performance trends across different noise levels (e.g., comparing TIAs with $5 \mu\text{A}$ vs. $8 \mu\text{A}$ of noise).

## Significance of the Work

This research provides a rigorous foundation for characterizing nonlinearities in the next generation of optical links. By moving away from simplistic RF metrics and embracing the BLA, engineers can gain a much more accurate picture of how hardware nonidealities interact with digital signals.

The findings are significant for several reasons. First, they disprove the common assumption that nonlinear distortion in these links is Gaussian, revealing a structured nature that could be exploited for better receiver design. Second, they provide a quantifiable methodology ($k^*$) for optimizing modulation depth, balancing the trade-off between signal strength and distortion. Finally, the work clarifies the roles of linear and nonlinear equalizers, showing that while advanced DSP can mitigate some distortion, the fundamental limits of the optical link are best understood through the lens of the Best Linear Approximation. As data rates continue to climb toward 200 Gbps per lane and beyond, such detailed characterization will be essential for maintaining the viability of cost-effective IM-DD technology.
