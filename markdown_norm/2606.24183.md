For large-scale industrial consumers, electricity is more than just a utility; it is a primary operational cost and a significant source of financial risk. As the energy landscape shifts toward variable renewable energy, electricity prices in day-ahead markets have become increasingly volatile. To mitigate these risks and potentially reduce costs, many industrial plants leverage "demand flexibility"—the ability to shift their electricity consumption in time without disrupting their core production processes.

However, participating in integrated European electricity markets requires consumers to submit bids that are compatible with the Pan-European Hybrid Electricity Market Integration Algorithm, known as EUPHEMIA. Translating complex industrial constraints (such as ramp rates, storage limits, and production targets) into the specific price-quantity formats required by EUPHEMIA while facing uncertain future prices remains a significant challenge. This paper develops a method for generating operationally feasible, EUPHEMIA-compatible bids for flexible industrial demand under imperfect information.

## The Mechanics of the Day-Ahead Market

The European day-ahead market clears once a day for the 24 hours of the following day. For industrial consumers, this creates a "look-ahead" problem: they must commit to a consumption schedule for tomorrow today, without knowing the actual prices that will be cleared. EUPHEMIA facilitates this by allowing several types of "complex bids."

The research focuses on two primary bid formats:
1.  **Hourly Bids (HBs):** These consist of price-quantity pairs for each specific hour. While they allow for granular responsiveness to prices in each hour, they do not explicitly link decisions across different hours.
2.  **Exclusive-group Bids (EBs):** These allow a consumer to submit a set of mutually exclusive consumption profiles (entire 24-hour schedules). The market algorithm then selects either one of these profiles in its entirety or none at all, based on whether the weighted average market price across those hours meets the bid's requirements.

The paper argues that the choice between these formats is not arbitrary; it depends heavily on the specific operational constraints of the industrial process and the prevailing volatility of the market.

## A Multi-Step Methodological Framework

To bridge the gap between industrial operations and market requirements, the paper proposes a four-step framework designed to handle price uncertainty and operational feasibility.

### 1. Price Forecasting and Residual Modeling
The first step involves predicting next-day electricity prices. The framework uses a Prophet-style decomposition model, which breaks down prices into seasonal components (daily, weekly, yearly) and incorporates exogenous variables like wind generation, solar generation, and total system load forecasts. To account for the "heavy tails" or extreme spikes often seen in electricity markets, the forecast residuals are modeled using a Lévy-stable distribution. This ensures that the model acknowledges the possibility of rare but impactful price events.

### 2. Scenario Generation and Reduction
Since a single "point forecast" is rarely accurate, the framework generates multiple plausible price trajectories (scenarios). These scenarios are created by adding correlated noise—drawn from the previously fitted Lévy-stable distribution—to the point forecast. Temporal correlations are imposed using a Cholesky factorization of a correlation matrix, ensuring that a price shock in one hour realistically affects subsequent hours. To keep the optimization problem computationally manageable, these scenarios are reduced to a representative subset using K-means clustering.

### 3. Risk-Averse Stochastic Optimization
With a set of price scenarios, the consumer must decide on a bidding strategy. The paper formulates this as a two-stage stochastic Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP). The goal is to minimize the total expected cost while controlling for "downside risk"—the risk of exceptionally high costs in the worst-case scenarios. This is achieved using the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) metric.

The objective function is expressed as:

$$\min \left[ (1 - \beta) \sum_{s \in S} \pi_s C_s + \beta \text{CVaR}_{\alpha}(C) \right]$$

In this expression, $\beta \in [0, 1]$ represents the consumer's risk aversion level, $\pi_s$ is the probability of scenario $s$, and $C_s$ is the operational cost in that scenario. The term $\text{CVaR}_{\alpha}$ focuses on the average cost in the $(1 - \alpha) \times 100\%$ worst cases.

### 4. Rolling-Horizon Implementation
In a real industrial setting, decisions made today affect the state of the plant (e.g., storage levels) tomorrow. The framework uses a rolling-horizon approach where a multi-day planning horizon is solved every day, but only the schedule for the immediate next 24 hours is implemented as a bid. After the market clears, the system state is updated, and the process repeats.

## Strategic Bidding: HBs vs. EBs

The core contribution of the paper is the detailed comparison of how different bid formats express flexibility.

### Hourly Bids (HBs)
In the HB strategy, the consumer submits a series of steps for each hour $h$. Each step $k$ has a quantity $q_{h,k}$ and a maximum price $p_{h,k}$ the consumer is willing to pay. For demand bids, these prices are non-increasing:

$$p_{h,k} \geq p_{h,k+1}$$

HBs allow for very precise price responsiveness on an hour-by-hour basis. However, because each hour is cleared independently, a plant might find its bids accepted in hour 1 but rejected in hour 2. For a system with strict ramping limits (the speed at which consumption can increase or decrease), this partial clearing can lead to operational infeasibility or high "redispatch" costs to correct the schedule.

### Exclusive-group Bids (EBs)
In the EB strategy, the consumer submits several complete 24-hour profiles. This "all-or-nothing" approach ensures that if a profile is accepted, it is operationally coherent across the entire day. The market clears a profile if the average price weighted by consumption is below the bid's threshold. While this protects the plant's operational feasibility, it is more "conservative" and may miss opportunities to avoid specific high-price hours if the rest of the profile remains economically attractive.

## Comparative Case Studies

The paper evaluates these strategies using two distinct industrial loads: a hydrogen electrolyzer and a steel plant.

### The Electrolyzer: High Flexibility
The electrolyzer is characterized by high ramping capabilities and hydrogen storage. It can quickly adjust its load from $5.5 \text{ MW}$ to $55 \text{ MW}$. 
- **Findings:** Under normal weather conditions, HBs performed slightly better as they allowed for precise hourly arbitrage. However, in "severe" weather years (periods of low renewable generation known as *Dunkelflaute*), EBs proved superior. The ability to offer diverse, pre-calculated 24-hour profiles allowed the electrolyzer to better navigate extreme price volatility.

### The Tata Steel Plant: Constrained Flexibility
The steel plant represents a more traditional industrial load with tighter operational dependencies, minimum load requirements, and stricter ramping limits.
- **Findings:** For the steel plant, HBs consistently resulted in lower costs than EBs across all weather scenarios. Because the plant's operational "maneuvering room" is narrow, the granular control offered by HBs allowed it to make small, precise adjustments that EBs—which require bundling hours together—could not match as efficiently.

## Practical Implications

The research highlights that the effectiveness of a bidding strategy is a function of both the industrial process's "flexibility profile" and the market's volatility.

For highly flexible loads like electrolyzers, which are expected to play a major role in the green hydrogen economy, the EUPHEMIA "Exclusive-group Bid" format offers a robust way to manage price risk in volatile markets. For more constrained, traditional industrial processes, refined "Hourly Bids" remain the more effective tool, provided they are generated through a risk-aware optimization process that considers the likelihood of partial clearing.

Furthermore, the paper addresses a critical gap in current market participation: the lack of short-term forward contracts. By providing a structured method to handle price uncertainty through scenario-based optimization, the framework allows industrial operators to participate in the day-ahead market with the same level of rigor as they would in a more certain environment. This not only reduces costs for the individual consumer but also enhances overall market efficiency by allowing the demand side to react more intelligently to supply-side fluctuations.
