## Introduction to Local Item Calibration

Operational testing programs, such as the Duolingo English Test, require a constant influx of new assessment items to replace retired ones, maintain security, and ensure the scale remains stable. To achieve this, "pilot items"—questions that do not contribute to an examinee's final score—are embedded into live test sessions. The goal is to collect enough data to estimate the psychometric properties of these new items, a process known as calibration.

In modern Computerized Adaptive Testing (CAT), the selection of these pilot items is often linked to the examinee's performance on the operational (scored) items. This "adaptive routing" creates a statistical challenge: the data for pilot items are not missing at random. To obtain accurate estimates, calibration models must account for the relationship between the operational responses and the pilot responses.

The most precise approach is joint calibration, where parameters for both thousands of operational items and hundreds of pilot items are estimated simultaneously. However, for large-scale platforms, this is computationally prohibitive. A common alternative is "local item calibration," where pilot items are estimated one by one using examinee proficiency estimates (proxies) derived from the operational items. While efficient, this simplification introduces "attenuation bias"—a systematic underestimation of the item's ability to discriminate between high and low proficiency learners. This paper introduces an analytically corrected Bayesian modularization framework to solve this bias while maintaining computational efficiency.

## The Challenge of Attenuation Bias

The standard model for item responses is the two-parameter logistic (2PL) model. For an examinee $i$ with latent trait proficiency $\theta_i$ and a pilot item $j$ with discrimination $a_j$ and intercept $d_j$, the probability of a correct response $Y_{ij} = 1$ is:

$$P(Y_{ij} = 1 \mid \theta_i) = \frac{\exp(a_j \theta_i + d_j)}{1 + \exp(a_j \theta_i + d_j)} = \text{logit}^{-1}(a_j \theta_i + d_j)$$

In local calibration, the true latent trait $\theta_i$ is unknown. Researchers instead use a proxy, such as the Expected A Posteriori (EAP) estimate $\bar{\theta}_i$ or a "plausible value" (PV) sampled from the examinee's posterior distribution. Because these proxies contain measurement error, they act like "noisy" predictors in a regression.

In classical statistics, using a noisy predictor causes the regression slope to shrink toward zero. In Item Response Theory (IRT), this means the estimated discrimination $a_j^*$ is consistently lower than the true $a_j$. The paper notes that for a discrimination $a_j$ and a scaling constant $D \approx 1.702$ (used to align logistic and normal distributions), the attenuated estimate approximately follows:

$$a_j^* = \frac{a_j}{\sqrt{1 + a_j^2 / D^2}} < a_j$$

Ignoring this error leads to items appearing less useful than they actually are, which can degrade the precision of the overall test once these items are promoted to the operational bank.

## Bayesian Modularization and Plausible Values

The authors frame their approach using "Bayesian modularization." The idea is to split the joint model into two modules:
1.  **The Operational Module:** Contains the high-stakes, well-calibrated item bank and the examinee proficiency parameters.
2.  **The Pilot Module:** Contains the new, uncalibrated items.

The modular approach ensures that "feedback" only flows from the operational module to the pilot module, not vice versa. This prevents a malfunctioning pilot item from distorting the established operational scale—a protection called "tautological contamination" prevention.

To implement this, the authors use Posterior Imputation. For each examinee, they draw $S$ independent Plausible Values (PVs) from the posterior distribution of $\theta_i$ conditioned on their operational responses. Instead of performing one complex multidimensional integration, they perform $S$ simple logistic regressions. Specifically, for each pilot item $j$, they solve:

$$L(\eta_j) = \int p(y_j \mid \theta, \eta_j) p(\theta \mid y_{op}, \eta_{op}) d\theta$$

where $\eta_j = \{a_j, d_j\}$ are the item parameters. By averaging results across these multiple imputations (using Rubin's Rules), they can capture the uncertainty inherent in the latent trait estimates.

## Analytic Disattenuation Mappings

The core contribution of the paper is a set of closed-form equations that "stretch" the attenuated estimates back to their true values without needing the heavy numerical integration required by existing methods like the "One Expectation-Maximization" (OEM) cycle.

The authors derive expansion factors based on the reliability of the trait proxies. For Point Estimate Local Calibration (PELC), which uses EAP estimates $\bar{\theta}_i$ directly, the expansion factor $\lambda_{PELC,j}$ depends on the empirical reliability $\bar{\rho}_j$ of the examinees who saw item $j$:

$$\lambda_{PELC,j} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar{\rho}_j - (a_j^*)^2 \sigma_{\delta, j}^2 / D^2}}$$

where $\bar{\rho}_j$ is defined as:

$$\bar{\rho}_j = \frac{\text{Var}(\bar{\theta}_i \mid i \in I_j)}{\text{Var}(\bar{\theta}_i \mid i \in I_j) + \bar{\sigma}_j^2} \approx \frac{\text{Var}(\text{True } \theta)}{\text{Var}(\text{True } \theta) + \text{Error}}$$

For Modular Local Calibration (MLC), which uses PVs, a different correction factor is applied to account for the specific "regression-to-the-mean" effects unique to posterior sampling. The corrected parameters are then recovered as $a_j = \lambda_j a_j^*$ and $d_j = \lambda_j d_j^* - \text{CorrectionTerm}$.

## Handling Sparse Data with Firth’s Penalty

Pilot items are often administered to a small number of examinees (e.g., $N=50$ or $100$). In such small samples, logistic regression often suffers from "separation," where a predictor perfectly distinguishes between 0 and 1 responses, causing the parameter estimates to go to infinity.

To handle this, the authors incorporate **Firth's penalized likelihood**. This method adds a penalty term based on the Jeffreys prior to the likelihood function. It ensures that estimates remain finite even in sparse data settings. A key finding of the paper is that this penalty, combined with the analytic correction, significantly reduces the Mean Squared Error (MSE) for small pilot samples compared to standard maximum likelihood approaches.

## Results and Performance

The authors conducted an extensive Monte Carlo simulation to compare their proposed Modular Local Calibration (MLC) and Point Estimate Local Calibration (PELC) methods against existing standards:
*   **Method A:** A common industry standard that ignores measurement error.
*   **OEM:** A more complex method that uses numerical integration.
*   **FFMLE:** A method designed for random pilot assignment.

The results showed that **Method A** suffered from severe, persistent bias (underestimating discrimination by about 40%) that did not go away even as the pilot sample size increased to 1,000. In contrast, the corrected MLC and PELC methods achieved near-zero bias once the pilot sample reached a moderate size.

Furthermore, the study looked at confidence interval coverage. A good statistical method should produce a 95% confidence interval that contains the true value 95% of the time. Method A's coverage collapsed toward zero as more data was added because it became "precisely wrong"—highly certain about an incorrect, attenuated value. The corrected MLC method maintained nominal coverage (near 95-98%), indicating that its standard errors correctly accounted for the uncertainty in the operational bank.

## Significance and Future Directions

The "Analytically Corrected Bayesian Modularization" provides a "best of both worlds" solution for large-scale testing. It offers the speed and scalability of local calibration (one item at a time) while achieving the statistical accuracy of more complex, integrated methods.

Key takeaways for the field include:
1.  **Computational Efficiency:** By using closed-form analytic corrections, practitioners can avoid the high cost of numerical integration per item.
2.  **Protection of the Scale:** The modular design ensures that the operational item bank remains a "gold standard" that is never corrupted by pilot data.
3.  **Robustness:** The integration of Firth's penalty allows for reliable calibration even when pilot data is very limited.

While this work focused on the 2PL model, the authors suggest that the framework could be extended to more complex models, such as those that account for guessing (3PL) or multidimensional traits. This research provides a roadmap for maintaining high-quality assessment scales in the era of high-volume, adaptive testing.
