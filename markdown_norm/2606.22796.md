## Bridging the Gap in Option Pricing

The Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) model is a cornerstone of modern quantitative finance, providing a standardized framework for the valuation of European options. However, the model is built upon simplifying assumptions that frequently diverge from the complexities of actual market behavior. Primary among these is the assumption of constant volatility and the belief that asset prices follow a Geometric Brownian Motion (GBM), which assumes continuous price paths and log-normal distributions of returns.

In practice, financial markets exhibit "volatility smiles"—where implied volatility varies across different strike prices—and sudden "jumps" in price that a continuous model cannot capture. To address these discrepancies, researchers have turned to more sophisticated stochastic processes. This paper explores an enhancement of the BSM framework by integrating Lévy processes and Malliavin calculus. By incorporating stochastic volatility with nested jumps, the model aims to provide a more accurate representation of the market's underlying dynamics.

## The Limitations of Classical Models

The BSM model's reliance on constant volatility implies that the risk of an asset remains uniform over the life of an option. Empirical evidence suggests otherwise; volatility is mean-reverting and often clusters, meaning high-volatility periods are followed by more high-volatility periods. Furthermore, the log-normal distribution assumed by BSM fails to account for "fat tails"—the higher-than-predicted probability of extreme price movements.

These limitations manifest as the volatility smile or skew. If the BSM assumptions were correct, the implied volatility for options on the same asset with the same maturity but different strikes would be identical. Instead, the market typically prices out-of-the-money options with higher implied volatilities, reflecting a demand for protection against tail risk or large downward jumps. Traditional extensions, like pure stochastic volatility models, improve upon BSM but often struggle to capture the suddenness of market crashes or the steepness of the volatility skew without the inclusion of a jump component.

## A Two-Dimensional Jump-Diffusion Framework

To resolve these issues, the paper proposes a two-dimensional model that simultaneously tracks the log-price of an asset and a volatility proxy. This model uses a Lévy process to drive common jump shocks in both variables, allowing for a correlated jump in price and volatility—a common occurrence during market stress.

The log-price $X_1$ is modeled as a semimartingale with the following dynamics:

$$dX_{1,t} = \left( r - q - \frac{1}{2}\sigma_t^2 - \kappa_t \right) dt + \sigma_t dW_{1,t} + \int_{\mathbb{R}} \eta(t,z) \tilde{N}(dt, dz)$$

In this expression, $r$ is the risk-free rate, $q$ is the dividend yield, and $\sigma_t$ represents the instantaneous stochastic volatility. The term $\kappa_t$ is a jump compensator that ensures the discounted price remains a martingale under the risk-neutral measure. The final term represents the jump component, where $\tilde{N}$ is a compensated Poisson random measure and $\eta$ governs the jump size.

Simultaneously, a variance factor $Y_t$ is defined as a mean-reverting process with jumps:

$$dY_t = \alpha(m - Y_t) dt + c dW_{2,t} + \int_{\mathbb{R}} \xi(t,z) N(dt, dz)$$

Here, $\alpha$ is the speed of mean reversion, $m$ is the long-run mean, and $c$ is the volatility-of-volatility. The volatility proxy $X_2$ used in the valuation is then defined as a scaled version of this factor:

$$X_{2,t} = \sqrt{\frac{Y_t}{2n(T + \delta - t)}}$$

This dual structure allows the model to capture both the continuous evolution of prices and the discrete shocks that characterize real-world financial data.

## Deriving the Enhanced Option Pricing Formula

The primary theoretical contribution of the paper is the derivation of a European call option pricing formula that incorporates these complex dynamics. By applying the multidimensional Itô-Lévy formula, the authors decompose the option price $V_t$ into the standard Black-Scholes price $u(t, X_t)$ and a correction term:

$$V_t = u(t, X_t) + E \left[ \int_t^T e^{-r(s-t)} C_s ds \mid \mathcal{F}_t \right]$$

The "Correction Operator" $C_s$ is the engine of this model, quantifying exactly how the price deviates from BSM due to three factors:

$$C_s = \frac{1}{2}(\sigma_s^2 - x_{2,s}^2) \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x_1^2} + \mathcal{A}_s u + \int_{\mathbb{R}} \left( u(s, X_{s-} + \Delta X_s) - u(s, X_{s-}) - \nabla u \cdot \Delta X_s \right) \nu(dz)$$

1.  **Variance Mismatch:** The first term accounts for the difference between the actual instantaneous volatility $\sigma_s^2$ and the volatility input $x_{2,s}^2$ used in the BSM component.
2.  **Time-Scaling Drift:** The operator $\mathcal{A}_s$ accounts for the deterministic time-scaling inherent in the definition of the volatility proxy.
3.  **Jump Compensator:** The integral term captures the non-linear effect of price jumps. It measures the difference between the option's value after a jump and a linear approximation of that change, effectively pricing the "convexity" or tail risk introduced by the Lévy process.

## Malliavin Calculus and Implied Volatility

A significant challenge in quantitative finance is moving from a complex model price back to an implied volatility—the single volatility parameter that, when plugged into the standard BSM formula, yields the model's price. Because the BSM formula cannot be analytically inverted for volatility in all cases, the authors employ Malliavin calculus to derive an exact expression for At-The-Money (ATM) implied volatility.

Malliavin calculus, or the calculus of variations on Wiener space, provides tools to analyze the sensitivity of random variables to the underlying noise (Brownian motion and jumps). The authors use the Clark-Ocone formula to express the option price as a combination of its expected value and stochastic integrals of Malliavin derivatives.

By applying the Itô formula to the inverse Black-Scholes function $BS^{-1}$, they derive a formula for the implied volatility $\sigma_{imp}$ where:

$$\sigma_{imp} = BS^{-1}(C_{ATM}, T)$$

This expression involves Malliavin derivatives $D_s$ (for the Gaussian part) and $D_{s,z}$ (for the jump part) of the volatility process. When specialized to the Stein-Stein model—where volatility follows an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process—the derivation yields a concrete formula that relates the market's implied volatility to the parameters of the mean-reverting stochastic process.

## Empirical Validation and Calibration

To test the model's practical utility, the authors calibrated a Stein-Stein stochastic volatility model using VIX index data from 2022. The VIX is a market-derived measure of expected 30-day volatility and serves as a proxy for ATM implied volatility.

The calibration yielded the following parameters:
*   **Mean-reversion speed ($\hat{\alpha}$):** $31.0046$
*   **Long-run mean ($\hat{m}$):** $0.2609$
*   **Vol-of-vol ($\hat{c}$):** $0.3153$

The high value of $\hat{\alpha}$ suggests that volatility shocks in the 2022 market environment were short-lived, with a half-life of roughly 5.6 trading days. While the model (as shown in {fig:atm-implied-volatility-market-vs-model-proxy}) successfully tracked the general trend of market volatility, it produced a much smoother path than the actual VIX. Specifically, the diffusion-based model failed to capture the sharp, sudden spikes in volatility seen during periods of high market stress.

This empirical gap highlights the necessity of the jump component proposed in the theoretical section of the paper. A pure diffusion model like Stein-Stein reacts continuously to new information, whereas the market often reacts discontinuously. By adding Lévy jumps, the model gains the ability to "snap" to new volatility levels instantly, better matching the behavior of the VIX during crises.

## Conclusion and Significance

This research provides a rigorous mathematical bridge between the idealized world of Black-Scholes and the reality of jump-prone, stochastic-volatility markets. By integrating Lévy processes, the model explicitly accounts for the extreme events that the original BSM model ignores. The use of Malliavin calculus allows for a high degree of precision in deriving implied volatility, offering a theoretical framework that can be adapted to various stochastic models beyond just the Stein-Stein case.

For practitioners, this framework offers a more granular way to decompose option prices. By understanding the specific contributions of variance mismatch and jump compensation, risk managers can better hedge against tail risks and volatility fluctuations. While the calibration exercise demonstrated the limitations of pure diffusion processes, it reinforced the value of the paper's overarching goal: creating a unified model that handles both the smooth evolution of prices and the sudden shocks that define financial markets.
