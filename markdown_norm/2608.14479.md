Integrated Sensing and Communication (ISAC) has emerged as a fundamental pillar for future wireless networks, such as 6G. The core premise of ISAC is the dual use of wireless signals: transmitting information while simultaneously sensing the physical environment. By analyzing how signals reflect off surfaces and interact with objects, receivers can infer geometric data, including device positions and the locations of reflecting surfaces. While previous research has successfully used this sensing data to improve beamforming, channel estimation, and pilot overhead, its direct application to the channel decoding process—the final step in recovering transmitted data—has remained largely underexplored.

Channel decoding performance relies heavily on the quality of soft information, typically expressed as Log-Likelihood Ratios (LLRs). These ratios represent the receiver's confidence in whether a specific bit was a 0 or a 1. In conventional systems, LLRs are derived from channel estimates produced solely by pilot signals. However, when pilots are scarce or the signal-to-noise ratio is low, these estimates become unreliable, leading to decoding errors. This paper proposes a framework to integrate environmental sensing information directly into the decoding pipeline of Ordered Reliability Bits Guessing Random Additive Noise Decoding (ORBGRAND). By using geometric priors to refine channel estimates, the system generates more accurate LLRs, significantly reducing both the Block Error Rate (BLER) and the computational effort required for decoding.

## The Mechanics of ORBGRAND

To understand the impact of sensing, one must first understand the decoding algorithm it assists. Guessing Random Additive Noise Decoding (GRAND) is a universal decoding paradigm that departs from traditional code-specific algorithms. Instead of trying to decode the structure of the code directly, GRAND attempts to "guess" the noise that corrupted the transmission.

The process begins with the received signal and the calculated LLRs. A hard-decision vector is created by taking the most likely bit value for each position. The decoder then generates noise patterns, starting from the most likely (e.g., no noise) and moving toward increasingly complex patterns. For each noise pattern, the decoder applies it to the hard-decision vector and checks if the resulting sequence is a valid codeword in the codebook. The first valid codeword found is identified as the transmitted message.

ORBGRAND is a sophisticated variant of this approach that uses soft information to optimize the order of guesses. It sorts the bits based on their reliability—the magnitude of their LLRs. Bits with low LLR magnitudes are deemed "unreliable" and are targeted first in the guessing process. This prioritized approach ensures that the decoder finds the correct codeword with fewer queries, provided the reliability ordering is accurate. If the LLRs are calculated using a poor channel estimate, the reliability ordering becomes flawed, causing the decoder to search through thousands of incorrect patterns before finding the right one, or failing entirely.

## Geometry-Based Channel Priors

The unique contribution of this work is the construction of a channel "prior" derived from environmental sensing. In a narrowband fading channel, the channel coefficient $h$ represents the total effect of the medium on the signal. The authors model this coefficient as a sum of three components:

$$
h = h_{LoS} + h_{NLoS} + h_{res} \sim \mathcal{CN}(\mu, \sigma^2)
$$

In this model, $h_{LoS}$ is the Line-of-Sight component, $h_{NLoS}$ is the Non-Line-of-Sight component (often a reflection from a wall or object), and $h_{res}$ is the residual component representing unmodeled scattering. 

Sensing information provides the geometric parameters necessary to calculate these components. If the receiver knows its own position $x_{Rx}$ relative to the transmitter $x_{Tx}$, it can calculate the expected gain and phase of the $h_{LoS}$ path. If it also knows the location of a "virtual anchor" $x_{VA}$—a point representing a reflecting surface—it can similarly predict the $h_{NLoS}$ path. 

This geometric knowledge transforms the channel coefficient from a completely unknown variable into a random variable with a known prior distribution. This prior, denoted as $p(h|I_{sen}) \sim \mathcal{CN}(\mu_{sen}, \sigma_{sen}^2)$, encapsulates what the receiver "expects" the channel to look like before it even observes any pilot symbols.

## Fusing Sensing with Pilot Observations

In a typical communication burst, the receiver also receives a set of known pilot symbols. These pilots allow the receiver to perform a standard Least-Squares (LS) estimation. If the transmitted pilot symbols are $x_p$ and the received signals are $y_p$, the LS estimate is:

$$
\hat{h}_{LS} = \frac{x_p^H y_p}{E_p} \sim \mathcal{CN}(h, \sigma_{LS}^2) \text{ where } \sigma_{LS}^2 = \frac{\sigma_w^2}{E_p}
$$

The proposed framework uses a Linear Minimum Mean Square Error (LMMSE) estimator to fuse the sensing-based prior with the pilot-based LS estimate. This fusion results in a refined channel estimate $\hat{h}_{sen}$ and a reduced posterior variance $\sigma_{post}^2$:

$$
\hat{h}_{sen} = \mu_{sen} + \frac{\sigma_{sen}^2}{\sigma_{sen}^2 + \sigma_{LS}^2}(\hat{h}_{LS} - \mu_{sen})
$$

$$
\sigma_{post}^2 = \frac{\sigma_{sen}^2 \sigma_{LS}^2}{\sigma_{sen}^2 + \sigma_{LS}^2}
$$

This refined estimate is then used to compute the LLRs $L_i$ for each bit $i$ in the data block:

$$
L_i = \ln \frac{P(y_i|c_i=0)}{P(y_i|c_i=1)} \approx \frac{4\text{Re}\{\hat{h}^*y_i\}}{\sigma_{eff}^2}
$$

Where the effective noise variance $\sigma_{eff}^2$ accounts for both the background noise and the remaining uncertainty in the channel estimate:

$$
\sigma_{eff}^2 \leq \sigma_w^2 + \sigma_{post}^2
$$

By incorporating the sensing prior, the receiver produces LLRs that are more representative of the true bit probabilities. This leads to a more accurate reliability ordering, which is the engine that drives ORBGRAND's efficiency.

## Performance Gains and Complexity Reduction

The authors evaluated this sensing-aided framework using an extended Bose-Chaudhuri-Hocquenghem (eBCH) code. They compared several scenarios: a "pilot-only" receiver (the baseline), an "Rx-aided" receiver (sensing only the receiver's position), and an "Rx+VA-aided" receiver (sensing both receiver and reflecting surface positions).

The results demonstrate that environmental awareness translates directly into communication performance. The Rx+VA-aided receiver achieved a Block Error Rate very close to the "perfect CSI" benchmark (where the channel is perfectly known). In practical terms, this provided a 1.8 dB gain over the standard pilot-only approach.

Beyond error rates, the integration of sensing significantly reduced the "query complexity"—the number of guesses the decoder must make before finding the correct codeword. When the channel estimate is poor, the decoder often explores a vast space of unlikely noise patterns. With sensing information, the reliability ordering becomes so accurate that the correct noise pattern is found much earlier. At an $E_s/N_0$ of 4 dB, the Rx+VA-aided scheme reduced the average query count by approximately 18% compared to the pilot-only baseline.

## The Impact of Pilot Scarcity and Information Accuracy

One of the most significant findings involves the relationship between sensing and pilot overhead. In modern high-speed communications, pilot symbols consume bandwidth that could otherwise be used for data. The authors found that sensing-aided ORBGRAND performs remarkably well even when the number of pilots is extremely small. 

For instance, an Rx+VA-aided receiver using only one or two pilot symbols can outperform a standard receiver using four or eight pilots. This suggests that sensing data can act as a substitute for pilot symbols, allowing systems to maintain high reliability while reducing overhead and increasing spectral efficiency.

However, the paper also highlights a critical caveat: the accuracy of the sensing information is paramount. The authors tested a "mismatched prior" scenario where the receiver possessed inaccurate information about the reflecting surface. In this case, the biased prior actually misled the estimator, resulting in worse performance than the pilot-only baseline at high signal-to-noise ratios. This underscores the need for robust sensing algorithms or "uncertainty-aware" priors that can account for potential errors in the geometric data.

## Conclusion

This work establishes a clear link between environmental sensing and universal channel decoding. By showing that geometric information can be converted into a mathematical prior for channel estimation, the authors demonstrate a path for ISAC systems to improve the very core of the communication receiver. The framework does not require changes to the underlying code or the ORBGRAND algorithm itself; instead, it optimizes the "fuel"—the LLRs—that the decoder consumes.

The ability to maintain low error rates and low computational complexity even with minimal pilot symbols makes this approach particularly relevant for future low-latency and high-efficiency wireless standards. As sensing capabilities become more integrated into hardware, the use of environmental context to aid decoding represents a promising direction for achieving more resilient wireless links.
