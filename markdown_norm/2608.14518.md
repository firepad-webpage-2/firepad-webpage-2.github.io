## The Challenge of Hydrogen in Sub-Neptunes

Sub-Neptunes are the most common type of exoplanet discovered to date, characterized by sizes between Earth and Neptune. These planets are generally understood to consist of a dense, silicate-rich core (a "magma ocean" in their early, hot stages) surrounded by a thick, hydrogen-rich gas envelope. A central question in planetary science is how much of that hydrogen actually enters the core. While thermodynamic calculations suggest that hydrogen should dissolve readily into molten rock at high pressures, the physical process of getting hydrogen from the atmosphere into the deep interior faces a significant mechanical hurdle.

*{fig:convective-surface-renewal-mechanism}: A schematic of the convective surface renewal process. Thermal cooling at the surface drives convective overturn with velocity $v_{conv}$, bringing fresh melt to the interface where hydrogen diffuses inward before being entrained into the deep magma ocean.*

Recent studies have proposed that sub-Neptune cores might contain several percent hydrogen by mass. If true, this would fundamentally change our understanding of their density, internal structure, and even whether they have distinct iron cores. However, traditional models of passive diffusion—where hydrogen simply leaks into the melt—predict the formation of a "buoyancy barrier." Because hydrogen is extremely light, adding it to the top layer of a magma ocean makes that layer less dense. This creates a stable, buoyant lid that floats on top, preventing the rest of the magma ocean from mixing and effectively "locking out" further hydrogen uptake. This paper investigates an intrinsic physical mechanism—convective surface renewal—that allows planets to overcome this barrier and engulf significant amounts of hydrogen during their formation and cooling.

## The Buoyancy Barrier and the Need for Surface Renewal

In a cooling planet, the magma ocean is kept well-mixed by thermal convection. Hot material rises from the deep interior, cools at the surface, becomes denser, and sinks back down. This process is driven by thermal buoyancy, $B_T$. However, the dissolution of hydrogen introduces a competing effect: compositional buoyancy, $B_X$.

When hydrogen from the envelope dissolves into the surface melt, it significantly reduces the melt's density. If the rate of hydrogen uptake is high enough, the compositional buoyancy $B_X$ can become stronger than the thermal buoyancy $B_T$. The net buoyancy $B_{net}$ is given by:

$$
B_{net} = B_T - B_X
$$

If $B_{net}$ drops to zero or becomes negative, convection stops near the surface. The light, hydrogen-rich melt stays at the top, and further hydrogen can only enter the core through slow molecular diffusion, which would take billions of years to reach the deep interior. To explain the hydrogen-rich cores inferred by recent research, a mechanism is needed that can continuously "renew" the surface, bringing fresh, hydrogen-poor melt to the interface to be enriched before the buoyancy barrier can fully arrest the process.

## Mechanism: Convective Surface Renewal

The author applies the Danckwerts-type surface renewal model to the magma ocean-atmosphere interface. In this framework, the interface is not a static boundary. Instead, it is viewed as a collection of fluid parcels that are periodically replaced by the ocean's internal convection. Each parcel stays at the surface for a brief time $t$, during which hydrogen diffuses into it, before it is swept back into the deep interior.

The efficiency of this mass transfer is governed by the mass transfer coefficient $k_L$, which is calculated as:

$$
k_L = \sqrt{D s}
$$

where $D$ is the molecular diffusivity of hydrogen in the melt and $s$ is the surface renewal rate. The renewal rate $s$ is linked to the physical dynamics of the magma ocean:

$$
s = v_{conv} / d_{ocean}
$$

In this equation, $v_{conv}$ is the convective overturn velocity and $d_{ocean}$ is the depth of the magma ocean. Unlike simpler "film" models where the transfer rate depends linearly on $D$, this model's $\sqrt{D}$ dependence reflects the transient nature of the contact between the melt and the gas. As long as the planet is cooling and driving convection, $v_{conv}$ remains non-zero, and the surface is continuously renewed.

## Modeling Planetary Evolution: The Engulfment Front

The paper models the uptake of hydrogen as a moving "engulfment front," denoted by the radius $r_\phi$. As hydrogen enters the melt, the total volume of the core expands, and the concentration of hydrogen in the interior, $X_{melt}$, increases. The flux of hydrogen $J_H$ into the melt is defined as:

$$
J_H = \rho k_L (X_{surf} - X_{melt})
$$

where $\rho$ is the melt density and $X_{surf}$ is the hydrogen mass fraction at the interface. The diffusivity $D$ is also adjusted by a factor $\Phi$ (the Darken factor) to account for the strong chemical potential gradients at high pressure:

$$
D = \Phi D_{self}
$$

A critical finding of the methodology is that the system is self-regulating. The ingassing flux $J_H$ is limited by a critical value $J_{crit}$ at which compositional buoyancy would perfectly cancel thermal buoyancy. By solving the balance of these forces, the model determines that the core engulfs hydrogen at a rate that is limited by the planet's cooling rate but never completely stopped. The energy required to mix the buoyant, hydrogen-rich surface material into the dense interior is provided by the kinetic energy of the convection itself.

## Thermodynamic Limits and the Role of the Binodal

The maximum amount of hydrogen the melt can hold is not infinite; it is governed by a thermodynamic boundary called the binodal. At high temperatures and pressures, hydrogen and silicates can become completely miscible, forming a single supercritical fluid. As the planet cools, this mixture eventually reaches a point where it wants to separate into two distinct phases: a hydrogen-rich gas and a silicate-rich melt.

![engulfment-schematic](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.14518v1/Engulfment_schematic_2.png)
*{fig:engulfment-schematic}: The evolution of the engulfment process. (Left) Initial state with a hydrogen-free core. (Center) The "growth phase" where an engulfment front $r_\phi$ moves outward as H2 is rapidly absorbed. (Right) The "equilibrium phase" where the front meets the binodal and follows its migration inward as the planet cools.*

The paper identifies two distinct regimes in a planet's life:
1.  **The Growth Phase:** Early in the planet's life (often while it is still embedded in the protoplanetary disk), the core is extremely hot and the chemical drive to absorb hydrogen is high. An "engulfment front" $r_\phi$ moves rapidly outward from the center, consuming hydrogen and expanding the core.
2.  **The Equilibrium Phase:** As the planet cools, the binodal (the thermodynamic limit) moves inward. Eventually, the outward-moving engulfment front meets the inward-moving binodal. From this point on, the core's hydrogen content is dictated by thermodynamic equilibrium. As the planet continues to cool over billions of years, the core may actually "exsolve" or release some hydrogen back into the atmosphere.

## Key Results: Where the Hydrogen Goes

The numerical simulations track a 6 Earth-mass ($6 M_\oplus$) planet over 4 billion years. The results show that hydrogen engulfment is most aggressive during the first few million years (the disk phase).

![evolution-log-scale](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.14518v1/6M_10xH2_7day_log.png)
*{fig:evolution-log-scale}: Logarithmic time evolution of a $6 M_\oplus$ planet. Panel A shows the rapid rise of interior hydrogen $X_{melt}$ (green line) during the disk phase (shaded region). Panel C shows the outward movement of the engulfment front $r_\phi$.*

Key observations from the modeling include:
*   **Mass Fractions:** During the disk phase, the core can reach hydrogen mass fractions of ~3.4%. This means that in a typical sub-Neptune, as much as 50% of the planet's total hydrogen could be hidden inside the core rather than in the atmosphere.
*   **Long-term Stability:** After the disk disappears, the core remains hydrogen-rich for billions of years. While cooling causes the core to release some hydrogen (dropping from ~3.4% to ~2.0% mass fraction), a significant reservoir remains trapped.
*   **Cooling Dependence:** The process is "cooling-limited." If the planet stays hot (a "hot start"), it absorbs more hydrogen. If it starts cooler, the convective driving force is weaker, and the total hydrogen uptake is reduced.

## Implications for Exoplanet Characterization

The ability of convective surface renewal to facilitate deep hydrogen engulfment has several major implications for how we interpret exoplanet observations:

1.  **The Radius Gap:** The transition between sub-Neptunes and super-Earths (the "radius gap") is often explained by the loss of hydrogen envelopes. If those cores are hydrogen-rich, they will be less dense than a standard rocky Earth. A "stripped" sub-Neptune core might appear as an unusually large, low-density super-Earth.
2.  **Internal Differentiation:** At the high hydrogen concentrations predicted (several percent by weight), silicates, iron, and hydrogen may become a single, well-mixed supercritical fluid. This suggests that young sub-Neptunes might not have a distinct iron core like Earth does; instead, they might be a homogeneous "soup" of materials that only differentiates as the planet cools and hydrogen is released.
3.  **Atmospheric Chemistry:** Because the core and atmosphere are in constant exchange via surface renewal, the chemical composition of the atmosphere we see with telescopes like JWST is directly influenced by the chemistry of the deep interior. 

By identifying an intrinsic physical mechanism for hydrogen uptake, this research provides a robust framework for understanding why sub-Neptunes look the way they do and how their interiors evolve over geological time. Convective surface renewal effectively bridges the gap between the rapid, violent processes of planet formation and the slow, billion-year cooling that shapes the worlds we observe today.
