## High-Order Numerical Modeling and Adaptive Refinement

Numerical modeling of partial differential equations (PDEs) is a fundamental task in scientific computing, especially in fields like fluid dynamics, meteorology, and astrophysics. To achieve high accuracy, researchers often turn to high-order discretization methods, which use high-degree polynomials to approximate solutions. The Discontinuous Galerkin Spectral-Element Method (DG-SEM) is one such approach that combines the flexibility of finite element methods with the high-order accuracy of spectral methods. However, high-order methods pose significant challenges for linear solvers, particularly when applied to complex geometries or meshes that require local refinement.

A major hurdle in adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) is the occurrence of "hanging nodes" at the interfaces between refined and unrefined elements. Managing these nodes typically requires complex modifications to the discrete operators or limits the mesh to specific types. This work addresses these challenges by developing an adaptive multigrid method based on the Full Approximation Scheme (FAS). By integrating FAS with DG-SEM on hexahedral meshes, the proposed method allows for local polynomial ($p$) and mesh ($h$) refinement without the need for hanging nodes, maintaining a consistent and conservative formulation across multiple resolution levels.

## The Full Approximation Scheme in DG-SEM

The core innovation of this research is the adaptation of the Full Approximation Scheme (FAS) to the high-order DG-SEM framework. Unlike standard multigrid methods that solve for the error (or correction) on coarser levels, FAS stores and solves for the full solution on every level. This characteristic is particularly advantageous for adaptive meshes because it allows the method to confine calculations to spatially localized refinement zones.

In this multilevel formulation, the domain is represented by a sequence of meshes $\{\Omega_l\}_{l=1}^L$. The root level ($l=1$) covers the entire computational domain, while finer levels consist of "active elements" where high resolution is required. To maintain boundary conditions at refinement interfaces without hanging nodes, the method introduces "frozen elements." These are buffer layers surrounding the active refinement zones that carry solution values interpolated from the parent level.

The global algebraic system for the model elliptic problem is represented as:

$$\kappa u - \nabla \cdot \nu \nabla u = f$$

which, after discretization, results in the system $\bar{A}\bar{u} = \bar{f}$. The FAS algorithm proceeds by performing smoothing operations on the current level, restricting the full solution and its residual to the next coarser level, solving the coarse problem, and then interpolating the correction back to the fine level. Because the transfer operators—interpolation ($\bar{I}_l$), restriction ($\bar{R}^t_l$), and projection ($\bar{P}^t_l$)—are defined consistently within the DG framework, the method preserves the underlying physics of the problem across scales.

## Robust Smoothers and Fast Diagonalization

The efficiency of any multigrid method depends heavily on its "smoother," the component responsible for removing high-frequency errors. For high-order methods on unstructured and potentially distorted meshes, designing an effective smoother is difficult. This work utilizes a Weighted Additive Schwarz (WAS) smoother, which decomposes the domain into overlapping subdomains. Each subdomain consists of a central element and a layer of its neighbors, including those connected via faces, edges, and vertices.

To ensure computational efficiency, the subproblems within each subdomain must be solved rapidly. The researchers employ a Fast Diagonalization (FD) method. By approximating the subdomain operator using a Cartesian surrogate domain, the tensor-product structure of the DG-SEM basis is preserved. This allows the multidimensional problem to be decomposed into a series of one-dimensional operations, significantly reducing the cost of inverting the local matrices.

To further enhance robustness against mesh anisotropy (elements with high aspect ratios) and curvature, the Schwarz smoother is accelerated using a Krylov method, specifically the Inexact Preconditioned Conjugate Gradient (IPCG) algorithm. This Schwarz-preconditioned Conjugate Gradient (SCG) approach ensures stable convergence even when the mesh is highly irregular or the elements are significantly deformed.

## Implementing Hierarchical Startup Strategies

Solving large-scale PDE systems requires not just a fast iterative solver, but also a good starting guess. This paper evaluates two hierarchical startup strategies: Cascadic Multigrid (FAS-CMG) and Full Multigrid (FAS-FMG).

FAS-FMG, in particular, proves highly effective. It begins by solving the problem on the coarsest level and then progressively introduces finer levels. At each step, it performs FAS V-cycles and interpolates the resulting solution to serve as the initial guess for the next level. This "nested iteration" approach often allows the solver to reach the level of discretization error in just one or two full cycles, dramatically reducing the total time to solution compared to starting from a zero initial guess on the finest mesh.

The implementation of these strategies is integrated into the HiSPEET library, which uses space-filling curves (specifically Hilbert curves) for element ordering. This ensures that the parent-child relationships in the mesh hierarchy are preserved in memory, facilitating efficient data transfer between levels and enabling effective parallelization across multiple processors.

## Experimental Results and Performance Analysis

The proposed adaptive multigrid method was tested against several benchmarks designed to stress-test its robustness and efficiency. These included uniform Cartesian grids, curved spherical shells, and highly irregular "diamond" meshes.

### Robustness Against Anisotropy and Distortion

One of the most significant findings is the method's resilience to high aspect ratios. In simulations involving Cartesian meshes with aspect ratios up to 48, the SCG-accelerated FAS-FMG solver maintained a nearly constant iteration count ($\leq 4$). In contrast, standard smoothers without Krylov acceleration often became unstable or required an impractical number of iterations as the elements became thinner.

The method also demonstrated strong performance on complex geometries. Using a mesh representing a linear compressor blade—characterized by curved elements and irregular connectivity—the FAS-FMG-SCG method reduced the residual by ten orders of magnitude in a single iteration. This suggests that the combination of overlapping Schwarz subdomains and Krylov acceleration effectively handles the complex coupling inherent in high-order DG discretizations.

### Efficiency of Local Adaptation

The benefits of local $hp$-adaptation were quantified using a spherical wave front benchmark. By refining only the regions where the solution exhibited sharp gradients, the adaptive method achieved savings of 93% to 98% in the total number of degrees of freedom (DOFs) compared to global refinement. For a target error level of $10^{-2}$ at a polynomial degree $p=8$, the adaptive approach was substantially more efficient than traditional continuous finite element methods.

Crucially, the overhead associated with managing the adaptive mesh—such as rebuilding levels and redistributing elements for load balancing—remained low, typically accounting for only about 10% of the total solver time. This indicates that the FAS-MG framework is well-suited for dynamic problems where the mesh must be updated frequently.

## Practical Implications for Computational Science

The development of a robust FAS-MG solver for high-order DG-SEM has broad implications for engineering and scientific simulations. Its ability to handle local refinement without hanging nodes simplifies the preprocessing pipeline for complex geometries, while the high-order accuracy of the DG method ensures that physical features are captured with minimal numerical dissipation.

Compared to other state-of-the-art techniques, such as those using Chebyshev smoothers, the proposed method shows significant speedups—often by a factor of 20 or more for higher polynomial degrees ($p \ge 8$). This performance gap widens as the mesh becomes more anisotropic, making the FAS-FMG-SCG approach a particularly strong candidate for large-scale simulations in aerospace engineering and environmental modeling.

Future work aims to extend this framework to fully nonlinear systems, such as the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations. Preliminary tests ({fig:vortex-shedding-behind-a-cylinder}) suggest that the adaptive multigrid strategy can successfully track transient features, such as vortex shedding behind a cylinder, while maintaining computational efficiency through local mesh updates.

By combining the Full Approximation Scheme with a robust, Krylov-accelerated Schwarz smoother, the method overcomes traditional barriers to local refinement in DG-SEM, offering a scalable path for high-fidelity numerical analysis.
