## Introduction to Rough Volatility and the Hurst Parameter

In the field of quantitative finance, stochastic volatility models are used to capture the complex dynamics of asset prices, particularly the way volatility changes over time. Historically, models like the classical Heston model assumed that volatility follows a process with the same regularity as a standard Brownian motion. However, empirical studies of high-frequency market data have revealed that volatility often exhibits "rougher" behavior than previously thought.

This "roughness" is characterized by the Hurst parameter $H$. In classical models, $H = 1/2$. In rough volatility models, $H$ is typically observed to be much smaller, often in the range of $(0, 0.2)$. These models, such as the rough Heston model, are highly effective at reproducing the steep "volatility skew" seen in short-maturity options—a feature that classical models often fail to capture.

Despite their descriptive power, rough volatility models present significant computational challenges. The core of the rough Heston model relies on a fractional Riccati equation. Unlike the classical Heston model, which has a closed-form solution, the fractional version requires solving a non-linear, non-local integral equation for every different value of $H$. This makes tasks like model calibration—which involves searching for the best $H$ to fit market data—computationally expensive. This paper addresses this bottleneck by demonstrating that the solution to the rough Heston model is an analytic function of $H$, allowing for highly efficient Taylor expansions.

## The Fractional Riccati Equation

The price of a European option in the rough Heston model is determined by the characteristic function of the log-price $X_T$. Due to the model's affine structure, this characteristic function can be expressed as:

$$\phi_H(z) = E[e^{izX_T}] = \exp(A_H(T, z) + V_0 \psi_H(T, z))$$

Here, $V_0$ is the initial variance, and $\psi_H(t, z)$ is the unique continuous solution to the following fractional Riccati equation:

$$\psi_H(t, z) = \int_0^t K_H(t - s)F(z, \psi_H(s, z)) ds$$

The function $F(z, x)$ is a quadratic driver typical of Heston-type models. The complexity arises from the kernel $K_H(t)$, which is defined as:

$$K_H(t) = \frac{t^{H-1/2}}{\Gamma(H + 1/2)}$$

When $H = 1/2$, this kernel becomes constant ($1/\Gamma(1) = 1$), and the integral equation simplifies into a standard ordinary differential equation (ODE) that can be solved analytically. However, for any other $H$, the kernel is singular at $t=0$, meaning the equation has "memory" and depends on the entire past trajectory of the solution. Traditionally, this requires expensive numerical integration schemes that must be re-run whenever $H$ changes.

## Analyticity and the Taylor Expansion Approach

The primary theoretical contribution of this research is the proof that $\psi_H(t, z)$ is not just differentiable, but real analytic with respect to the Hurst parameter $H$ over the interval $H \in (-1/2, 0.95)$. This is a powerful result because it implies that the solution for a difficult value of $H$ (like $H=0.1$) can be accurately approximated by a Taylor series centered at a more convenient expansion point $H_0$.

The authors propose approximating the solution using an $N$-th order Taylor polynomial:

$$P_{N,H_0}(t; H, z) = \sum_{n=0}^N \frac{(H - H_0)^n}{n!} \partial^n_H \psi(t; H_0, z)$$

To implement this, one must calculate the derivatives $\partial^n_H \psi$. The paper shows that these derivatives satisfy a sequence of linear Volterra equations:

$$\partial^n_H \psi(t; H, z) = \int_0^t K_H(t - s)F_x(z, \psi(s; H, z)) \partial^n_H \psi(s; H, z) ds + A_n(t; H, z)$$

In this structure, $F_x$ is the derivative of the quadratic driver. The term $A_n(t; H, z)$ is a "source" term that depends only on the lower-order derivatives ($n-1, n-2, \dots$) and the derivatives of the kernel $K_H$ with respect to $H$. This recursive structure is key: once the base solution at $H_0$ is known, each subsequent Taylor coefficient can be found by solving a linear equation, which is much simpler than the original non-linear Riccati equation.

## Choosing Expansion Points: $H_0 = 1/2$ and $H_0 = 0$

The choice of the expansion point $H_0$ significantly impacts the efficiency and accuracy of the method. The authors focus on two primary candidates.

### Expansion around $H_0 = 1/2$
The most natural choice is $H_0 = 1/2$, which corresponds to the classical Heston model. At this point, the fractional Riccati equation reduces to a standard ODE, and its solution $\psi(t; 1/2, z)$ is known in closed form. 

Furthermore, the linear Volterra equations for the derivatives also simplify into linear ODEs. These can be solved using the "variation of constants" method, providing a very fast way to generate expansion coefficients. This approach is particularly effective for "moderately rough" volatility regimes where $H$ is close to $0.5$.

### Expansion around $H_0 = 0$
While $H \approx 0.1$ is common in equity markets, some researchers have explored the "hyper-rough" regime where $H$ is very close to zero or even negative ($H < 0$). Standard numerical schemes often struggle as $H \to -1/2$ because the singularity in the kernel becomes increasingly severe.

By expanding around $H_0 = 0$, the authors provide a tool for pricing in these challenging regimes. Although there is no closed-form solution at $H=0$, the authors use a high-precision rational approximation (a root-Padé method) to establish a reference solution. The Taylor expansion then allows them to move smoothly into the negative $H$ territory with high stability.

## Numerical Implementation and Efficiency

To validate the method, the authors compare the Taylor expansion results against a benchmark solution obtained via a robust (but slow) implicit product-integration scheme.

The numerical workflow involves:
1.  **Reference Solution:** Computing the Riccati solution at $H_0$ across a grid of Fourier frequencies $z$ and time steps.
2.  **Recursive Coefficients:** Solving the sequence of linear Volterra equations for the Taylor terms. This is done using a piecewise-linear product-integration method.
3.  **Summation:** Evaluating the Taylor polynomial for the desired target $H$.
4.  **Pricing:** Inserting the approximated $\psi$ into the characteristic function and using Fourier inversion (Lewis's formula) to find the option price.

The results show that the Taylor expansion is remarkably efficient. In one test case, computing the expansion up to the 12th order for 100 different $H$ values and 300 Fourier frequencies took less than four seconds. This speed is a result of the method's modularity; the heavy lifting (calculating the derivatives at $H_0$) only needs to be done once. Changing the target $H$ simply requires re-evaluating a polynomial.

## Accuracy and Regimes of Convergence

The experiments demonstrate that even low-order expansions ($N=2$ or $N=4$) provide high accuracy for implied volatilities across various strikes and maturities. 

*   **Proximity to $H_0$:** As expected, the accuracy is highest when the target $H$ is near $H_0$. For $H_0 = 1/2$, the expansion remains highly accurate for $H=0.3$ and $H=0.4$. 
*   **Maturity Effects:** The Taylor expansion performs exceptionally well for longer maturities. For very short maturities (e.g., $T < 0.02$), higher-order expansions are needed to maintain accuracy, as the characteristic function becomes more sensitive to the fine details of the Riccati solution.
*   **The Hyper-Rough Advantage:** One of the most striking findings is the expansion's performance around $H_0 = 0$. It maintains high accuracy even for $H = -0.2$, a regime where most standard solvers become unstable or prohibitively slow.

## Significance for Financial Engineering

The shift from viewing $H$ as a fixed parameter to viewing the model as an analytic function of $H$ has several practical implications:

1.  **Calibration Speed:** Model calibration usually requires solving the Riccati equation thousands of times to find the parameters that match market prices. With this Taylor approach, the solver only needs to be run once at $H_0$, with the rest of the calibration performed using fast polynomial evaluations.
2.  **Sensitivity Analysis ($H$-Greeks):** Risk managers need to know how sensitive an option's price is to changes in model parameters. Since the Taylor coefficients are themselves the derivatives of the solution with respect to $H$, the "H-Greek" (the derivative of the price with respect to the Hurst parameter) is a byproduct of the expansion.
3.  **Unified Modeling:** This framework allows for a unified treatment of classical and rough Heston models. A single implementation can bridge the gap between the two regimes by treating the classical model as the center of an expansion that explores the rough volatility space.

By establishing the analyticity of the rough Heston model with respect to its most characteristic parameter, this work provides a theoretical foundation for a new class of approximation methods. It moves the field away from "pointwise" numerical solutions toward a more global, functional understanding of how roughness affects derivative pricing.
