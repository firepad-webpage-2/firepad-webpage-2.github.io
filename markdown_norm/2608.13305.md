## Overview of Physics-Informed DRT Estimation and Latent-Space Monitoring

Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) is a standard diagnostic tool used to probe the internal processes of electrochemical devices like solid oxide fuel cells (SOFCs) and solid oxide electrolysis cells (SOECs). By applying a small alternating current and measuring the voltage response across a range of frequencies, researchers can identify different physical phenomena—such as ion transport, gas diffusion, and chemical reactions—based on their characteristic timescales. However, interpreting the resulting "impedance spectra" is difficult because these processes often overlap in frequency, appearing as a single, distorted arc in a Nyquist plot.

To resolve these overlapping features, researchers often use the Distribution of Relaxation Times (DRT) method. Instead of fitting an Equivalent Circuit Model (ECM), which requires making specific assumptions about the cell's internal structure, DRT decomposes the impedance signal into a continuous distribution of relaxation processes. While powerful, calculating the DRT from raw impedance data is an "ill-posed" inverse problem: tiny amounts of measurement noise can lead to wildly different and physically impossible DRT estimates.

This paper introduces a physics-informed convolutional neural network (PI-CNN) framework that automates DRT estimation while ensuring physical consistency. Unlike traditional methods that treat each spectrum individually, this approach learns a shared representation across entire datasets. This allows for more stable monitoring of cell degradation and operational events through a compact "latent space."

## The Distribution of Relaxation Times

The fundamental challenge in EIS is that the measured impedance $Z(j\omega)$ at a frequency $\omega$ is a complex-valued summation of all internal electrochemical processes. The DRT method assumes that the impedance can be represented as a series of elementary resistor-capacitor (RC) circuits, each with a specific relaxation time $\tau$. The relationship is defined by the following integral:

$$Z(j\omega) = R_s + j\omega L + \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{g(\ln \tau)}{1 + j\omega \tau} d\ln \tau$$

In this equation, $R_s$ represents the series resistance (ohmic losses), $L$ is the inductance (often related to wiring and high-frequency effects), and $g(\ln \tau)$ is the DRT function. The DRT function acts as a map, where peaks at specific values of $\tau$ indicate dominant electrochemical processes occurring at those timescales.

Extracting $g(\ln \tau)$ from $Z(j\omega)$ is difficult because the integral effectively "blurs" the distribution. Standard mathematical techniques to reverse this blurring (deconvolution) are highly sensitive to noise. To obtain a smooth, usable DRT, researchers typically use Tikhonov regularization, which adds a penalty for "wiggly" distributions. However, choosing the right amount of regularization is often a manual, subjective process that can obscure real changes in the cell's state.

## Physics-Informed Convolutional Autoencoders

The framework proposed in this work replaces manual regularization with a physics-informed neural network. The architecture is a convolutional autoencoder, consisting of three main parts: an encoder, a decoder, and a physics-informed reconstruction layer.

### Convolutional Logic
The researchers noticed that the integral relationship between impedance and DRT can be viewed as a mathematical convolution. This makes convolutional neural networks (CNNs) a natural fit. Unlike standard dense networks, CNNs use sliding filters that are well-suited for capturing frequency-dependent patterns in the impedance spectra.

### The Encoder and Decoder
The encoder takes the real and imaginary parts of the impedance spectrum as input and compresses them into a compact tensor called the "latent representation." The decoder then takes this latent tensor and expands it to predict the DRT function, $g(\ln \tau)$. To ensure physical plausibility, the decoder uses a Softplus activation function at its output, which forces the DRT values to be non-negative ($g(\ln \tau) > 0$).

### The Physics Layer
The defining feature of this approach is that the network is never told what the "correct" DRT looks like during training. Instead, the predicted DRT is passed into a reconstruction layer that implements a discretized version of the physical integral:

$$\hat{Z}(j\omega_n) = R_s + j\omega_n L + \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{g(\ln \tau_m)}{1 + j\omega_n \tau_m} w_m$$

Here, $\hat{Z}$ is the reconstructed impedance, $\tau_m$ is a grid of relaxation times, and $w_m$ are weights for numerical integration (using the trapezoidal rule). The loss function used to train the network measures the difference between this reconstructed impedance and the actual measured impedance:

$$\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \left( (\text{Re}\{Z_n\} - \text{Re}\{\hat{Z}_n\})^2 + (\text{Im}\{Z_n\} - \text{Im}\{\hat{Z}_n\})^2 \right) + \lambda_L L^2$$

By minimizing this loss, the network is forced to find a DRT that, when passed through the laws of physics, perfectly matches the experimental measurements. Because the same network is trained on the entire dataset at once, it learns a consistent "implicit regularization" that applies to every spectrum, making it much easier to compare results over time.

## Interpreting the Latent Space

A common criticism of neural networks is that they act as "black boxes." In this framework, however, the researchers demonstrated that the latent space—the internal representation between the encoder and decoder—spontaneously organizes itself according to physical principles.

To test this, they used a "decoder probe" analysis. By manually activating specific parts of the latent tensor and observing the resulting DRT output, they found that different sections of the latent space correspond to different relaxation timescales.

This organization happens without any explicit instructions from the researchers; the network simply finds this to be the most efficient way to store the electrochemical information.

## Condition Monitoring and Event Detection

Because the latent space is physically organized and consistent, it can be used for "condition monitoring"—tracking the health and status of the fuel cell in real-time. Instead of looking at complex DRT curves, researchers can monitor the "distance" a cell has moved in the latent space from its starting point.

The Euclidean distance $D_{\text{ref}}(t)$ between a latent vector at time $t$ ($\mathbf{z}_t$) and the initial vector at time zero ($\mathbf{z}_0$) provides a single number that summarizes the total change in the system:

$$D_{\text{ref}}(t) = \| \mathbf{z}_t - \mathbf{z}_0 \|_2$$

Additionally, calculating the absolute difference element-by-element ($|\mathbf{z}_t - \mathbf{z}_0|$) allows researchers to pinpoint exactly which latent features are changing.

In experimental trials, this method successfully identified operational events such as hydrogen shortages (fuel starvation), power outages, and shifts in gas composition. The latent space metrics acted as a "early warning system," showing deviations long before they might be obvious in simple voltage or current measurements.

## Validation and Generalizability

The researchers validated their approach using both synthetic and experimental data.

1.  **Synthetic Data:** The model was tested on simulated spectra with two heavily overlapping relaxation processes (two-ZARC model). Even with added noise, the PI-CNN was able to resolve two distinct peaks in the DRT, whereas traditional methods often fail to separate such closely spaced features.
2.  **Long-term SOFC Monitoring:** In a 3600-hour test of a fuel cell stack, the framework accurately captured the evolution of the DRT as the stack aged. It provided a continuous, artifact-free view of degradation that matched the findings of previous manual studies.
3.  **Multi-Regime Degradation:** The framework was applied to a complex 2650-hour campaign where a cell was cycled through steam electrolysis, co-electrolysis, and reversible operation.

A major achievement of this work is that the **exact same neural network architecture** was used for all these different datasets without any modification or hyperparameter tuning. This demonstrates a high level of generalizability, suggesting that the model has learned the fundamental relationship between impedance and relaxation times rather than just memorizing a specific dataset.

## Significance of the Framework

The proposed physics-informed framework represents a shift in how EIS data is processed and interpreted. Its significance lies in three main areas:

*   **Consistency:** By processing an entire dataset with a single model, it eliminates the "fitting artifacts" that plague per-spectrum analysis. This ensures that when a researcher sees a peak move in the DRT, they can be sure it represents a physical change in the cell, not just a variation in how the math was solved.
*   **Automation:** It removes the need for expert-led, manual regularization and equivalent circuit selection. This makes high-quality electrochemical diagnostics accessible to non-experts and suitable for automated industrial monitoring.
*   **Dimensionality Reduction:** The ability to compress a complex, high-dimensional impedance spectrum into a small latent vector (e.g., 160 numbers) enables efficient condition monitoring and long-term data storage. The latent space provides a simplified "fingerprint" of the cell's state that is both interpretable and actionable.

By combining the rigorous constraints of electrochemical physics with the pattern-recognition power of convolutional networks, this approach provides a robust pipeline for characterizing the next generation of energy conversion and storage devices.
