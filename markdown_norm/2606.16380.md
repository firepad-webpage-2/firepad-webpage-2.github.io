In many economic environments, the act of consumption is rarely just about immediate satisfaction. For instance, when a doctor prescribes a specific treatment for a patient, they observe the outcome not only to fulfill the immediate goal of healing but also to gain information that informs how they treat future patients. Similarly, a consumer trying a new brand of coffee experiences its taste while simultaneously learning whether to purchase it again. 

Traditional economic models typically treat risk and information as separate entities. Risk is often seen as a hurdle to be mitigated through risk aversion, while information is treated as a resource to be acquired. "Informative Consumption" by Jiang and Lim proposes a framework that integrates these two perspectives. The paper argues that many risky decisions provide an "information incentive"—a benefit derived from the knowledge gained during consumption that improves future decision-making. By formalizing this trade-off, the researchers provide a method to distinguish between an individual's inherent dislike of risk and their specific opportunities to capitalize on new information.

## Situating the Framework

The paper positions itself at the intersection of several established literatures in economic theory. Most notably, it differs from the traditional **subjective learning** literature. In standard subjective learning models, a researcher observes a decision-maker's (DM) preferences over sets of options (menus) to infer what the DM expects to learn in the future. In those models, the information structure is usually unobserved. 

In contrast, Jiang and Lim focus on scenarios where the information structures are objective and known, represented by Anscombe–Aumann acts (which are essentially maps from states of the world to lotteries over prizes). What remains unobserved in their model is the DM's subjective future opportunities—the "future menu" of choices they expect to face after learning from their current consumption.

This shift in focus allows the authors to address **extrinsic preferences for information**. While some recent work explores "intrinsic" preferences (where people simply like knowing things, even if it doesn't change their behavior), this paper focuses on the practical, extrinsic value of information. It characterizes how information helps a DM make better choices later, and how this potential for better future choices influences their willingness to take risks today.

## The Informative Consumption Model

The core of the paper is the Informative Consumption (IC) representation. In this model, a decision-maker evaluates an act $f$ by balancing two distinct components: immediate consumption utility and future information utility. The total utility is expressed as:

$$
U(f) = \alpha U_C(f) + (1-\alpha) U_I(f)
$$

In this equation, $\alpha \in (0, 1]$ serves as a weight that determines the relative importance of current consumption versus future information. If $\alpha=1$, the model collapses back into the standard Subjective Expected Utility (SEU) framework, where the DM only cares about the immediate expected payoff.

The first component, $U_C(f)$, is the standard expected utility of consumption:

$$
U_C(f) = \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} \mu(\omega) \sum_{x \in \text{supp}(f)} f(x|\omega) u(x)
$$

Here, $\Omega$ represents the states of the world, $\mu$ is the DM's prior belief, $f(x|\omega)$ is the probability of receiving prize $x$ given state $\omega$, and $u$ is the Bernoulli utility function.

The second component, $U_I(f)$, represents the ex-ante value of the information gained from the realization of act $f$. When a prize $x$ is realized, the DM uses Bayesian updating to form a posterior belief about the true state of the world:

$$
\mu_{x,f}(\omega) = \frac{\mu(\omega) f(x|\omega)}{\sum_{\omega' \in \Omega} \mu(\omega') f(x|\omega')} = \frac{\mu(\omega) f(x|\omega)}{\mathbb{P}_f(x)}
$$

After updating their belief to $\mu_{x,f}$, the DM chooses an action from an unobserved subjective menu $A$ that maximizes their expected utility. The information utility $U_I(f)$ is the expected value of these maximized future utilities:

$$
U_I(f) = \sum_{x \in \text{supp}(f)} \mathbb{P}_f(x) \max_{a \in A} \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} \mu_{x,f}(\omega) u(a(\omega))
$$

This structure formalizes the idea that consumption acts as a signal. Different prizes $x$ provide different evidence about the underlying state $\omega$, and better signals allow the DM to make better subsequent choices from their future menu $A$.

## Axiomatic Foundations

The authors establish the IC model through a set of behavioral axioms. Beyond the standard rationality axioms (completeness, transitivity, continuity, and monotonicity), two axioms are central to the information-seeking behavior:

1.  **Skewness Independence**: Traditional independence axioms in decision theory assume that mixing two acts with a third should not change their relative ranking. Jiang and Lim weaken this. They argue that mixing can change the "skewness" of an act—how the probabilities of prizes are distributed across states. Since this skewness determines the information content of the act, the DM may not be indifferent to mixing. Skewness Independence only applies when the relative likelihoods across states remain proportional, ensuring that the informational "signal" is not distorted by the mixture.

2.  **Mixture Aversion**: This is a defining feature of the IC model. If a DM is indifferent between two acts $f$ and $g$ ($f \sim g$), they will weakly prefer either act to a mixture of the two: 

$$
f \sim g \implies f \succsim a f + (1-a) g \text{ for } a \in (0,1)
$$

This represents a preference for "clearer" or "stronger" signals. Mixing acts tends to blur the relationship between states and prizes, making the resulting act less informative. A DM who values information for future decisions will therefore dislike such mixtures. This axiom ensures that the value function for information is convex, a standard property in information theory where more "spread out" posterior beliefs are more valuable than their average.

## Decomposing Risk and Information

One of the paper's most intuitive contributions is the decomposition of the **certainty equivalent** ($CE$). The $CE$ is the guaranteed amount of money that a DM would accept instead of taking a gamble. In standard models, the $CE$ is simply the expected value minus a risk premium. In the IC model, the authors introduce a third term: the **information premium** ($IP$).

$$
CE(f) = E_f(x) - RP(f) + IP(f)
$$

-   $E_f(x)$ is the expected payoff of the act.
-   $RP(f)$ is the standard **risk premium**. It represents the amount of utility the DM is willing to give up to avoid the objective uncertainty of the lottery.
-   $IP(f)$ is the **information premium**. It represents the additional value the DM places on the act because it provides useful information.

This decomposition reveals a fundamental trade-off. For an act to be informative, it must be risky (it must produce different prizes in different states to provide a signal). Thus, if $IP(f) > 0$, the act must involve some risk, and for a risk-averse person, this implies $RP(f) > 0$. The information premium essentially "compensates" the DM for the risk they must take to learn. This provides a microfoundation for why information is often seen as "costly": the cost is the foregone utility from avoiding risk or the physical resources spent on the informative-but-risky consumption.

## The Arrow–Debreu–Pratt Parameterization

To make the model more applicable to empirical data, the authors introduce a tractable two-parameter version. They use a Constant Absolute Risk Aversion (CARA) utility function:

$$
u_{\rho}(x) = 1 - e^{-\rho x}
$$

where $\rho$ is the Arrow–Pratt coefficient of risk aversion. They also simplify the subjective future menu $A$ to consist of "Arrow–Debreu assets"—imaginary contracts that pay a fixed amount $\iota$ if a specific state occurs and zero otherwise. The parameter $\iota$ represents the **information incentive**.

In this parameterized version, the information utility simplifies significantly:

$$
U_I(f) = u(\iota) I(f, \mu)
$$

The term $I(f, \mu)$ is defined as the **Information Index**:

$$
I(f, \mu) = \sum_{x \in \text{supp}(f)} \max_{\omega \in \Omega} \mu(\omega) f(x|\omega)
$$

This index satisfies several desirable properties. It equals 1 if an act is perfectly informative (completely resolving uncertainty) and reaches its minimum for acts that provide no information. It also respects the Blackwell ordering of informativeness. 

## Explaining Heterogeneity in Risk-Taking

A significant implication of this work is how it changes our understanding of individual differences. Traditionally, if two people face the same gamble and one takes it while the other doesn't, we assume the first person is less risk-averse (lower $\rho$). 

The IC model suggests a different explanation. Two individuals might have the exact same level of risk aversion ($\rho$), but one might have much better future opportunities to use the information gained from the gamble. For instance, an experienced investor and a novice might both dislike risk equally, but the experienced investor might have a "future menu" that allows them to profit much more from the market signals they observe today.

The authors prove that such divergent behavior can be explained solely by differences in the information incentive $\iota$, provided that the act being chosen has a higher Information Index $I(f, \mu)$ than the alternative. This suggests that what looks like "risk-taking" may actually be "information-seeking" driven by unobserved future potential.

## Conclusion and Significance

By formalizing the "informative" side of consumption, Jiang and Lim provide a framework that explains why individuals might seek out risky prospects. The unique contribution of this work lies in its ability to separate objective risk from the subjective value of information without needing to observe the DM's future choices.

The introduction of the **Information Premium** and the **Information Index** provides researchers with new metrics to analyze behavior in finance, healthcare, and consumer markets. Ultimately, the paper suggests that to understand why people take risks, we must look beyond their immediate feelings about uncertainty and consider what they stand to learn for the future.
