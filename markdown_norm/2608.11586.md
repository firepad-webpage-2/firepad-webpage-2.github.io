## The Challenge of p-Laplace Regularity

The study of partial differential equations (PDEs) of $p$-Laplace type is a central theme in mathematical analysis, particularly in the context of non-Newtonian fluid mechanics, glaciology, and nonlinear diffusion. The standard $p$-Laplace equation is given by:

$$\text{div}(|Du|^{p-2}Du) = f$$

where $u$ is the unknown function, $Du$ is its gradient, and $p$ is a parameter typically in the range $(1, \infty)$. When $p=2$, the equation becomes the linear Laplace equation, which is well-understood and yields $C^\infty$ (infinitely smooth) solutions for smooth data $f$. However, for $p \neq 2$, the equation is nonlinear and exhibits a change in character. When $p > 2$, the equation is "degenerate" at points where the gradient vanishes ($Du = 0$), meaning the elliptic behavior of the operator breaks down. When $1 < p < 2$, the equation is "singular" at those same points.

The primary difficulty in this field involves the critical set $Z(u) := \{x \in \Omega : Du(x) = 0\}$. While classical results have established that weak solutions have gradients that are locally Hölder continuous ($Du \in C^{0,\alpha_0}_{\text{loc}}$), higher-order regularity is generally not achievable for the solution $u$ itself. For instance, even with a perfectly smooth source term $f$, a solution might fail to be $C^2$ (twice continuously differentiable) across the critical set. Consider the one-dimensional example:

$$u(x) = \frac{p-1}{p} |x_1|^{p/(p-1)}$$

This function satisfies $\Delta_p u = 1$ but lacks $C^2$ regularity at the origin for $p > 2$. This limitation suggests that to find smoother objects in the context of $p$-Laplace equations, one must look not at $u$ itself, but at specific nonlinear compositions of its gradient.

## Homogeneous Gradient Compositions

The paper by Nguyen and Truong addresses this limitation by investigating the classical regularity of smooth, homogeneous functions of the gradient, denoted as $\Phi(Du)$. A function $\Phi$ is positively homogeneous of degree $m$ if $\Phi(t\xi) = t^m \Phi(\xi)$ for all $t > 0$. Common examples include powers of the gradient magnitude, such as $|Du|^m$.

The core thesis of the research is that while the second derivative of $u$ may blow up or behave poorly near the critical set $Z(u)$, a composition $\Phi(Du)$ can remain highly smooth if the degree of homogeneity $m$ is sufficiently large. Specifically, the authors establish that for a given order of differentiability $k$, the function $\Phi(Du)$ belongs to $C^k$ if $m$ satisfies a specific threshold relative to the existing regularity of the gradient.

This approach differs from previous work that focused on the weak regularity (such as Sobolev or BMO estimates) of nonlinear fields. Instead, this paper provides a path to classical $C^k$ regularity across the entire domain, including the problematic critical set. This result is significant because it provides a precise condition—relating the algebraic structure of the composition (degree $m$) to the analytic properties of the solution (Hölder exponent $\alpha$)—to guarantee smoothness.

## Methodology: Intrinsic Scaling and Normalization

The technical heart of the paper lies in a technique called intrinsic scaling. To analyze the regularity of $u$ near a point $x$, the authors distinguish whether the gradient is zero or non-zero. If $Du(x) \neq 0$, the equation is locally uniformly elliptic, but the "strength" of this ellipticity depends on the magnitude $|Du(x)|$.

To handle this, the authors define an intrinsic radius $r$ that depends on the gradient magnitude $q = |Du(x)|$ and the local Hölder seminorm $H$:

$$r := (q / (8H))^{1/\alpha}$$

By zooming in on a ball $B_{2r}(x)$ using this specific radius, the authors ensure that the gradient of $u$ does not change too much within that neighborhood. Specifically, they maintain the bound $3q/4 \leq |Du(z)| \leq 5q/4$ for all $z$ in the ball. They then define a normalized function $v$:

$$v(y) := \frac{u(x + ry) - u(x)}{rq}$$

On the unit ball $B_2$ in the rescaled coordinate $y$, the gradient magnitude of $v$ is close to 1. This normalization transforms the original degenerate or singular equation into a new equation for $v$ that is uniformly elliptic and has bounded coefficients:

$$\text{div}(|Dv|^{p-2}Dv) = g(y)$$

The term $g(y) = r q^{1-p} f(x + ry)$ represents the rescaled source term. A key requirement for this method is that $g(y)$ remains controlled as $q$ approaches zero. This leads to the condition $\alpha \leq 1/(p-1)$, which ensures that the rescaling does not cause the source term to explode.

Once the problem is transformed into a uniformly elliptic one with controlled data, the authors apply classical Schauder estimates. These estimates provide uniform bounds on the higher-order derivatives of $v$. Because the derivatives of $v$ are bounded independently of the original gradient magnitude $q$, the authors can "translate" these bounds back to the original solution $u$.

## Establishing Regularity Across the Critical Set

The estimates obtained via Schauder theory are valid only where $Du \neq 0$. To prove that $\Phi(Du)$ is $C^k$ everywhere, including on the critical set $Z(u)$, the authors use an extension lemma. This lemma states that if a function $F$ and its derivatives decay rapidly enough as they approach a set $Z$, then $F$ can be extended to a $C^k$ function that is flat (has zero derivatives) on $Z$.

By combining the Schauder estimates for $v$ with the scaling relations, the authors derive a decay estimate for the derivatives of the composition $\Phi(Du)$:

$$|D^j(\Phi(Du))(x)| \leq C|Du(x)|^{m-j/\alpha}$$

for $0 \leq j \leq k$. To apply the extension lemma, the exponent $m-j/\alpha$ must be positive for all $j$ up to $k$. This requirement directly leads to the central condition of the paper:

$$m > k/\alpha$$

If this condition holds, the derivatives of $\Phi(Du)$ vanish at the critical set, and the function is $C^k$ across the entire domain. This explains why the degree of homogeneity $m$ must be higher to achieve higher orders of differentiability $k$. Essentially, the "flatness" of the homogeneous function $\Phi$ at the origin compensates for the potential lack of regularity in the underlying gradient $Du$.

## Main Results and Broad Applicability

The paper establishes several key results across different types of equations:

1.  **Scalar p-Laplace Equation:** For a solution $u \in W^{1,p}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)$ and a smooth $m$-homogeneous function $\Phi$, if $m > k/\alpha$, then $\Phi(Du) \in C^k_{\text{loc}}(\Omega; \mathbb{R}^N)$. A notable corollary is that $|Du|^m \in C^1_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)$ whenever $m > \max\{1/\alpha_0, p-1\}$.
2.  **Autonomous Anisotropic Operators:** The methodology extends to equations involving more general operators $\text{div } A(Du) = f$, provided the flux $A$ is $(p-1)$-homogeneous and satisfies certain ellipticity conditions.
3.  **Vectorial Systems:** The results are shown to hold for systems where $U$ is a vector-valued function. Here, the gradient $DU$ is a matrix. The paper proves that $\Phi(DU) \in C^k_{\text{loc}}$ for homogeneous $\Phi$ defined on matrices, with the same $m > k/\alpha$ condition.
4.  **Parabolic Systems:** The authors adapt the framework to time-dependent problems of the form:
    $$\partial_t U - \text{div}(|DU|^{p-2}DU) = F$$
    In the parabolic setting, space and time scale differently. The authors define two separate exponents, $\gamma$ for spatial regularity and $\eta$ for temporal/mixed regularity:
    $$\gamma := \max\{1/\alpha, (\beta^{-1} + p-2)/2\}$$
    $$\eta := 2\gamma + 2 - p$$
    They show that $\Phi(DU)$ achieves higher regularity in space if $m > k\gamma$, and in the full space-time sense if $m > k\eta$.
5.  **Porous Medium Equation:** The paper also applies these principles to the porous medium equation, showing that powers of the solution $u^m$ exhibit higher regularity based on the solution's own Hölder continuity.

## Significance of the Work

This research provides a unified and general framework for understanding the regularity of gradient-dependent quantities in non-smooth PDE settings. Its significance can be summarized in three points:

*   **Universality:** Unlike many previous approaches that required the parameter $p$ to be close to 2 (allowing for perturbative linear analysis), the method of pointwise normalization used here works for the entire range $1 < p < \infty$. It relies on the intrinsic geometry of the equation rather than proximity to the linear case.
*   **Predictive Power:** The condition $m > k/\alpha$ provides a simple algebraic criterion. If a researcher knows the Hölder exponent of the gradient (a standard piece of information in regularity theory), they can immediately determine the smoothness of any homogeneous composition of that gradient.
*   **Structural Insight:** The result that derivatives of $\Phi(Du)$ vanish on the critical set provides a deep look into the structure of solutions. It suggests that while the gradient itself may have singular behavior, the energy-like quantities or stress tensors (which are often homogeneous functions of the gradient) can be very well-behaved.

By bridging the gap between the limited regularity of the solution $u$ and the potentially high regularity of its gradient's compositions, Nguyen and Truong offer a more complete picture of the behavior of $p$-Laplace type equations near their most challenging points.
