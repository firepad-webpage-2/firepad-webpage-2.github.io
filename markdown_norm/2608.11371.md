## Human Responses to AI Financial Advice

As artificial intelligence (AI) becomes increasingly capable of providing guidance on complex economic decisions, understanding the behavioral response of users is critical. While significant research has focused on the technical performance and reliability of large language models (LLMs) in financial domains, less is known about how individuals actually incorporate these recommendations into their final choices. Choi et al. (2026) investigate this dynamic through a controlled experiment focused on pension portfolio allocation, a high-stakes decision involving multi-dimensional trade-offs between risk and return.

The study specifically examines how advice from general-purpose LLMs influences the behavior of retail investors. Unlike specialized "robo-advisors" that often manage funds directly, general AI systems provide non-binding advice that users must choose to implement. The researchers aimed to quantify the "pass-through" of this advice—the extent to which the differences between AI recommendations are reflected in the final choices made by individuals—and to identify the psychological barriers that prevent full adoption of AI guidance.

## Experimental Framework and Design

The researchers conducted an experiment with 400 employed adults in South Korea, all of whom were active participants in defined contribution (DC) pension plans. The task required participants to allocate a hypothetical pension balance across a menu of eleven investment products. Each product was presented with comprehensive data, including returns over various horizons, risk grades, and standard deviations. This setup mirrors the complexity of real-world retirement planning, where investors must balance long-term growth against volatility.

The study employed a 2x2 between-subjects design. The first dimension varied the substantive content of the advice by using two different AI models:
*   **Aggressive Recommendation:** Generated by GPT-4 Turbo, which suggested a portfolio with higher risk and higher expected returns.
*   **Conservative Recommendation:** Generated by GPT-4o, which suggested a more diversified portfolio with lower risk.

The second dimension varied the presence of a rationale. Half of the participants received only the numerical allocation suggested by the AI, while the other half received the allocation alongside a paragraph explaining the AI’s logic.

The experimental procedure followed a four-step process. First, participants made a baseline allocation $w$ without any AI guidance. Second, they were shown the AI recommendation $r$ alongside their own baseline $w$ and were asked to make a final allocation $v$. Third, they answered survey questions regarding their trust in the AI and their self-perception. Finally, they completed incentivized quizzes on financial literacy and cognitive reflection. To ensure the task was taken seriously, participants were paid a performance-based bonus determined by a computer simulation of their final portfolio's retirement wealth.

## Measuring Causal Pass-Through

A central contribution of this work is the quantification of "causal pass-through." In the context of this study, pass-through represents the fraction of the difference between the two AI recommendations that is actually adopted by the participants. If the pass-through is 0%, participants ignored the differences in advice; if it is 100%, they followed the advice perfectly.

To measure this, the researchers projected the participants' portfolios onto an axis connecting the aggressive and conservative recommendations. They found a causal pass-through of approximately 37%. This result was statistically significant and robust across various models, indicating that while individuals are influenced by the specific content of AI advice, they systematically discount it by approximately two-thirds.

![causal-effect-on-product-allocations](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11371v1/figures/fig_product_pass_through.png)

Interestingly, the provision of a rationale—an explanatory text explaining the AI's logic—had no detectable effect on the pass-through rate. Participants were influenced by the numerical targets regardless of whether the AI explained its reasoning. This suggests that for general-purpose AI advice in finance, the substantive recommendation carries more weight than the accompanying justification.

## Impact on Portfolio Risk and Performance

The researchers also analyzed how AI advice changed specific portfolio characteristics. The pass-through was most evident in risk exposure. Participants assigned to the aggressive recommendation significantly increased their expected returns (by 1.22 percentage points) and their portfolio risk (standard deviation increased by 2.15 percentage points). They also shifted their allocations away from principal-protected products and toward high-risk assets.

However, the study found no detectable pass-through for risk-adjusted performance, measured by the Sharpe ratio. While the AI models themselves offered recommendations with different Sharpe ratios, these differences did not translate into the participants' final portfolios. This implies that while AI can steer people toward higher or lower levels of risk, it does not necessarily lead to more efficient portfolios in the sense of providing higher returns for the same level of risk. 

This finding is particularly relevant for financial regulation. If AI systems can shift large numbers of retail investors toward higher risk without improving their risk-adjusted efficiency, the accessibility of LLMs might lead to increased market volatility or individual financial distress without a corresponding benefit in long-term wealth accumulation.

## Behavioral Margins of Advice Implementation

To understand why pass-through is incomplete, the study decomposes the revision behavior into three distinct margins:
1.  **Revision Rate (Extensive Margin):** 81% of participants changed their portfolio after seeing the AI advice. This high rate suggests that users generally view AI as a credible source of information.
2.  **Directedness ($\cos \theta_i$):** Among those who revised, 95% moved in a direction consistent with the AI advice. The researchers measured this using the cosine of the angle $\theta_i$ between the revision vector $\Delta_i = v_i - w_i$ and the advice gap $g_i = r_i - w_i$. The mean directedness was $\cos \theta_i = 0.65$.
3.  **Weight on Advice ($\beta_i$):** This measures the magnitude of the recommended move that was implemented. It is defined as:
$$\beta_i = \frac{\langle \Delta_i, g_i \rangle}{||g_i||^2}$$
The mean weight on advice was 0.51, meaning that even when people moved in the right direction, they only went about halfway to the recommended target.

This decomposition reveals that incomplete pass-through is not driven by people ignoring the AI, but by a combination of "under-adjustment" (not moving far enough) and "idiosyncratic revision" (making changes that the AI did not suggest). Users appear to treat the AI as a signal that suggests a direction for improvement rather than a definitive target to be reached.

## Drivers of Individual Adherence

The study identifies specific individual characteristics that correlate with how well people follow AI advice. A prominent factor is **perceived dominance** or overconfidence. Approximately 27% of participants mistakenly believed their baseline portfolio was better than the AI's recommendation across all relevant dimensions (e.g., higher returns and lower risk). These "overconfident" individuals closed significantly less of the gap toward the recommendation (14% compared to 26% for others). Interestingly, they still revised their portfolios at similar rates and magnitudes, but their revisions were poorly directed toward the AI's suggestions.

Another factor is **baseline inefficiency**. Participants whose initial portfolios were objectively poor (low Sharpe ratios) tended to make larger revisions in magnitude. However, these larger changes did not result in them ending up significantly closer to the AI's target. This suggests that while these individuals recognized the need for change, they struggled to use the AI advice effectively to reach the recommended state.

## Conclusions and Significance

The findings of Choi et al. (2026) offer a nuanced view of the role of AI in personal finance. The study demonstrates that different frontier AI models can provide substantively different advice even when given identical inputs. Because users follow this advice—albeit partially—the choice of which AI model to consult has a causal impact on the risk profiles of retail investors.

The results suggest that AI functions as a powerful steering mechanism. However, because pass-through is incomplete and often fails to improve risk-adjusted performance, the widespread adoption of AI advice may not be a panacea for investment errors. The study emphasizes that the "user side" of AI interaction is characterized by systematic discounting and psychological biases like overconfidence, which must be accounted for in the design and regulation of AI-driven financial services. 

Ultimately, this work highlights the responsibility of AI developers and financial institutions. Since the specific model configuration significantly influences user outcomes, the design of these systems is not merely a technical challenge but an economic and ethical one with direct consequences for household welfare.
