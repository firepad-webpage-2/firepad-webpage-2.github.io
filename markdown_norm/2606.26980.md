## The Challenge of Climate-Sensitive Mortality

Traditional mortality forecasting models are generally designed to capture long-term structural trends, such as improvements in healthcare and lifestyle. However, these models often struggle to account for the increasing frequency and intensity of climate extremes, such as severe heatwaves or prolonged cold spells. Because standard actuarial models typically assume mortality patterns evolve slowly and predictably, sudden spikes driven by weather events can lead to significant misestimations of financial liabilities for life insurers and pension funds.

The link between environmental conditions and mortality is well-documented in epidemiological research. Factors such as extreme temperatures, humidity, and air quality exert both immediate and delayed effects on human health, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations like the elderly. In regulatory environments such as Solvency II, the inability to model these climate-driven shocks creates a gap in risk management, as the inherent volatility of weather is not fully reflected in long-term mortality projections.

To address this, the research presented here introduces a framework that integrates high-frequency climate data with demographic records. By moving beyond aggregate national statistics and focusing on regional and age-specific data, the authors aim to build models that can capture the non-linear relationship between climate variables and death rates. This involves not just tracking when a climate event occurs, but also understanding the "lagged" effects—where a weather extreme in one week causes a peak in mortality several weeks later—and the spatial propagation of these risks across neighboring regions.

## A Two-Stage Mortality Forecasting Framework

The researchers propose a two-stage modeling approach. The primary motivation for this separation is to isolate the climate-driven "noise" from the underlying structural mortality trends. By first establishing a robust baseline, the model can then focus exclusively on the residuals—the differences between observed mortality and the baseline prediction—which are hypothesized to contain the signal of climate-related shocks.

### Stage 1: The Baseline Model
The first stage employs a weekly seasonal Lee-Carter model. This is an extension of the classic Lee-Carter framework, adapted to handle intra-annual (seasonal) variation and regional differences. The model assumes that deaths follow a negative binomial distribution, which is useful for capturing "overdispersion" often found in count data. The log-mortality rate for a specific age group $a$, year $t$, week $w$, and region $r$ is defined as:

$$\ln(\mu_{a,t,w,r}^{base}) = \alpha_{a,r} + \beta_a \kappa_{t,r} + \gamma_a \lambda_{w,r}$$

In this equation, $\alpha_{a,r}$ represents the baseline log-mortality, $\kappa_{t,r}$ is the annual trend, and $\lambda_{w,r}$ captures the seasonal fluctuations throughout the year. The terms $\beta_a$ and $\gamma_a$ determine how sensitive different age groups are to the annual trends and seasonal changes, respectively. To project these into the future, the model identifies structural breakpoints in national trends and uses a random walk with drift for the national component, while regional deviations are modeled as autoregressive processes.

### Stage 2: Deep Learning for Excess Mortality
The second stage focuses on the mortality residuals $R_{a,t,w,r}$, defined as the difference between the observed log-mortality and the baseline estimate:

$$R_{a,t,w,r} = \ln(\mu_{a,t,w,r}^{obs}) - \ln(\mu_{a,t,w,r}^{base}$$

Deep learning architectures are then trained to map climate covariates—such as maximum temperature $T_{Max}$, average temperature $T_{Avg}$, and minimum temperature $T_{Min}$—to these residuals. By doing so, the neural networks learn the complex, non-linear interactions between weather patterns and the spikes in mortality that the baseline model fails to capture.

## Capturing Temporal and Spatial Heterogeneity

The paper introduces two sophisticated deep learning architectures: a Convolutional Neural Network-Long Short-Term Memory (CNN–LSTM) model and a Graph Neural Network-Long Short-Term Memory (GNN–LSTM) model. These architectures are designed to overcome the limitations of simpler benchmarks that assume climate effects are uniform across all ages or regions.

### CNN–LSTM: Local Temporal Patterns
The CNN–LSTM model treats the climate data for a single region as a sequence. It uses a 1D convolutional layer to extract local features from the temporal history of climate variables (e.g., a 4-week window). These features are combined with a learnable "region embedding"—a vector that represents the unique characteristics of a region—and a standardized age scalar. An LSTM layer then processes these features to capture how mortality responses evolve over the observation window. This allows the model to learn that an 85-year-old in a northern region might respond differently to a cold snap than a 65-year-old in a southern region.

### GNN–LSTM: Spatial Interdependence
The GNN–LSTM model takes the framework a step further by incorporating spatial context. Regions are not isolated entities; a heatwave or a flu-intensifying weather system typically moves across neighboring areas. The GNN–LSTM treats the 21 NUTS 2 regions of France as nodes in a graph, where edges are defined by shared borders. 

![region-clusters-side-by-side](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.26980v1/images/regionClusters_side_by_side_gnn_lstm.png)

At each time step, a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) aggregates climate observations from a target region and its neighbors. This spatial information is then fed into an LSTM. By explicitly modeling regional adjacency, the GNN–LSTM can capture how climate shocks propagate and how regional similarities in demographic composition or climate regimes affect mortality.

## Regional Responses and Temperature Sensitivity

The effectiveness of these models was tested using French mortality and climate data from 1990 to 2019. The results indicate that the deep learning approaches significantly outperform both the traditional Lee-Carter baseline and simpler neural network benchmarks like MortFCNet.

### Performance Gains
The GNN–LSTM and CNN–LSTM models reduced the overall test mean squared error (MSE) by approximately 24% compared to the baseline. While the two models performed similarly, the GNN–LSTM showed a slight advantage in regions with smoother mortality dynamics, whereas the CNN–LSTM was slightly better at handling highly volatile regions. A key finding was that the improvement over the baseline increased with age; the deep learning models were particularly effective at capturing the sharp mortality spikes seen in the 90+ age group during winter months.

### Climate Drivers
Permutation feature importance was used to identify which climate variables most influenced the predictions. Temperature variables were the dominant predictors, specifically the mean temperature $T_{Avg}$ at various lags.

![climate-feature-importance](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.26980v1/images/figure_8_gnn_lstm_climate_feature_importance.png)
*{fig:climate-feature-importance}: Relative importance of climate features for the GNN–LSTM model, highlighting the dominance of mean temperature at current (Lag 0) and previous weeks (Lag 1) in predicting excess mortality.*

Interestingly, the models revealed a strong "cold-mortality" response. When the weekly mean temperature dropped below 5°C, the predicted excess mortality rose sharply. In contrast, the "heat-mortality" response was less pronounced in the test period (2015–2019), likely because that specific window lacked the extreme summer heatwaves necessary to train a high-temperature signal. This highlights a critical aspect of these models: they are data-driven and their sensitivity to specific events depends on the historical sequences they have observed.

## Quantifying Uncertainty in Mortality Projections

In actuarial science, a single point estimate is rarely sufficient; understanding the range of possible outcomes is essential for solvency and risk assessment. The researchers developed a unified uncertainty quantification framework that combines three distinct sources of error:

1.  **Baseline Uncertainty:** Propagated through a parametric bootstrap (simulating synthetic death counts and re-estimating the Lee-Carter parameters) and stochastic simulations of future trends.
2.  **Climate Residual Uncertainty:** The deep learning models were extended to a "quantile LSTM" framework. Instead of predicting just the mean residual, the models were trained to output multiple quantiles (e.g., the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles) by minimizing the pinball loss function.
3.  **Combined Distribution:** The predicted quantiles were used to parameterize a split-normal distribution. For each Monte Carlo simulation, a residual $\hat{R}_{a,t,w,r}$ was sampled from this distribution and added to the bootstrapped baseline.

![forecast-uncertainty-gnn-lstm](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.26980v1/images/Region/GNN_LSTM_FRK2_best_age_region_forecast.png)
*{fig:forecast-uncertainty-gnn-lstm}: Log-mortality forecast for the 90+ age group in the Rhône-Alpes region (FRK2), showing the 90% prediction intervals generated by the GNN–LSTM framework effectively capturing observed mortality peaks.*

This integrated approach produced time-varying prediction intervals that adapt to climatic stress. During periods of extreme weather, the intervals naturally widen to reflect higher uncertainty. The evaluation showed that while the baseline model significantly under-covered the actual mortality rates (capturing only 38.6% of observations within its 90% interval), the GNN–LSTM and CNN–LSTM achieved coverage rates of approximately 91%, meeting the nominal target while maintaining precise, informative widths.

## Conclusion and Practical Implications

This research demonstrates that explicitly incorporating climate data and regional spatial structures can lead to more accurate and reliable mortality forecasts. By using a two-stage approach, the models preserve the interpretable structural trends of traditional actuarial methods while leveraging the power of deep learning to handle the volatile, non-linear impacts of the environment.

For the insurance and pension industries, the primary value lies in the granular understanding of risk. The models show that climate-driven mortality is not just a uniform national phenomenon but is deeply tied to geography and age. The ability of the GNN–LSTM to learn spatial dependencies and the quantile framework to produce adaptive uncertainty bands provides a new tool for assessing longevity risk in an era of increasing climate variability. As climate change continues to alter the distribution of extreme weather events, these data-driven frameworks will be essential for ensuring the financial stability of long-term mortality-linked liabilities.
