## Causal Inference in Outcome-Dependent Sampling

Case-control studies are a staple of epidemiology and biomedical research. By sampling individuals based on their outcome status—selecting "cases" who have a disease and "controls" who do not—researchers can efficiently study rare conditions without the enormous time and cost required for large-scale prospective cohorts. However, this efficiency comes at a cost for causal analysis. Because the sampling mechanism depends directly on the outcome, the resulting data does not represent the true distribution of the target population. Specifically, the proportion of cases in the sample is much higher than the actual disease prevalence in the population.

![figure-1-conceptual-flow-of-the-three-stage-weighting-3s-weighting-framework-illustrating-the-transformation-of-outcome-dependent-data-into-pseudo-populations-for-causal-estimation](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.26543v1/x1.png)

Modern causal inference methods, such as Inverse Probability Weighting (IPW), are typically designed for population-based data where individuals are sampled regardless of their outcome. Directly applying these methods to case-control data leads to biased results because the marginal distribution of the outcome is distorted. While previous research has attempted to adapt these methods, most current approaches require researchers to already know the population disease prevalence $\pi$. In many real-world scenarios, this information is unknown or measured with significant error.

This paper develops a unified Three-Stage Weighting (3S-weighting) framework that estimates unknown population prevalence and provides a modular path for both total effect estimation and complex causal mediation analysis. By reconstructing the population distribution from the retrospective sample, the framework allows for population-level causal estimands to be recovered without restrictive assumptions like the rare disease approximation.

## Recovering Population Prevalence from Marginal Constraints

The first major challenge addressed by the 3S-weighting framework is the estimation of the unknown population prevalence $\pi$ directly from the case-control data. This stage leverages the fact that while the joint distribution of covariates $P(X)$ is unknown in the case-control sample, the marginal distributions of individual covariates $P(X_j)$ (e.g., the age or sex distribution of the general population) are often available from census data or health registries.

The framework begins by generating a synthetic population sample $D_{syn}$. This sample is created by independently drawing covariates from their known marginal distributions. From a maximum entropy perspective, this approximates the most conservative joint distribution that satisfies the known marginal constraints. This synthetic sample is then combined with the observed case-control sample $D_{cc}$.

To bridge the gap between the two datasets, the authors use a density ratio estimation technique. They define a binary indicator $Z$ where $Z=1$ for the synthetic sample and $Z=0$ for the case-control sample. A probabilistic classifier $R(X) = P(Z=1|X)$ is trained on the combined data. The ratio of densities $r(X) = P_{pop}(X)/P_{CC}(X)$—which represents how much the case-control covariate distribution deviates from the population distribution—is then estimated as:

$$
r(X) = R(X) / (1-R(X))
$$

Once the density ratio is established, the framework applies a "label-shift" correction. While the intercept in a logistic regression model fitted to case-control data is biased, the conditional odds structure remains informative. The authors utilize the relationship $\text{odds}_{pop}(X) = \alpha(\pi) \times \text{odds}_{CC}(X)$, where $\alpha(\pi)$ is a function of the unknown prevalence. This allows for the definition of a population-level conditional risk function $S_{pop}(X; \pi)$. The final estimate $\hat{\pi}$ is found by solving a fixed-point equation:

$$
\pi = E_{CC}[r(X) \times S_{pop}(X; \pi)] / E_{CC}[r(X)]
$$

This approach allows researchers to recover the true population prevalence even when it was not previously known, provided they have access to external information about the distribution of covariates in the target population.

## Reconstructing the Target Population

Once the population prevalence $\hat{\pi}$ is estimated, the second stage of the framework focuses on reconstructing the target population outcome distribution. This is achieved through design-based weighting.

In a case-control study, the probability of being sampled depends on the outcome $Y$. To undo this distortion, the framework assigns a weight $w^{(D)}_i$ to each individual. For the cases ($Y_i=1$), the weight is $\hat{\pi} / \hat{\pi}_{CC}$, where $\hat{\pi}_{CC}$ is the proportion of cases in the study. For the controls ($Y_i=0$), the weight is $(1-\hat{\pi}) / (1-\hat{\pi}_{CC})$. 

Applying these weights creates a "Pseudo-Population 1" (as seen in {fig:figure-1-conceptual-flow-of-the-three-stage-weighting-3s-weighting-framework-illustrating-the-transformation-of-outcome-dependent-data-into-pseudo-populations-for-causal-estimation}) where the prevalence of the outcome matches the estimated population prevalence. This step effectively transforms the retrospective, outcome-dependent sample into a pseudo-sample that behaves as if it were drawn from a cross-sectional study of the target population. This reconstruction is the key foundation that allows standard causal tools to be used in the final stage.

## Causal and Mediation Weighting

The final stage of the framework applies causal weighting to the reconstructed population to estimate intervention effects. The methodology supports both total causal effects and a detailed decomposition of mediation effects.

### Total Effect Estimation
To estimate the total effect of an exposure $A$ on outcome $Y$, the framework uses standard causal weighting. A propensity score model $e(X) = P(A=1|X)$ is fitted within the weighted pseudo-population to account for confounding. Stabilized treatment weights are then calculated as:

$$
SW_A = P(A=A_i) / P(A=A_i|X_i)
$$

The final weights for total effect estimation, $W_{TE,i} = w^{(D)}_i \times SW_{A,i}$, are applied to a Marginal Structural Model (MSM). For binary outcomes, a log-link model is often used:

$$
\log P(Y=1|A=a) = \alpha_0 + \alpha_a a
$$

The resulting exponentiated coefficient provides the population-level Total Effect relative risk ($RR_{TE}$).

### Mediation Analysis
One of the distinct contributions of this work is the unified treatment of causal mediation analysis. Rather than just looking at the total effect, the framework decomposes the effect into pathways involving a mediator $M$. This decomposition identifies the Pure Direct Effect (PDE), the Pure Indirect Effect (PIE), and the Interaction Effect (InE) on a multiplicative scale.

These effects are defined using nested counterfactual means $E(Y_a, M_{a^*})$, which represent the average outcome if individuals were assigned exposure level $a$ but their mediator was set to the value it would have taken under exposure level $a^*$. To estimate these, the framework constructs specialized mediation weights. These weights utilize mediator models $f_a(M|X) = P(M|A=a,X)$ to create density ratios such as $f(M|A=a^*,X) / f(M|A=a,X)$.

By combining the design weights with these mediation weights, the framework estimates:
1.  **Pure Direct Effect ($RR_{PDE}$):** The effect of the exposure on the outcome that does not go through the mediator.
2.  **Pure Indirect Effect ($RR_{PIE}$):** The effect of the exposure that is mediated by changes in the mediator.
3.  **Interaction Effect ($RR_{InE}$):** The effect due to the interaction between the exposure and the mediator.

The total effect is recovered through the product of these components: $RR_{TE} = RR_{PDE} \times RR_{PIE} \times RR_{InE}$.

## Performance and Empirical Application

The authors validated the 3S-weighting framework using both simulations and an application to the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

### Simulation Results
The simulation study tested the framework's ability to recover true population parameters from retrospective samples of varying sizes. The results, summarized in {fig:figure-2-illustration-of-a-longitudinal-study-design-with-time-varying-exposures-event-occurrences-and-censoring-which-the-framework-can-accommodate-through-sequential-weighting}, show that the estimators are consistent. As the number of cases increases, the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) drops significantly, and the reduction in standard deviation (SD) reaches over 70% for large samples compared to the baseline. Most importantly, the empirical bias remained very small across all scenarios, demonstrating that the reconstruction of the population distribution was successful.

![figure-3-a-root-mean-squared-error-rmse-and-b-percentage-reduction-in-standard-deviation-sd-for-the-various-causal-effect-estimators-as-the-number-of-cases-increases](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.26543v1/x3.png)

### Real-World Application: Smoking, Hypertension, and CVD
The framework was applied to NHANES data to study the effect of cigarette smoking on cardiovascular disease (CVD), with physician-diagnosed hypertension as a potential mediator. By treating the full NHANES cohort as the population and drawing a case-control sample from it, the authors could compare their 3S-weighting results to the "true" population values.

The framework estimated a population prevalence of 11.4%, which was very close to the actual NHANES prevalence of 11.1%. The mediation analysis showed a total effect relative risk of 1.16. Interestingly, the pure direct effect was 1.13, suggesting that the majority of the effect of smoking on CVD in this population occurred through pathways other than hypertension.

## Longitudinal and Dynamic Extensions

The 3S-weighting framework is conceptually flexible enough to be extended to longitudinal settings. In many epidemiological studies, exposures and mediators change over time. The paper outlines a strategy for "Dynamic Case-Control Sampling," where individuals are sampled from risk sets at various time points.

![figure-2-illustration-of-a-longitudinal-study-design-with-time-varying-exposures-event-occurrences-and-censoring-which-the-framework-can-accommodate-through-sequential-weighting](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.26543v1/x2.png)

In this extension, Stage 1 is applied sequentially to estimate time-specific prevalence and density ratios. Stage 2 involves constructing longitudinal design weights that account for the probability of being sampled at each specific time point. Finally, Stage 3 uses longitudinal IPW to adjust for time-varying confounding. This ensures that the framework can be applied to complex survival data and chronic disease trajectories, as illustrated in {fig:figure-3-a-root-mean-squared-error-rmse-and-b-percentage-reduction-in-standard-deviation-sd-for-the-various-causal-effect-estimators-as-the-number-of-cases-increases}.

## Summary of Contributions

The 3S-weighting framework provides a robust alternative to traditional case-control analysis. Its primary strengths are:
*   **Independence from Known Prevalence:** It removes the need for researchers to know the population disease prevalence beforehand by leveraging marginal covariate data.
*   **Unified Mediation Support:** It offers a mathematically grounded way to decompose causal pathways into direct, indirect, and interaction effects without relying on rare-disease assumptions.
*   **Modular Architecture:** By separating the problem into prevalence estimation, population reconstruction, and causal weighting, the framework allows researchers to use familiar causal inference tools (like MSMs) within a retrospective design.

This approach bridges the gap between efficient study designs and rigorous population-level causal inference, providing a path toward a deeper understanding of the mechanisms driving public health outcomes.
