## Challenges of Non-Exchangeability in Time Series

Conformal prediction has gained prominence as a robust framework for generating prediction intervals with mathematically guaranteed coverage. At its core, standard conformal prediction relies on the assumption of exchangeability, which implies that the order of the data points does not affect their joint distribution. While this holds in many static machine learning tasks, it is frequently violated in time-indexed data. Time series often exhibit non-exchangeable structures such as seasonality, recurring regimes, and evolving frequencies. When these dependencies are present, a prediction interval calibrated on historical data may fail to represent the current uncertainty of the system, leading to miscalibrated forecasts.

![coverage-width-tradeoff-across-different-datasets-and-simulations](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.15950v1/figures/12_coverage_width_tradeoff.png)

The research landscape has responded to this challenge with two primary strategies. The first involves "localization," where the model prioritizes calibration residuals from points that are "close" to the current test point, usually in terms of time or covariate space. The second is "online adaptation," such as Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI). ACI dynamically updates the target error rate based on recent prediction performance, widening intervals after a miss and narrowing them after successful coverage. However, both have limitations: temporal localization may ignore relevant but distant historical patterns, while ACI is purely reactive, adjusting only after errors have already occurred.

Spectral Adaptive Conformal Prediction (SACP) introduces a hybrid approach. It defines locality not by time, but by spectral similarity. By identifying historical periods that share the same oscillatory or frequency characteristics as the current window, SACP selects the most relevant residuals for calibration. This "proactive" structural selection is then combined with the "reactive" online update of ACI to handle both recurring patterns and shifting global uncertainty.

## Local Spectral Features for Residual Selection

The foundational innovation of SACP is the use of local spectral features, denoted as $Z_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$, to characterize the behavior of a time series at any given point $i$. These features are extracted from a causal moving window of length $m$ preceding the time index. To maintain the validity of the prediction, the window only includes information available before the prediction time.

Within this window, the local mean is removed to focus on fluctuations. The method then computes Fourier powers at a pre-selected set of frequencies. These powers are normalized so that they sum to one, creating a feature vector that represents the relative frequency content. For instance, a high value in the low-frequency component of $Z_i$ suggests a period of slow, smooth movement, while high values in the high-frequency components indicate rapid oscillations or volatility.

By mapping every point in the time series to this spectral feature space, the method can calculate a spectral distance between a test point at time $t$ and a historical calibration point at time $i$:

$$
d_{spec}(t, i) = \|Z_t - Z_i\|_2
$$

This distance serves as the basis for the weighting mechanism. If the current spectral signature matches a pattern observed three years ago, those distant residuals will receive higher weights than more recent residuals that might belong to a different spectral regime.

## The Weighted Conformal Framework

SACP operates as a calibration layer that sits on top of any point prediction model. Let $\hat{f}(X_t)$ be the point prediction at time $t$. The method first calculates absolute residuals on a calibration set $I_{cal}$:

$$
R_i = |Y_i - \hat{f}(X_i)|
$$

Instead of using the standard empirical quantile of all residuals, SACP calculates a weighted quantile. Each residual $R_i$ is assigned a weight $w_{t,i} \geq 0$ such that $\sum_{i \in I_{cal}} w_{t,i} = 1$. These weights are determined by a Gaussian kernel applied to the spectral distance:

$$
w_{t,i} = \frac{\exp(-\|Z_t - Z_i\|_2^2 / (2b^2))}{\sum_{j \in I_{cal}} \exp(-\|Z_t - Z_j\|_2^2 / (2b^2))}
$$

Here, $b$ is a bandwidth parameter that controls the degree of localization. A small $b$ makes the method very selective, focusing only on the most spectrally similar points, while a large $b$ causes the weights to flatten, approaching the behavior of standard split conformal prediction. The weighted conformal quantile $q_t(\alpha)$ is the value that satisfies:

$$
q_t(\alpha) = \inf \left\{ q : \sum_{i \in I_{cal}} w_{t,i} \cdot \mathbb{I}(R_i \leq q) \geq 1 - \alpha \right\}
$$

The resulting prediction interval is then:

$$
C_t(X_t) = [\hat{f}(X_t) - q_t(\alpha), \hat{f}(X_t) + q_t(\alpha)]
$$

## Online Adaptation of Miscoverage Levels

While spectral weighting helps select the "right" residuals, the scale of uncertainty can still change over time in ways that spectral features might not fully capture. To address this, SACP integrates an adaptive update rule for the target miscoverage level, inspired by ACI.

At each time step $t$, the method uses a time-varying miscoverage level $\alpha_t$. After the true value $Y_t$ is observed, the method determines if the prediction was a "miss" using the indicator:

$$
M_t = \mathbb{I}(Y_t \notin C_{SA,t}(X_t))
$$

The miscoverage level for the next step is updated as:

$$
\alpha_{t+1} = \alpha_t + \gamma(\alpha - M_t)
$$

The parameter $\gamma > 0$ is a learning rate. If the interval fails to cover the true value ($M_t = 1$), $\alpha_{t+1}$ decreases, which forces the next interval to be wider. Conversely, if the interval covers the true value ($M_t = 0$), $\alpha_{t+1}$ increases slightly, allowing the interval to narrow and stay efficient. This mechanism ensures that, in the long run, the average error rate converges to the target $\alpha$, regardless of the underlying data distribution.

## The Importance of Effective Sample Size

A critical challenge in weighted conformal prediction is the potential for quantile instability. If the spectral weighting is too aggressive (i.e., the bandwidth $b$ is too small), the effective number of residuals used to calculate the quantile might be very low. This leads to high variance in the interval width and can cause the method to under-cover.

The authors use the effective sample size $n_{eff}(t)$ as a diagnostic tool:

$$
n_{eff}(t) = \left( \sum_{i \in I_{cal}} w_{t,i}^2 \right)^{-1}
$$

An $n_{eff}$ near 1 indicates that the method is relying almost entirely on a single historical residual, whereas an $n_{eff}$ near the size of the calibration set suggests a broad, stable selection. The research highlights that the performance of SACP is highly sensitive to this balance. In datasets with strong, clean cycles, a high degree of localization is beneficial. However, in noisier datasets, maintaining a larger $n_{eff}$ is necessary to prevent the intervals from becoming erratic.

## Performance Across Simulated and Real-World Data

The utility of SACP was evaluated through both synthetic simulations and real-world economic and environmental data.

### Simulation Experiments
In a "Recurring Regimes" simulation, the data alternated between four distinct oscillatory patterns separated by long intervals. SACP outperformed standard ACI in the most challenging regime, demonstrating its ability to retrieve relevant information from the distant past. In a "Slowly Changing Frequency" (chirp signal) simulation, the adaptive component was found to be essential. Methods without the online $\alpha_t$ update tended to be overly conservative, while SACP and ACI both successfully maintained the 90% target coverage.

![heatmap-showing-coverage-by-regime-in-simulation-1](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.15950v1/figures/15_simulation1_regime_coverage_heatmap.png)

### Real-World U.S. Data
The real-world results provided more nuanced insights into the method's behavior:
*   **U.S. Gasoline Prices:** This series displayed sudden regime shifts and periods of high volatility. SACP achieved 89.5% coverage, significantly better than non-adaptive methods (which fell as low as 46%) and slightly more accurate than standard ACI.
*   **Seattle Daily Max Temperature:** In this seasonal dataset with a high $n_{eff}$, both ACI and SACP performed identically, reaching the 90% target. SACP provided slightly narrower intervals, indicating better efficiency.
*   **U.S. Utilities Production:** This case served as a cautionary example. SACP under-covered (78.8%) because the spectral localization was too aggressive, resulting in a very low $n_{eff}$ (average of ~7). This underscored that spectral features are most effective when the "neighborhoods" in the spectral space are sufficiently populated.

## Future Directions and Impact

The Spectral Adaptive Conformal Prediction framework provides a significant step toward making distribution-free uncertainty quantification practical for complex time series. Its main contribution is the formalization of spectral similarity as a measure of "locality," allowing models to transcend the limitation of only looking at recent history.

Future work may focus on more sophisticated ways to learn the spectral features $Z_i$ rather than using fixed Fourier powers. Additionally, automating the selection of the bandwidth $b$ to ensure a minimum $n_{eff}$ could make the method more robust across diverse datasets. By combining structural awareness with online feedback, SACP offers a template for how conformal prediction can adapt to the non-exchangeable reality of the physical and economic world.
