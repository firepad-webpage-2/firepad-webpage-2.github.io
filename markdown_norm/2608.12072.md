The study of anticipatory neural activity focuses on how the brain prepares for upcoming events. Researchers typically observe signals like the contingent negative variation (CNV)—a slow negative shift in electroencephalography (EEG) data—and explain them using information available to the participant before the event occurs. These factors, known as "past-adapted" explanations, include the time elapsed since a cue, the probability of an event happening (hazard function), and the participant's recent trial history.

While existing models often achieve a high degree of fit to these signals, George Sopasakis and Alexandros Sopasakis argue that "fit" is not the same as "sufficiency." A model might predict an EEG signal well using only historical data, but this does not prove that historical data accounts for *all* systematic variation in that signal. The authors introduce a new methodological framework, **Level II-A**, designed to rigorously test whether past-adapted information is truly sufficient or if the neural signal contains information that relates to events determined only after the signal has been recorded.

## The Information Boundary: Post-Endpoint Randomization

The core principle of the Level II-A framework is **post-endpoint randomization**. To test the limits of what the past can explain, the experiment must establish a strict "information boundary." 

In a typical anticipatory EEG task, a participant sees a warning cue followed by a delay, and then reacts to an "imperative" stimulus. In the Level II-A design, the researchers define a specific EEG endpoint—for example, the average amplitude of the CNV in a window just before the expected event—and "commit" this value at a specific time $t_1$. Crucially, the system determines the actual delay to the imperative stimulus, denoted as $\tau_L$, only *after* the EEG endpoint $A_{\text{pre}}$ has been irrevocably fixed and logged.

Under a purely past-adapted account, the already-committed endpoint $A_{\text{pre}}$ should have no systematic relationship with the future, randomly assigned delay $\tau_L$. Because $\tau_L$ is generated by a computer after $t_1$, it is a randomized negative-control exposure. If a systematic correlation is found between $A_{\text{pre}}$ and $\tau_L$, it suggests that the information set available at $t_1$, denoted by the filtration $\mathcal{F}_{t_1}$, is insufficient to explain the neural signal.

## Operational Integrity and Audits

Establishing this information boundary requires more than just a sequence of events; it requires verifiable "operational integrity." The authors define three foundational conditions (R1–R3) that must be met for the results to be valid:

1.  **Post-endpoint Scheduler (R1):** The software must request the random assignment $\tau_L$ only after receiving a confirmation that the endpoint $A_{\text{pre}}$ is committed. This prevents the brain or the recording system from being "contaminated" by knowledge of the delay before the measurement is finalized.
2.  **Temporal Integrity (R2):** The analysis pipeline must be "causal." It cannot use any future information to process past data. For instance, standard zero-phase filters used in many EEG pipelines are acausal; they "smear" information from the future back into the past. The authors implement a "temporal-leakage audit" that injects pulses into the data after $t_1$ to ensure they do not change the value of the pre-$t_1$ endpoint.
3.  **Retention Neutrality (R3):** Even if the first two conditions hold, selection bias can create spurious correlations. If trials with certain EEG characteristics are more likely to be excluded (e.g., due to artifacts) depending on the assigned delay, a false association appears. Level II-A uses "retention audits" and collider diagnostics to ensure that the final analysis sample is not biased by the joint configuration of the endpoint and the assigned delay.

## The Forward-Only Comparator

To increase the sensitivity of the test, the authors use a "forward-only comparator." This is a predictive model trained to estimate the endpoint $A_{\text{pre}}$ using only past-adapted variables (like the hazard function or previous trial outcomes). 

This comparator is "cross-fitted," meaning it is trained on one portion of the data and used to predict another. Its prediction, $\hat{A}_{\text{pre}}$, is subtracted from the raw endpoint to create a "frozen residual":

$$
A_{\text{pre}}^{\text{resid}} = A_{\text{pre}} - \hat{A}_{\text{pre}}
$$

The analysis then looks for a linear relationship between this residual and the assigned delay. By removing the variation that we *already know* is explained by the past, any remaining signal that correlates with the future delay becomes much more apparent and harder to dismiss as a known anticipatory effect.

## Categorizing Outcomes: The Decision Rule

Level II-A does not simply rely on a single p-value. Instead, it uses a non-compensatory decision rule that classifies each experiment into one of several categories based on audits and statistical thresholds.

*   **Supported Negative Departure:** This occurs when all audits pass, the statistical test for a negative slope ($\beta_{\tau} < 0$) is significant, and the effect size is larger than a pre-defined "resolution floor" $\beta_{\min}$. This outcome indicates that the past-adapted explanation is conditionally insufficient.
*   **Forward-only Adequate (Affirmative Null):** If no effect is found, but the experiment was sensitive enough to detect one (i.e., it passed the "false-adequacy" calibration), the authors can state with confidence that no material effect exists within specific bounds.
*   **Diagnostic/Selection Failure:** If any audit (like the temporal leakage or retention audit) fails, the result is flagged as a failure of experimental integrity rather than a scientific finding about the brain.

## Statistical Inference Routes

The framework provides two primary ways to calculate statistical significance, depending on whether trials are independent or if "carryover" effects exist between them:

1.  **Assignment-Isolation Route:** Used when trials are relatively independent. It relies on a "plus-one" randomization value, $p_{\text{rand}}$, generated by shuffling the delay labels.
2.  **Sequential Martingale Route:** Used when trials might influence one another. This involves calculating "e-values," which are more robust to complex dependencies over time. The e-value for a fold of data is used to derive a sequential p-value:

$$
p_{\text{seq}} = \min\{1, 1/E_{\text{mix}}\}
$$

## Validation via Synthetic Benchmarks

Because human EEG is inherently noisy and its underlying "ground truth" is unknown, the authors validated the Level II-A framework using 1,200 simulated datasets per scenario. This ensures that the pipeline behaves as expected before it is ever applied to real human data.

In the "Clean Forward-Only Null" scenario, where the data was generated purely from past-adapted rules, the pipeline correctly identified **0/1200** as departures. It classified 89.4% as "forward-only adequate," demonstrating its ability to provide a magnitude-qualified affirmative null.

![simulation-results-for-a-past-adapted-dataset-the-residual-shows-no-slope-and-the-result-is-correctly-classified-as-forward-only-adequate](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12072v1/img-2.jpeg)

When a signal was intentionally injected (e.g., a slope of $-60\,\mu\text{V s}^{-1}$), the framework detected it in over 99% of cases. Crucially, when the researchers simulated a "temporal leak" (letting future information slip through), the temporal-leakage audit fired in every single case, blocking the result from being falsely recorded as a scientific discovery.

![simulation-results-where-a-negative-slope-was-injected-the-pipeline-recovers-the-slope-and-classifies-it-as-a-supported-negative-departure](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12072v1/img-3.jpeg)

The framework also proved robust to "collider bias," a subtle form of selection bias where the inclusion of a trial depends on both the EEG endpoint and the delay. The endpoint-by-delay collider diagnostic caught this bias in 99.9% of the simulations, preventing a "false support" outcome.

## Significance and Future Impact

The significance of the Level II-A framework lies in its transparency and its demand for sufficiency rather than mere correlation. In cognitive neuroscience, it offers a way to move beyond the assumption that "the past explains it" and provides a tool to detect when our current models are incomplete.

The framework is highly generalizable. While demonstrated with EEG and the CNV, it can be applied to any field where a measurement is committed before a randomized label is assigned. This includes behavioral science, clinical trial integrity, and the auditing of machine learning models. By establishing clear "resolution floors" (e.g., $15\,\mu\text{V s}^{-1}$) and rigorous audits, Level II-A provides a path toward more reproducible and trustworthy scientific claims.
