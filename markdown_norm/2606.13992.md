The Heston model is a cornerstone of quantitative finance, widely used for pricing and risk-managing derivatives because it accounts for stochastic volatility—the fact that the "speed" of market price changes is not constant. While the model is prized for its analytical tractability, most derivations of its pricing formulas rely on an algebraic ansatz (a clever guess) that the solution takes an "exponential-affine" form. This approach leads to the well-known Riccati ordinary differential equations, but it often obscures the underlying geometric structure that makes such a solution possible.

In "Group Quantization and Mellin Representations of the Heston Model," Santiago García provides a formal geometric foundation for the Heston model. By applying the principles of Group Quantization (GQ), the author demonstrates that the Heston pricing operator and its associated Riccati equations are not independent entities but rather two sides of the same geometric coin. This work moves beyond the standard Black-Scholes setting—which can be described by simple Lie groups—to a more complex framework involving local Lie groupoids and projective flows.

## The Challenge of Stochastic Volatility

In the classic Black-Scholes model, volatility is a fixed constant. This allows the model to be described using a global Lie group, a mathematical structure where "symmetries" (like shifts in time or price) can be composed globally. However, the Heston model assumes that the variance $v$ follows its own stochastic process. Crucially, the diffusion of the price depends on this instantaneous variance.

This state-dependence breaks the global symmetry of the Black-Scholes model. When the variance $v$ approaches zero, the diffusion matrix becomes degenerate. To handle this, the author transitions from Lie groups to a **local Lie groupoid**. While a group allows any two elements to be multiplied, a groupoid only allows composition between elements that "match" according to certain conditions. In this case, the groupoid is locally structured over a base space $B$ that represents the possible states of the variance $v$.

## Constructing the Lifted local Lie Groupoid

The core of the paper’s methodology is the construction of a "lifted" local Lie groupoid, denoted $\tilde{H}$. This object lives in an enlarged space that includes not just the physical variables like price and variance, but also their conjugate momenta and auxiliary fiber variables. An element $g$ of this groupoid is represented by a set of coordinates:
$$
g = (t, M_H(t,k), a, u, \zeta)
$$
where:
- $t$ is the time parameter.
- $M_H(t,k)$ is a transport matrix derived from the Heston generator.
- $a = (x, p_x, v, p_v)^T$ contains the phase-space variables: log-price $x$, variance $v$, and their respective momenta $p_x$ and $p_v$.
- $u$ represents auxiliary variables that help track the "lifting" of the system.
- $\zeta \in \mathbb{R}^+$ is a central scale coordinate.

A unique feature of this work is the use of an $\mathbb{R}^+$-central extension. In traditional quantum mechanics, geometric quantization often uses $U(1)$ (complex numbers with magnitude one) for central variables. However, finance is concerned with prices and discount factors, which are naturally positive and multiplicative. The $\mathbb{R}^+$ choice allows the geometric framework to naturally represent the scaling of money over time and different price levels.

## Polarization: The Duality of Pricing and Transport

The most significant contribution of the paper is showing how the choice of "polarization" determines whether we see the Heston model as a pricing equation or a transport equation. In geometric quantization, polarization is a way of choosing which variables the wave function (or in this case, the pricing function) depends on.

### Coordinate Polarization
If we choose a polarization that depends on the coordinates $(x, v)$, the framework yields a higher-polarization operator $H_H$. When this operator is applied to functions, it reduces to the standard Heston pricing partial differential equation (PDE):
$$
H_{red}^H = \partial_t + (r - \frac{1}{2}v)\partial_x + \kappa(\theta - v)\partial_v + \frac{1}{2}v\partial_x^2 + \rho\sigma_{\nu} v \partial_x\partial_v + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_{\nu}^2 v \partial_v^2
$$
where $r$ is the risk-free rate, $\kappa$ is the rate of mean reversion, $\theta$ is the long-term variance, and $\sigma_{\nu}$ is the volatility of volatility. This PDE describes how an option's price evolves in the space of price and variance.

### Momentum Polarization
If we instead choose a polarization that depends on the momenta $(p_x, p_v)$, the same geometric structure yields the Riccati characteristic equations. In this "momentum space," the evolution of the system is governed by a flow that tracks how the sensitivities of the pricing kernel change over time. This explains why the Heston model can be solved using Riccati equations: they are the "momentum-side" manifestation of the underlying groupoid symmetry.

## Riccati Flows as Projective Actions

The paper provides a new perspective on the Heston Riccati equations. Typically, these are seen as a pair of coupled, non-linear ordinary differential equations. García shows that these equations can be interpreted as a **projectivization** of a linear flow in a two-dimensional space, specifically an $SL(2, \mathbb{R})$ flow.

The Heston Riccati equation for the variance coefficient $D(\tau,q)$ takes the form:
$$
\frac{dD}{d\tau} = \alpha D^2 + \beta D + \gamma
$$
By recognizing this as a projective action, the author can solve for $D$ using simple matrix exponentials of a $2 \times 2$ matrix. This avoids the need for complex analytical derivations or step-by-step numerical integration of the ODE. This projective approach also offers a more stable way to calculate the "affine amplitude" (the part of the pricing kernel not dependent on variance), bypassing common numerical issues related to branch cuts in complex logarithms.

## Mellin Representations and Conserved Momentum

The paper further integrates the Mellin transform into this geometric framework. The Mellin transform is a mathematical tool that decomposes an option's payoff into power-law modes of the form $S^{-q}$, where $S$ is the stock price and $q$ is a complex variable.

In the group-quantization framework, these Mellin modes are shown to correspond exactly to **conserved momentum orbits**. The log-price momentum $k$ is identified with $-q$. Because this momentum is conserved by the groupoid action, the pricing of a complex payoff can be viewed as the propagation of these individual Mellin modes along the Riccati characteristics.

This provides a clear geometric link:
1.  **Payoff Decomposition:** The Mellin transform breaks the payoff into modes $S^{-q}$.
2.  **Momentum Orbit:** Each mode corresponds to a specific momentum $k$.
3.  **Riccati Propagation:** The momentum higher-polarization operator transports these modes through time using the projective $SL(2, \mathbb{R})$ flow.
4.  **Reconstruction:** The final price is reconstructed by integrating these transported modes.

## Significance and Practical Implications

By unifying these concepts, the paper achieves several goals:

-   **Theoretical Consistency:** It shows that the Black-Scholes model is a "reduction" of the Heston framework. If the variance is held constant, the Heston groupoid simplifies back to the standard Lie group structures used in earlier financial quantization research.
-   **Numerical Precision:** As shown in {fig:heston-model-numerical-validation}, the Mellin-projective formulas derived from this geometry match semi-analytic benchmarks with extremely high precision (errors on the order of $10^{-7}$ to $10^{-8}$).
-   **Algorithmic Efficiency:** The projective solution for the Riccati equations suggests more efficient ways to implement Heston pricing in software, as it replaces ODE solvers with matrix operations.

In summary, this research replaces the "ansatz-based" understanding of the Heston model with a rigorous geometric derivation. It demonstrates that the analytical tractability of stochastic volatility models is a direct consequence of the projective symmetries inherent in their underlying local Lie groupoids. For the researcher, it provides a roadmap for extending these techniques to even more complex models, such as those involving jumps or multi-factor volatility.
