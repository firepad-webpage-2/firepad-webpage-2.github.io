## Overcoming the Structural Obstruction in Quantum Many-Body Systems

Finding the ground state of a quantum many-body system—the configuration of lowest energy—is a cornerstone problem in modern physics and chemistry. For a system of $N$ spins, the mathematical space required to describe the state grows as $2^N$. This exponential scaling, often called the "curse of dimensionality," makes exact solutions impossible for systems larger than a few dozen particles. While methods like Tensor Networks (TN) and Neural Quantum States (NQS) have provided powerful approximations, they typically suffer from a "structural obstruction": a model trained for one specific system cannot easily be applied to another with a different size, interaction pattern, or physical layout.

Hamilton-Zero is introduced as a foundation model designed to bypass this limitation. Unlike traditional approaches that optimize a wavefunction from scratch for every new Hamiltonian, Hamilton-Zero is pre-trained on a massive, diverse dataset of 5,000 distinct quadratic spin systems. This allows the model to develop a generalized understanding of quantum interactions, enabling it to predict ground states for entirely new systems "zero-shot" (without further training) or with minimal fine-tuning.

## A Continuous Manifold for Quantum States

A key departure from standard methods is how Hamilton-Zero represents the quantum state. Instead of tracking discrete amplitudes in a high-dimensional Hilbert space, the model treats the state as a smooth function $\psi_{\theta}: SU(2)^N \to \mathbb{C}$ living on a continuous manifold. Each spin $i$ is represented by a unit quaternion $q_i \in SU(2) \cong S^3 \subset \mathbb{R}^4$. This means the input to the neural network scales linearly with the number of spins ($4N$ coordinates) rather than exponentially.

To compute physical observables like energy, the model uses Lie derivatives. Standard spin operators $\sigma_i^a$ are replaced by differential operators $-iL_i^a$ that act on the manifold function. The local energy is then calculated as:

$$ E_{\text{loc}} = \frac{\hat{H}\psi_{\theta}}{\psi_{\theta}} $$

Crucially, the architecture ensures that the calculated energy is a rigorous upper bound to the true ground state energy—a requirement for the variational principle. This is achieved by constraining the network to produce "centrally odd" functions, which satisfy $\psi(..., -q_i, ...) = -\psi(..., q_i, ...)$. According to the Peter–Weyl theorem, this restriction perfectly confines the model's expressive power to the physical spin-1/2 sector, denoted as $\mathcal{F}_{\text{lin}}$, preventing "leakage" into unphysical high-spin states.

## The Foundation Architecture: Trunk and Merge Trees

The model architecture is split into two primary components: the Hamiltonian processing "trunk" and the "readout" tree.

1.  **The Hamiltonian Trunk:** This component acts as a featurizer. It takes the interaction strengths $J$ and local fields $h$ as inputs and processes them through $L$ residual blocks. These blocks use multi-head self-attention and edge-update layers to understand the "topology" of the system—how the spins are connected. A unique feature is the use of "learned absent tokens," which allow the model to recognize when an interaction is missing rather than just being weak, helping it handle diverse graphs.
2.  **The Merge-Tree Readout:** Inspired by tree tensor networks, this part of the model contracts information from individual spins into a single value representing the wavefunction amplitude $\log \psi_{\theta}$. It uses a bit-reversed canonical frame to map physical sites to the leaves of a binary tree. At each node of the tree, a shared "quadrilinear merge" module combines information from sub-regions. Because this module is shared across the entire tree, the model can scale to any system size $N$ that fits within a tree of depth $2^K$.

This separation of concerns—understanding the Hamiltonian's structure versus calculating the state's amplitude—is what allows Hamilton-Zero to generalize across different physics problems without changing its underlying parameters.

## Deep Reinforcement Learning for Optimal Contraction

In tensor network theory, the order in which you contract spins (the "routing") significantly impacts how well the model can capture entanglement. For complex interaction graphs, finding the best routing is a non-trivial optimization problem. Hamilton-Zero solves this by incorporating a router policy trained via Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL).

The router uses a pointer network to decide how to map physical spins to the leaves of the merge tree. It learns to recognize physical structures; for example, in a 2D magnet, it discovers how to group spins into local "plaquettes" to capture local correlations efficiently. This routing policy is Hamiltonian-conditioned, meaning it can look at a new interaction graph and propose an effective entanglement pathway based on its prior training.

## Training and Amortized Performance

Hamilton-Zero was optimized using a specialized version of the Kronecker-Factored Approximate Curvature (KFAC) optimizer, which allows for efficient training of large-scale neural networks by approximating the natural gradient. The training process also utilized a custom MCMC sampler to draw spin configurations $q$ from the distribution $| \psi_{\theta}(q) |^2$. To ensure global exploration of the energy landscape, the sampler employed replica exchange (tempering) with $R$ parallel replicas at different "temperatures" $\beta_1 > \dots > \beta_R$.

The research identified clear scaling laws during pre-training. The error (the "gap" to the exact ground state energy) was found to decrease as compute time $C$ increased, following a power law $C^{-0.61}$. This efficiency is approximately five times better than the scaling seen in large language models.

## Large-Scale Results and Generalization

The model's performance was tested on systems far exceeding its training limits. While pre-trained on systems up to $N=64$, it was applied to lattices as large as $N=8100$ spins.

*   **Zero-Shot Inference:** On unseen systems, Hamilton-Zero provides a high-quality initial guess for the ground state. For instance, it correctly identified the quantum critical point $g_c = 1$ in the Transverse Field Ising Model simply by observing the peak in fidelity susceptibility $\chi_F/N$.
*   **Fine-Tuning:** By freezing the trunk and only training the merge-tree parameters, the model's accuracy can be improved by several orders of magnitude. In many held-out test sets, fine-tuning brought the energy gap to the exact solution (where available for $N \leq 22$) to within $0.01\%$.
*   **Large Systems:** For systems like the 1024-qubit MaxCut problem or carbon chains, the model recovered over $95\%$ of the known best-available results, demonstrating that the structural priors learned during pre-training remain valid at large scales.

## Redefining the Baseline for Quantum Advantage

One of the most significant contributions of Hamilton-Zero is its impact on the search for "quantum advantage." Traditionally, quantum computers are compared to classical algorithms that start from scratch for every problem. Hamilton-Zero changes this comparison by amortizing the cost of training.

The marginal cost to evaluate a new Hamiltonian with Hamilton-Zero is extremely low (estimated at $10^{-6}$ $\text{USD}$ per sample). To demonstrate a true advantage, quantum hardware must not only provide an answer but must do so more cheaply, quickly, and accurately than a pre-trained foundation model like Hamilton-Zero. By providing a differentiable, high-accuracy wavefunction for thousands of spins on classical hardware, Hamilton-Zero raises the bar for what quantum devices must achieve to be considered practically useful.

While currently restricted to quadratic Hamiltonians (where the computational cost of the Hamiltonian action scales as $\text{poly}(2^{\lceil \log_2 N \rceil})$), the framework provides a template for future foundation models in quantum physics, potentially extending to more complex interactions and dynamic simulations. The release of the $\$30,000$ $\text{USD}$ pre-trained weights makes these advanced capabilities accessible to the broader research community.
