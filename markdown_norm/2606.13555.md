## Quantifying Gas Demand Sensitivity in Decentralized Networks

In blockchain networks like Ethereum and its Layer 2 (L2) counterparts, the cost of executing a transaction—commonly known as the "gas fee"—is not static. It fluctuates based on network congestion, a design popularized by mechanisms like EIP-1559. While it is a fundamental economic assumption that users will reduce their demand as prices rise, empirical evidence for the exact sensitivity of this relationship remains scarce. This sensitivity, termed "price elasticity of demand," measures the percentage change in the quantity of gas demanded in response to a one percent change in the gas price.

Estimating this elasticity is critical for several reasons. First, protocol developers need to calibrate the parameters of fee adjustment algorithms to ensure network stability and predictable costs. Second, as networks move toward multi-dimensional resource pricing—where computation, storage, and data availability are priced independently—understanding the specific sensitivity for each resource becomes necessary. Finally, for L2 networks like Arbitrum, which seek to offer lower costs and higher throughput, characterizing user behavior is essential for competitive positioning and capacity planning.

The study addresses these needs by applying rigorous econometric techniques to a massive dataset of Ethereum and Arbitrum transactions. By tracking the behavior of individual "wallets" over time, researchers can isolate the causal effect of price changes on demand, moving beyond simple correlations that are often misleading in congested systems.

## The Endogeneity Challenge in Blockchain Fees

A major hurdle in estimating demand elasticity in blockchains is the problem of "endogeneity." In a system like Ethereum, the base fee is determined by recent network usage. When demand is high, the base fee automatically increases to deter congestion. Consequently, a simple look at the data would show that periods of high fees often coincide with periods of high gas usage.

If a researcher were to perform a simple regression on this data, they might conclude that higher prices lead to higher usage—a positive elasticity that contradicts basic economic theory. This is a classic "simultaneity bias": the price is determined by the demand, while the demand is simultaneously influenced by the price. To solve this, researchers must find a way to isolate "supply-side" price movements that are not directly caused by the immediate demand shocks of the specific users being studied.

The authors address this by using two primary tools: Two-Way Fixed Effects (FE) and Instrumental Variables (IV). The fixed effects allow the model to account for the unique baseline behavior of each wallet (wallet fixed effects) and the broader market conditions at any given time (time fixed effects). The instrumental variable approach uses the previous period's fee as a "proxy" for the current fee. Because the EIP-1559 mechanism adjusts fees gradually, the past fee is highly correlated with the current fee but is determined by past activity, making it a suitable instrument to identify causal shifts in demand.

## Econometric Framework and Model Specification

The core of the analysis relies on a log-log regression model. This specific form is chosen because the coefficient of the price term directly represents the price elasticity. The researchers specify the demand equation as:

$$\log g_{it} = \alpha_i + \gamma_t + \beta \log p_{it} + \phi \log n_{it} + u_{it}$$

In this model, $g_{it}$ represents the total gas used by wallet $i$ during time period $t$. The variable $p_{it}$ is the average base fee the wallet paid, while $n_{it}$ is the number of transactions, which serves as a control variable to isolate the "intensive margin"—how much gas is used per transaction. The terms $\alpha_i$ and $\gamma_t$ are the wallet and time fixed effects, respectively, and $u_{it}$ is the error term.

The coefficient $\beta$ is the value of interest: the price elasticity of demand. To estimate this accurately, the researchers employ a Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) procedure. In the first stage, they predict the current fee using an instrument $z_{it} = \log p_{i,t-1}$, which is the wallet's fee in the prior active period. By using this predicted fee in the second stage, the model filters out the "noise" of immediate demand-driven fee spikes, focusing instead on the persistent price signals that users actually respond to.

## Identifying User Archetypes through Clustering

Blockchain users are not a monolithic group. A high-frequency trading bot behaves differently than an individual user interacting with a decentralized exchange once a week. To account for this heterogeneity, the study uses k-means clustering to segment wallets into six distinct behavioral groups.

![t-sne-of-wallet-clusters](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.13555v2/aft_paper/results/l2_total/tsne_clusters.png)
*{fig:t-sne-of-wallet-clusters}: A t-SNE visualization of wallet clusters on Arbitrum. This clustering helps identify distinct user types, such as "always-on" protocol wallets and "congestion-active" wallets, which exhibit different sensitivities to gas prices.*

The clusters are defined by features such as transaction frequency, average gas per transaction, and the "high-fee fraction"—the percentage of a wallet's activity that occurs when fees are above the median. This segmentation reveals critical insights:
*   **Always-on Wallets:** These are often automated protocols or oracles that must transact regardless of the cost. Their demand is virtually perfectly inelastic (near-zero elasticity).
*   **Congestion-Active Wallets:** Some wallets specifically target periods of high congestion, likely for MEV (Maximum Extractable Value) opportunities. These wallets often show a "false" positive elasticity because their profit opportunities only exist when the network is busy and fees are high.
*   **Fee-Sensitive Wallets:** Individual users or cost-optimized bots that reduce activity or switch to cheaper operations when the base fee rises.

By analyzing these clusters separately, the research provides a more granular view of how different sectors of the crypto-economy respond to price changes.

## Comparing Demand Sensitivity: Ethereum L1 vs. Arbitrum L2

One of the most significant findings is the difference in elasticity between the Ethereum mainnet (L1) and the Arbitrum rollup (L2). 

On Ethereum L1, the pooled IV elasticity was estimated at $-0.0059$. This is an extremely low value, indicating that for every 10% increase in gas fees, demand only drops by about 0.06%. This suggests that users who remain on the L1 mainnet are largely price-insensitive, likely because the transactions they are performing are either highly profitable or mandatory (e.g., protocol maintenance).

In contrast, Arbitrum L2 showed a pooled IV elasticity of $-0.0359$. While still relatively inelastic in absolute terms, it is roughly six times more responsive than L1. This indicates that L2 users are more likely to adjust their behavior in response to fee fluctuations. This makes sense from an ecosystem perspective: users who are highly sensitive to fees have already migrated to L2s to seek lower costs, while those who are not sensitive remain on L1.

Furthermore, the first-stage regression on Arbitrum yielded a coefficient $\pi = 0.052$. This lower value, compared to L1, reflects the fact that Arbitrum's fees frequently hit a minimum floor, limiting the amount of price variation available for the model to analyze.

## Multi-Dimensional Resource Elasticity

Modern blockchains like Arbitrum decompose "gas" into several underlying resources. For example, a transaction might use CPU cycles for computation, write data to permanent storage, or include "calldata" that must be posted to L1. The study provides a unique breakdown of how sensitive demand is for each of these specific dimensions.

![elasticity-comparison-by-dimension](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.13555v2/aft_paper/results/l2_multidim/dim_elasticity_comparison.png)
*{fig:elasticity-comparison-by-dimension}: Median gas usage and elasticity ($\beta$) across different resource dimensions on Arbitrum. Some resources, like storage growth and refunds, show much higher sensitivity than others.*

The results show significant variance across dimensions:
*   **Refunds:** With an elasticity of $-0.2688$, this is the most sensitive category. Users are highly responsive to the cost of operations that trigger gas refunds.
*   **Storage Growth:** This dimension, which tracks the creation of new state entries, showed an elasticity of $-0.1510$. This suggests that users are willing to curb state-expanding operations when prices rise, which is a positive sign for long-term network sustainability.
*   **Computation:** Computation proved to be much less sensitive, with an elasticity of only -0.027. This implies that the core "logic" of transactions is relatively fixed; users might change *how* they store data or post data, but they don't easily change the complexity of the code they execute.

Interestingly, some dimensions like `storageAccessRead` showed positive coefficients. The authors interpret this not as a sign that users want to pay more, but as a "selection effect." When fees are high, simple transactions drop out of the sample, leaving behind complex transactions that inherently require more storage reads. This composition shift can mask the underlying price sensitivity.

## Implications for the Future of Blockchain Economics

The empirical elasticities identified in this paper have immediate practical applications for the design of decentralized systems. 

**Refining EIP-1559:** The low aggregate elasticity suggests that the "base fee" mechanism must be quite aggressive to effectively clear congestion during demand spikes. If users are very slow to reduce their demand as prices rise, the fee adjustment rule must increase prices rapidly to find the market-clearing level.

**Managing State Bloat:** The higher sensitivity of "storage growth" compared to "computation" provides a roadmap for protocol upgrades. If a network wants to limit the growth of its permanent state (which is a major bottleneck for nodes), it can increase the relative price of storage creation. These findings suggest that users will actually respond to such a price signal by reducing their storage footprint, rather than just paying the higher fee.

**L2 Competitive Strategy:** For L2 networks, the higher elasticity suggests that price competition is more effective than on L1. Small reductions in fees on an L2 are more likely to attract or retain usage, whereas L1 demand is dominated by "sticky," price-insensitive activity.

In conclusion, this research moves blockchain economics from the realm of theoretical modeling into empirical measurement. By accounting for user diversity and overcoming the inherent biases of congestion pricing, the study provides the first comprehensive look at how the "invisible hand" of the market operates within the code-governed world of Ethereum and Arbitrum.
