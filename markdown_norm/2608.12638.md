## Thermal Safety in Fast Charging Lithium-ion Batteries

The rapid expansion of electric vehicles and portable electronics has placed significant pressure on the development of fast charging protocols for Lithium-ion (Li-ion) batteries. However, charging at high rates (high C-rates) generates substantial heat within the battery cell due to internal resistance and chemical reactions. This heat leads to a rise in the battery's temperature, which can accelerate material degradation, shorten the battery's lifespan, and, in extreme cases, lead to thermal runaway.

A primary challenge in managing thermal safety is that the internal "core" temperature $T_c$ of a battery cell is typically much higher and more critical than the surface temperature $T_s$ during fast charging. Despite its importance, $T_c$ cannot be directly measured using standard sensors in commercial battery management systems (BMS). Most current systems rely on surface-mounted thermistors, which may fail to detect internal hot spots until it is too late to prevent damage.

This research addresses this gap by integrating a Kolmogorov-Arnold Network (KAN) for core temperature estimation within a Robust Control Barrier Function (rCBF) framework. This combined approach allows for the estimation of the hidden internal state $T_c$ with high accuracy and low computational overhead, while simultaneously providing mathematical guarantees that the battery will remain within safe operating limits even in the presence of system uncertainties and estimation errors.

## Limitations of Current Charging Strategies

Current methods for battery fast charging generally fall into three categories, each with distinct trade-offs:

1.  **Heuristic Methods:** Strategies like multi-stage constant current (MSCC) or pulse charging are simple to implement but rely on predefined rules. They are not adaptive to real-time changes in battery health or ambient conditions, often resulting in overly conservative or accidentally unsafe charging patterns.
2.  **Data-Driven Methods:** Deep learning models can learn complex charging policies from large datasets. While they are often efficient and accurate, they are frequently viewed as "black boxes" that lack interpretability and cannot provide the formal safety guarantees required for critical applications like automotive systems.
3.  **Model-Based Methods:** These use physics-based equations to predict and control battery behavior. While they offer high interpretability and can incorporate safety constraints via techniques like Model Predictive Control (MPC), they are often computationally expensive. Furthermore, they are highly sensitive to model inaccuracies and parameter variations over the battery's life.

The proposed framework seeks to combine the best of these approaches by using a machine learning model (KAN) for fast, interpretable state estimation and a control-theoretic method (rCBF) for rigorous safety enforcement.

## State Estimation via Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KAN)

A central contribution of this work is the use of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KAN) to estimate core temperature. Traditional neural networks (Multi-Layer Perceptrons or MLPs) use fixed activation functions (like ReLU or Sigmoid) at nodes and learn weights on the edges. In contrast, KANs learn nonlinear activation functions directly on the edges of the network.

This architectural shift is based on the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem, which suggests that complex multivariate functions can be broken down into simpler univariate functions. In this battery application, the KAN is trained to map measurable inputs—current $I$, cooling power $\dot{Q}_c$, surface temperature $T_s$, and ambient temperature $T_\infty$—to the estimated core temperature $\hat{T}_c$:

$$\hat{T}_c = (\Theta_G^M \circ \Theta_G^{M-1} \circ \cdots \circ \Theta_G^1)(I, \dot{Q}_c, T_s, T_\infty)$$

This model structure offers three specific benefits for battery management:
- **Efficiency:** KANs can often achieve higher accuracy with significantly fewer parameters than MLPs, making them suitable for real-time execution on embedded BMS hardware.
- **Interpretability:** The learned functions in a KAN can sometimes be converted into symbolic mathematical expressions, providing insight into the thermal dynamics of the battery.
- **Theoretical Error Bounds:** The Kolmogorov-Arnold Approximation Theorem provides an analytical upper bound on the estimation error, expressed as $MG^{-(k+1-m)}$, where $G$ relates to the grid density of the functions and $k$ is the order of the splines used. This error bound is critical for the "robust" part of the safety framework.

## Safety Enforcement with Robust Control Barrier Functions

The framework ensures safety by formulating the charging problem as a constrained optimization. It defines a "safe set" of states where the battery is allowed to operate. These states are bounded by Control Barrier Functions (CBFs). For a battery cell, the safe set is defined by four primary constraints:

1.  **Terminal Voltage:** $B_1(x, u) = \bar{V} - V$ (to prevent overvoltage).
2.  **State-of-Charge (SoC):** $B_2(x) = \overline{SoC} - SoC$ (to prevent overcharging).
3.  **Surface Temperature:** $B_4(x) = \bar{T}_s - T_s$.
4.  **Core Temperature:** $B_3(x) = \bar{T}_c - T_c$.

To maintain safety, the system must ensure that the derivative of these barrier functions satisfies a specific condition. For a system with relative degree $r \ge 1$, the robust condition is:

$$L_f^r B(x) + L_g L_f^{r-1} B(x)U + \Delta_r(x, t) \ge -K_\alpha \eta(x)$$

In this equation, $\Delta_r(x, t)$ represents the uncertainties in the system, such as sensor noise or model inaccuracies $\nu(t)$. By incorporating the KAN's theoretical error bound into the constraint for $B_3(x)$, the framework ensures that even if the estimation of $T_c$ is slightly off, the battery will remain safe.

At each time step, the system solves a Quadratic Programming (QP) problem. The goal is to find a charging current $u_{safe}$ that is as close as possible to the desired high-rate reference $I_{ref}$ without violating any of the barrier function constraints.

## Results and Performance Comparison

The researchers validated the KAN-rCBF framework using a simulated 2.3Ah A123 cylindrical battery cell subjected to a 10C fast-charge profile. They compared their approach to a standard Surface-CBF (S-CBF) method, which only monitors surface temperature.

### KAN Estimation Accuracy
The KAN model proved to be highly accurate in estimating the unmeasured core temperature. As shown in {fig:figure-2-accuracy-of-the-kan-core-temperature-estimator}, the estimated temperature $\hat{T}_c$ closely followed the true core temperature $T_c$ with a Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of only 0.0417K. Crucially, the estimation error $e = T_c - \hat{T}_c$ stayed well within the theoretically predicted bounds.

### Safety Performance Comparison
The limitations of relying solely on surface temperature were evident in the S-CBF results. In that scenario, while the surface temperature was mostly contained, the core temperature $T_c$ spiked to 327.98K, exceeding the safety limit of 323K by nearly 5 degrees.

In contrast, the KAN-rCBF method successfully throttled the charging current as the core temperature approached the 323K limit. While this resulted in a slightly longer charging time (784.54 seconds vs. 712.96 seconds for the S-CBF), it ensured that the battery never entered the "unsafe zone" for either core or surface temperatures.

## Conclusion

The integration of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks with Robust Control Barrier Functions represents a shift toward more transparent and safe battery management. By providing a reliable way to monitor unmeasurable internal temperatures and a rigorous mathematical framework to act on that information, the KAN-rCBF approach mitigates one of the primary risks of fast charging. 

The study demonstrates that while prioritizing internal thermal safety may slightly increase total charging time, the trade-off is essential for preserving battery health and preventing hazardous failures. The efficiency and theoretical error bounds of KANs make them particularly suitable for these safety-critical, real-time control applications in the next generation of electric vehicle power systems.
