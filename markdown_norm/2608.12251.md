## Navigating Nonstationarity in Financial Markets

Financial volatility forecasting is a cornerstone of modern risk management, portfolio construction, and derivative pricing. In equity markets, the challenge lies in predicting future realized volatility for a large cross-section of stocks simultaneously. While deep learning has improved the ability to capture complex, non-linear relationships in financial data, it faces a significant hurdle: nonstationarity. Financial markets transition through distinct regimes, such as periods of calm growth, sudden crises, or volatile recoveries. A model that performs well during a bull market might fail during a liquidity crunch if the underlying relationship between historical indicators and future volatility has shifted.

Incorporating regime information—indicators that describe the current state of the market—is a common strategy to address this. However, the optimal way to integrate these variables into a neural network remains a subject of debate. Many existing approaches simply append regime indicators to the input features of a model. This research investigates the specific pathway through which regime information should flow, proposing a Regime-Gated Residual Mixture-of-Experts (RG-ResMoE) architecture. The study demonstrates that where regime information is placed within the network is just as important as the information itself.

## The RG-ResMoE Architecture

The proposed RG-ResMoE model is designed to disentangle the core forecasting task from the regime-dependent adjustments. Rather than building a single large network that tries to learn everything at once, the authors decompose the prediction into a stable base forecast and a set of expert-driven corrections.

The architecture consists of three primary components:
1.  **A Frozen Base Network:** A simple Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) block, defined as $Block(x; \theta_b)$, is trained first to provide a baseline forecast $\hat{y}_{base}$ using only stock-specific features $x$. Once trained, its parameters $\theta_b$ are frozen. This provides a stable anchor for the model.
2.  **Residual Experts:** A set of $K$ experts, each a separate MLP block $r_k(x) = Block(x; \theta_k)$, is initialized to output zero. During training, these experts learn to provide small "corrections" to the base forecast. Because they are zero-initialized, the model defaults to the base forecast at the start of training, which prevents the optimization from becoming unstable.
3.  **A Soft Gate Network:** The gate $g(u)$ receives a combination of stock features $x$ and regime variables $z$. It produces a set of weights $\pi_k$ (which sum to 1) that determine how much each expert contributes to the final correction.

The final prediction $\hat{y}$ is the sum of the base forecast and the weighted sum of these residual corrections:

$$\hat{y} = \hat{y}_{base} + \hat{y}_{correction}$$

where the correction term is calculated as:

$$\hat{y}_{correction} = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k r_k(x)$$

This structure ensures that the regime information $z$ influences the forecast only by choosing *which* correction to apply, rather than directly altering the internal representations of the stock-specific features $x$.

## Identifying the Optimal Integration Pathway

A central question in the study is the "pathway" of regime information. The authors compare the proposed "Gate Pathway" (where $z$ is used only in the gate) to the common "Input Pathway" (where $z$ is concatenated to $x$ at the very beginning).

The findings suggest that the Input Pathway is frequently detrimental to both performance and training stability. When regime variables like market volatility are added directly to the input, neural networks often experience "training collapse"—situations where the model fails to learn and produces highly inaccurate forecasts. For instance, a capacity-matched baseline using the Input Pathway collapsed in all 30 experimental runs, whereas the RG-ResMoE model completed all runs successfully.

The instability of the Input Pathway is attributed to the fact that regime variables often have a high correlation with the target (volatility) but a different scale and distribution than stock-specific features. Directly injecting them can dominate the gradient signal during training, leading the model to overfit to short-term regime shifts at the expense of long-term predictive power. By restricting regime information to the gate, RG-ResMoE allows the model to switch between experts that are already specialized in stock-level patterns, leading to more robust generalization.

## Experimental Framework and Data

To ensure the results were not specific to a single market or time period, the authors conducted a large-scale evaluation using two major equity panels:
*   **U.S. Equity Panel:** 1,027 stocks from the S&P 1500, observed from 2015 to 2025.
*   **Japanese Equity Panel:** 1,552 stocks from the TSE Prime market over the same period.

The stock-level inputs $x_{i,t}$ for a stock $i$ at time $t$ included 16 features, such as trailing realized volatility, cumulative returns, and technical indicators like the Relative Strength Index (RSI). The regime variables $z_{i,t}$ were specifically chosen to capture different dimensions of market state: market-wide volatility (the 20-day rolling volatility of the equal-weighted market return) and idiosyncratic volatility (the volatility of a stock's residual return after removing market effects).

The models were evaluated using a "walk-forward" approach. This mimics a real-world scenario where a model is periodically retrained on recent data and then used to forecast the next few months. Across 30 non-overlapping test windows, the researchers generated approximately 1.9 million individual forecasts.

## Performance and Stability Results

The RG-ResMoE model demonstrated consistent advantages across several key metrics:
*   **Ranking Accuracy (Information Coefficient):** In the U.S. panel, RG-ResMoE achieved the highest Information Coefficient (IC) of 0.5469, outperforming both simple MLP baselines and standard Mixture-of-Experts architectures. The IC measures the correlation between predicted and actual volatility rankings, which is critical for portfolio managers who need to identify which stocks will be the most or least volatile.
*   **Training Robustness:** One of the most significant results was the elimination of training failures. While standard MoE models collapsed in 24 out of 30 seeds, RG-ResMoE with $K=4$ experts had zero collapses. This suggests that the residual decomposition—grounding the experts in a frozen base—is essential for making MoE architectures practical for financial time series.
*   **Risk Calibration (Value at Risk):** The model's forecasts were used to estimate Value at Risk (VaR), a standard measure of potential loss. RG-ResMoE provided better-calibrated risk estimates, particularly during high-volatility periods like the COVID-19 market crash. During these stress periods, the gains of RG-ResMoE over capacity-matched baselines were amplified, showing its utility when accurate forecasts matter most.
*   **Replication:** The results were mirrored in the Japanese market, where RG-ResMoE again provided the best ranking performance and significantly higher stability than models using the Input Pathway.

## Implications for Future Research

The research provides a specific design principle for building neural networks in finance: regime information should be used to *modulate* corrections rather than to *define* the primary prediction. This disentanglement helps the model handle nonstationarity without sacrificing the stability of the core forecasting logic.

Furthermore, the study clarifies the role of Mixture-of-Experts in this domain. In many fields, MoE is used to scale models to billions of parameters. In financial forecasting, however, the benefit of MoE appears to be structural rather than just a matter of capacity. By providing a gated mechanism for experts to handle different market states, RG-ResMoE manages the "drift" in data distributions that naturally occurs in financial markets.

For practitioners, these results suggest that simply adding more "macro" or "regime" data to an existing neural network may not be enough—and could even be harmful. Instead, adopting a residual architecture that uses a stable base forecast and modulates expert corrections through a gate offers a more reliable path to improved accuracy and training stability.
