In the modern digital economy, every transaction generates a trail of data. When a consumer shops at a grocery store, their purchase history might reveal their health habits to a life insurer. When a user browses a travel site, their behavior might signal their price sensitivity to a car rental agency. This research, "The Privacy Externality of Disclosing Correlated Data," examines the strategic incentives of firms to disclose such data and the resulting consequences for other consumers whose information is correlated with the original buyer.

The core of the study lies in understanding how one firm (the "source" seller) chooses to share information about its customers with another firm (the "downstream" seller). Crucially, the paper identifies a "privacy externality": a phenomenon where the disclosure of one person's data changes the prices offered to entire populations of similar individuals. Because the disclosing firm does not account for the welfare of these third parties, its choice to share data is often socially inefficient.

## The Sequential Contracting Framework

The research models a sequence of two trades. A buyer first interacts with Seller 1 (S1) and subsequently with Seller 2 (S2). The buyer's valuations for the goods offered by these sellers are denoted as $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, respectively. A critical feature of this model is "sequential resolution of uncertainty": the buyer knows their current valuation $\theta_1$ but only learns their future valuation $\theta_2$ at the time of the second trade.

The valuations are assumed to be imperfectly correlated. In the binary version of the model, valuations can be either High ($H$) or Low ($L$). The strength and direction of the correlation are defined by the conditional probabilities:
$$
P(\theta_2=H | \theta_1=H) = \bar{H} \quad \text{and} \quad P(\theta_2=H | \theta_1=L) = \bar{L}
$$
Seller 1 acts as the information designer. Before any trade occurs, S1 commits to a disclosure rule—a mathematical mapping that sends signals to S2 based on the buyer's reported type. This signal changes S2's belief about the buyer's $\theta_2$, which in turn determines the price S2 offers. If S2 believes the buyer is likely a High type, it will set a high price $H$; if it believes the buyer is likely a Low type, it may offer a discount price $L$.

## Disclosure as a Screening Instrument

Why would S1 want to help the buyer get a discount from S2? In this model, disclosure is not just an information transfer; it is a screening instrument. By promising to reveal information that leads to a downstream discount, S1 makes its own contract more attractive. S1 can then extract the value of this future discount by charging the buyer more in the first period.

However, using disclosure is not free. S1 must ensure that the buyer has the incentive to report their type $\theta_1$ truthfully. This introduces two types of incentive costs:
1.  **Information Rent**: If the High type buyer sees that the Low type buyer gets a frequent downstream discount, they might be tempted to lie and claim to be a Low type. To prevent this, S1 must leave the High type some "rent"—essentially a lower price or better terms—which reduces S1's own profit.
2.  **Supply Distortion**: S1 might have to artificially restrict the quantity or quality of the good it sells to the Low type to make the Low type's contract less appealing to the High type.

The paper demonstrates that informative disclosure is strictly optimal for S1 when the correlation is positive ($\bar{H} > \bar{L}$) and the parameters fall within a specific range:
$$
\max\{\bar{L}, \mu_0 \bar{H}\} < L/H < \mu_0 \bar{H} + (1-\mu_0)\bar{L}
$$
where $\mu_0$ is the prior probability that $\theta_1 = H$. This condition shows that disclosure is used to "manufacture" a discount that S2 would not otherwise offer. Interestingly, if the correlation is perfect ($\bar{H}=1, \bar{L}=0$), the incentive cost for S1 becomes so high that it chooses not to disclose anything, preferring complete privacy. This recovers and generalizes previous findings in economic literature.

## Continuous Types and Rationed Disclosure

While the binary model provides intuition, the research extends the analysis to a continuum of buyer types. This transition is mathematically complex because it involves "multidimensional screening"—S1 must choose both the probability of its own trade $x$ and the probability of a downstream discount $\phi$ for every possible buyer type $\theta$.

The paper solves this by introducing a "z-reduction." It shows that for a buyer, the value of the contract can be summarized by a single index $z = x + c\phi$, where $c$ is a constant representing the relative value of the future discount. This reduction allows the researcher to use standard one-dimensional screening tools to find the optimal policy.

![rationed-disclosure](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.15947v1/img-1.jpeg)
*{fig:rationed-disclosure}: The structure of optimal disclosure with a continuum of types. Rather than a simple "reveal or hide" rule, the firm uses "rationed disclosure," where higher types receive the discount with a constant, interior probability.*

A unique finding here is the "rationed disclosure" structure. In many information models, firms use "bang-bang" solutions—either revealing everything or nothing. Here, S1 finds it optimal to pool the lowest types to create a discount but "rations" the discount for higher types, giving it to them only with a fixed probability $\bar{\phi}$. This prevents higher types from mimicking lower ones without requiring S1 to completely destroy the value of the information.

## The Privacy Externality on Correlated Buyers

The most significant contribution of the paper is the characterization of the externality. When S1 discloses the data of a "source" buyer, S2 updates its beliefs about all buyers in the population whose types are correlated with that source.

The impact on a correlated buyer $i$ is defined by a "wedge" $W_i$, which measures the change in that buyer's expected downstream deadweight loss. The externality is not uniform; it can be positive or negative:
-   **Harm**: Buyers who were previously receiving a discount ($q^0_i \le q^*$) might lose it if the disclosure makes S2 believe they are more likely to be High types.
-   **Gain**: Buyers who were previously facing a high price ($q^0_i > q^*$) might receive a discount if the disclosure shifts S2's belief below the pricing threshold $q^* = L/H$.

Crucially, S1's optimal disclosure rule is independent of the distribution of these third parties. S1 only cares about its own relationship with the source buyer. This creates a disconnect: the firm might choose to share data to help one customer get a discount, even if doing so causes a much larger aggregate loss by destroying discounts for thousands of other correlated individuals.

## Policy Implications and Data Regulation

The paper evaluates several common regulatory approaches to data privacy through the lens of this model:

### Consent Mechanisms
The research finds that endowing individual consumers with an "ex-ante consent right"—the ability to veto the use of S1's signals in S2's pricing of them—is highly effective. Because each buyer's individual interest aligns with minimizing their own deadweight loss, they will only consent to data use if it benefits them (creates a discount) and veto it if it harms them (destroys a discount). This "consent dominance" suggests that individual control can implement a socially optimal outcome that blanket bans cannot.

### Data Markets and Liquidity
What happens if S1 can sell its data directly to S2? The model identifies a "liquidity threshold" $\bar{\alpha}$. If the data market is not very liquid (meaning S1 only gets a small cut of S2's increased profit), the disclosure strategy remains unchanged. However, once the market becomes sufficiently liquid, S1's strategy "tips" to full revelation. This implies that the growth of data brokers and sophisticated data markets might lead to a sudden shift toward total transparency, regardless of the incentive costs in the original trade.

### Data Minimization
Regulations that cap the amount of information a firm can disclose (data minimization) can be counterproductive if they are applied broadly. The paper suggests that a more effective approach is to target the "favorable tail" of disclosure—limiting how much a firm can prove a buyer is a "good" type—to prevent the negative externalities that occur when these "good" signals inadvertently expose other "marginal" buyers to higher prices.

## Conclusion

By integrating Bayesian persuasion with sequential screening, this research provides a formal framework for the "privacy externality." It shows that privacy is not just a matter of individual preference but a market equilibrium outcome shaped by correlation and downstream market power. The finding that disclosure acts as a rationed screening tool and carries asymmetric welfare effects for third parties offers a new perspective on why data regulation is necessary and how mechanisms like individual consent can be leveraged to align private incentives with social welfare. The study emphasizes that in a world of correlated data, my privacy is never just about me—it is a shared resource that firms strategically manage to maximize their own gains.
