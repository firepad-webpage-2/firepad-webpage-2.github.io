## Introduction to Genetic Evidence in Drug Discovery

The pharmaceutical industry operates under a challenging reality: approximately 90% of drug candidates that enter Phase 1 clinical trials eventually fail. Most of these failures occur not because of safety concerns, but because the drug simply does not work as intended—a lack of clinical efficacy. To address this, researchers have increasingly turned to human genetics to guide "target selection," the process of choosing which specific protein or gene a drug should interact with to treat a disease.

The core premise is straightforward: if a natural genetic variation in humans is already known to influence a disease, then a drug that mimics that genetic effect is more likely to be successful. Previous landmark studies established that drugs targeting genes with such support are significantly more likely to reach the market. However, these earlier analyses often treated genetic evidence as a simple "yes/no" variable and struggled to account for the "noise" in scientific literature.

This paper, authored by Victoria Paterson from the University of Edinburgh, provides a comprehensive observational analysis of 26,278 target-disease pairs. By integrating six different types of evidence—genetic associations, somatic mutations, literature mining, RNA expression, animal models, and affected pathways—the study quantifies exactly how much each evidence type contributes to drug approval. Crucially, the work introduces methodological refinements to account for biological dependencies between different diseases and the tendency for successful drugs to generate a "halo effect" in published research.

## Measuring Success: The Enrichment of Genetic Support

The primary finding of the study confirms that genetic evidence is a powerful indicator of a drug’s potential for regulatory approval. Across the entire dataset, targets with any form of genetic support (a genetic association score greater than 0) showed an approval rate of 13.6%, compared to only 4.6% for those without. This represents a 3.25-fold enrichment.

To quantify this association more formally, the study uses the Odds Ratio ($OR$), which compares the odds of approval for genetically supported targets against the odds for those without support. The global $OR$ was found to be $OR = 3.25$ ($95\%$ confidence interval: 2.79–3.79, $p = 1.91 \times 10^{-42}$).

![genetic-evidence-enrichment](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.14823v1/results/forest_plot_target_level.png)
*{fig:genetic-evidence-enrichment}: Enrichment of genetic evidence across different therapeutic areas. The plot shows the target-level Odds Ratios, demonstrating that while genetic support is broadly beneficial, its impact varies significantly by disease category.*

The research also identified a "dose-response" relationship. As the stringency of the genetic evidence increased (using higher score thresholds like 0.5 or 0.8), the likelihood of approval rose even further, reaching an $OR$ as high as 5.06 for the strongest associations.

## Methodological Innovations: Pairs vs. Targets

One of the unique contributions of this paper is how it handles the non-independence of data. In drug discovery databases, a single gene (target) might be linked to dozens of different diseases (e.g., TNF-alpha is linked to Crohn's disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and psoriasis). If we treat every target-disease pair as an independent data point, we risk "double-counting" a single successful gene many times over, which can artificially inflate the perceived strength of the evidence.

To solve this, Paterson performed a "target-level" sensitivity analysis. Instead of counting every pair, the analysis looked at unique genes: was *any* drug for this gene approved, and did the gene have *any* genetic evidence? Even after this strict correction, the signal remained robust, with a target-level $OR = 2.79$. This confirms that the genetic advantage is a genuine biological signal and not just a statistical artifact of well-studied genes.

## Therapeutic Area Heterogeneity

Not all diseases are created equal when it comes to genetic guidance. The study broke down the 26,278 pairs into five key therapeutic areas to see if the genetic signal was consistent. The results revealed striking differences:

1.  **CVRM (Cardiovascular, Renal, and Metabolism):** This area showed the strongest genetic signal, with an $OR = 6.04$. This suggests that for metabolic and heart diseases, human genetics is an exceptionally reliable guide for drug success.
2.  **Oncology:** Cancer drug targets also showed strong enrichment ($OR = 2.71$), though the nature of the evidence often involves somatic mutations (mutations that happen in tumor cells) rather than the inherited variations used in other areas.
3.  **Immunology:** This area reached significance at the target level with an $OR = 1.92$.
4.  **Rare Diseases:** Interestingly, rare diseases did not show significant enrichment ($OR = 0.86$). This may be because many rare diseases are "monogenic" (caused by a single gene), and the drug discovery process for them is already so heavily predicated on that known genetic cause that there isn't a "baseline" of non-genetic drugs to compare against.

## The Challenge of Literature Leakage

A significant portion of the paper is dedicated to "literature mining" scores. Platforms like Open Targets use automated tools to scan millions of scientific papers and assign a score based on how often a gene and a disease are mentioned together. While these scores are highly correlated with drug approval, they suffer from a problem called "temporal leakage."

Essentially, once a drug is approved or shows promise in late-stage trials, scientists write more papers about it. This creates a feedback loop where the high literature score might be a *consequence* of the drug's success rather than a *predictor* of it.

To investigate this, the study used feature ablation—systematically removing one type of evidence at a time to see how the model's accuracy changed. By using a temporal split (training on drugs approved before 2015 and testing on those approved after), the study isolated the "pre-approval" signal.

The results showed that while literature mining is the strongest individual predictor in a snapshot of current data, its predictive power is likely inflated. When literature was removed, the remaining biological features (genetics, animal models, etc.) still provided a predictive signal significantly above the random baseline, proving that the underlying biology holds value even without the "hype" of published papers.

## Machine Learning and Predictive Performance

The researcher trained two types of machine learning models—Logistic Regression and XGBoost—to predict whether a specific target-disease pair would reach Phase 4 (approval).

$$OR = \frac{p_{1}/(1-p_{1})}{p_{2}/(1-p_{2})}$$

The models achieved modest discrimination. The primary metric used was the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC), which is useful for imbalanced datasets (where only 5.2% of examples are "positive" approvals). Logistic Regression achieved an $AUPRC = 0.121 \pm 0.015$, which is more than double the random baseline of $0.052$.

![model-performance-curves](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.14823v1/results/roc_prc_curves.png)
*{fig:model-performance-curves}: ROC and Precision-Recall curves for the predictive models. The models show a clear, albeit modest, ability to distinguish between targets that will succeed versus those that remain in early-stage trials.*

However, the models were poorly "calibrated." This means that the probability scores they output (often between $0.25$ and $0.75$) did not match the actual approval rates (which topped out around $12\%$). This led to the conclusion that while these features are excellent for *prioritizing* a list of targets (ranking them from best to worst), they are not yet accurate enough to act as a "crystal ball" for individual drug programs.

## Interpreting the Model with SHAP

To understand *why* the models made certain predictions, the study used SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations). SHAP values quantify the contribution of each feature to the final prediction.

![shap-importance](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.14823v1/results/shap_importance.png)
*{fig:shap-importance}: Feature importance ranking according to SHAP values. Literature mining dominates the model's decisions, followed by somatic mutations and genetic associations.*

One surprising finding from the SHAP analysis was a "suppression effect" involving somatic mutations. While having a somatic mutation generally makes a cancer drug more likely to succeed in a simple one-to-one comparison, in the complex multivariate model, the SHAP value for somatic mutations was sometimes negative. This happens because somatic mutations are often highly correlated with literature mining ($\rho = 0.36$). Once the model has already "seen" the literature evidence, the remaining signal from somatic mutations sometimes appears contradictory. This highlights the importance of using advanced interpretability tools to untangle overlapping data sources.

## Identifying Missed Opportunities

Perhaps the most practical outcome of the research is the identification of "missed opportunities." These are target-disease pairs that have high genetic support but are currently "stalled" in Phase 1 or Phase 2 of clinical development.

The study catalogued 1,433 such pairs. Some of these are targets where the biology is strong, but perhaps the drug molecules developed so far have had poor chemical properties, or the commercial interest has shifted.

![missed-opportunity-targets](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.14823v1/results/missed_opportunities.png)
*{fig:missed-opportunity-targets}: Analysis of targets currently in early clinical phases. Many targets have high genetic association scores (y-axis) but varying levels of literature support, highlighting potential areas for renewed research focus.*

By highlighting targets that have high genetic scores but low literature scores, the study points toward "under-studied" areas of the genome where the next generation of successful drugs might be hiding.

## Conclusion and Significance

The work by Victoria Paterson provides a refined, methodologically rigorous validation of human genetics in drug discovery. By moving beyond simple binary correlations and addressing the complexities of "data leakage" and "gene-level dependencies," it offers a more realistic view of how genetic data should be used in the industry.

The study concludes that while human genetic evidence is not a guarantee of success—approval is still influenced by complex factors like chemistry, dosage, and business strategy—it remains one of the most reliable tools for "tilting the scales" in favor of the patient. For pharmaceutical companies, prioritizing genetically supported targets is not just a scientific preference; it is a statistically validated strategy to improve the efficiency of bringing new medicines to market.
