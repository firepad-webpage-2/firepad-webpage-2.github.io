## Clinical Context and the Need for Precision

In intensive care units (ICUs) and emergency departments, the placement of catheters and tubes is a safety-critical procedure performed multiple times daily. Devices such as endotracheal tubes (ETTs), nasogastric tubes (NETs), and central venous catheters (CVCs) are essential for life support, medication delivery, and monitoring. However, their correct positioning is vital; a misplaced ETT can lead to lung collapse, while an incorrectly positioned CVC can cause cardiac arrhythmias or vessel perforation. 

Standard clinical practice relies on chest X-rays (CXRs) to verify device placement. Given the high volume of these X-rays—many ordered solely for placement confirmation—manual review is time-consuming and vulnerable to human error. Estimates suggest that a small but significant percentage of devices require repositioning after the initial check. Delays in identifying these malpositions can lead to preventable patient complications.

The research presented here introduces UCompCXR, an uncertainty-aware compositional framework designed to automate the localization and assessment of these devices. Unlike previous automated methods that provide general image-level alerts, this approach focuses on instance-level detection, allowing clinicians to identify exactly which device is problematic and providing a measure of spatial uncertainty for its tip location.

## Challenges in Automated Assessment

Current computational approaches to catheter assessment generally fall into two categories: global classification and semantic segmentation. Global classification models predict image-level labels, such as "ETT Abnormal." While useful for triaging, these models do not specify which device is malpositioned in images containing multiple catheters. This lack of localization limits their clinical utility, as the clinician still needs to manually find the problematic device.

Semantic segmentation approaches attempt to trace the paths of devices by predicting a pixel-wise mask. However, these methods typically struggle when multiple devices of the same type overlap or cross paths. In a crowded ICU chest film, traditional segmentation may merge several catheters into a single blob, making it impossible to assess individual placement or determine which specific tip belongs to which path.

Furthermore, most existing models provide "point" estimates for device tips without conveying confidence. In a clinical setting, knowing the model's uncertainty is essential. If a model predicts a tip location but indicates high spatial uncertainty—perhaps due to low image contrast or anatomical occlusion—the clinician knows to exercise more caution. UCompCXR addresses these gaps by explicitly modeling the compositional structure of the devices and estimating the variance associated with its predictions.

## The UCompCXR Architecture

UCompCXR (Uncertainty-Aware Compositional Chest X-Ray) is built upon a lightweight backbone to ensure it can be deployed on standard hospital hardware. The framework utilizes a MobileNetV3-Small encoder, which is an efficient architecture pre-trained on ImageNet. This encoder extracts features at multiple scales, which are then fused using a Compact Feature Pyramid Network (CompactFPN). The resulting unified feature tensor $F$ at stride $4$ (for a $768 \times 768$ input, this results in $192 \times 192$ feature maps) serves as the basis for several specialized prediction heads.

The core of the framework is its compositional, bottom-up design. Rather than trying to detect a long, curving catheter as a single object, the model breaks it down into small "fragments." The system then learns to associate these fragments into coherent device instances. This detect-associate-fuse-classify pipeline allows the model to handle an arbitrary number of devices and maintain individual identities even during overlap.

## Fragment-Based Detection and Association

At every spatial location in the feature map, the model predicts several attributes through dedicated heads:
1.  **Fragment Heatmap:** The probability that a location corresponds to a catheter fragment.
2.  **Geometry:** Endpoint offsets and the local length of the fragment.
3.  **Tangent Direction:** The local orientation of the catheter.
4.  **Discriminative Embedding:** An $8$-dimensional vector used to distinguish different devices. Fragments from the same catheter are trained to have similar embeddings, while those from different catheters are pushed apart.
5.  **Tip Mean and Variance:** Each fragment provides its own estimate of where the device tip is located and the uncertainty (log-variance) of that estimate.

Once fragments are detected, the model builds an association graph. An "EdgeMLP" evaluates pairs of fragments to decide if they belong to the same device. This evaluation is based on geometric cues (like how well their tangents align) and the similarity of their discriminative embeddings. 

To group these fragments into final device instances, the system uses a constrained Union-Find algorithm. Two fragments are merged only if they meet specific criteria: the edge score must be above $0.5$, the resulting topology must remain linear (limiting the degree of any fragment to $2$), and their tip predictions must be consistent. This consistency is checked using the Mahalanobis distance $d$, which accounts for the predicted uncertainty:

$$
d = \sqrt{(\mu_1 - \mu_2)^T (\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2)^{-1} (\mu_1 - \mu_2)}
$$

where $\mu$ represents the predicted tip mean and $\Sigma$ represents the predicted covariance for two fragments. This ensures that fragments are only joined if they agree on the destination of the catheter tip.

## Precision-Weighted Tip Localization

A unique aspect of UCompCXR is how it produces the final tip location for each detected device. Instead of relying on a single prediction, it fuses the tip estimates from all fragments associated with that specific device. 

This fusion process uses Iteratively-Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) with a Huber kernel. Fragments that the model is more confident about (those with lower predicted variance or higher precision) are given more weight in the final calculation. This "precision-weighted" approach makes the system robust to outlier fragments that might be misled by image noise or overlapping structures. 

The output is not just a coordinate, but a full covariance matrix that defines a confidence ellipse. This provides clinicians with a visual representation of the $95\%$ confidence interval for the tip's location, directly communicating the model's reliability for that specific instance.

## Learning from Mixed Supervision

Medical datasets often contain a mixture of "strong" labels (detailed traces of catheters) and "weak" labels (simple image-level classifications). Strongly labeled data is expensive and time-consuming to produce, whereas image-level labels are more abundant.

UCompCXR is designed to learn from both. During training, the model uses a two-phase curriculum. First, it learns localization and association from the strongly labeled images. In the second phase, it incorporates weakly labeled data using a "Noisy-OR" aggregation model. 

In this model, the probability that an image receives a certain label (like "CVC Abnormal") is derived from the per-device predictions $p_i$ of all detected CVCs in that image:

$$
P(\text{image label} = 1) = 1 - \prod_{i} (1 - p_i)
$$

This formulation assumes that if at least one device is abnormal, the entire image is flagged. This allows the network to backpropagate signals from image-level labels down to the individual device instances, effectively "teaching" the placement head even when pixel-level ground truth is missing.

## Results and Benchmarking

The framework was evaluated using the RANZCR CLiP dataset, which contains over $30,083$ chest X-rays. It was compared against a strong "DirectMultiTask" baseline (B2) that uses a standard segmentation and heatmap regression approach without instance-level association.

The evaluation revealed several key findings:
- **Improved Detection:** UCompCXR (B7) detected significantly more device instances than the baseline, increasing sensitivity from $0.674$ to $0.851$. 
- **Reduced False Positives:** The precision of the compositional model was $3.3$ times higher than the baseline, drastically reducing the number of false alerts.
- **Handling Difficult Cases:** While the average tip error appeared higher for B7, a stratified analysis showed this was because B7 was successfully detecting very difficult, thin devices (like NETs) that the baseline missed entirely. For ETTs that both models detected, B7 showed a much lower $95\text{th}$ percentile error ($3.359$ vs $5.735$), indicating fewer "catastrophic" failures where the model predicts a tip far from its actual location.
- **Calibrated Uncertainty:** The tip uncertainty was found to be highly accurate. The predicted $95\%$ confidence ellipses contained the true tip location in $94.8\%$ of cases, demonstrating that the uncertainty estimates are reliable for clinical use.

## Clinical Implications and Efficiency

The ability to perform instance-level assessment is a significant step forward for automated medical imaging. By providing per-device labels and visual confidence intervals, UCompCXR offers actionable information rather than just binary flags. A clinician can see exactly which catheter is borderline and how much they should trust the model's localization of its tip.

From a deployment perspective, the model is highly efficient. With only $2.27\text{M}$ parameters, it is substantially smaller than many common deep learning backbones like ResNet-50 or EfficientNet. This small footprint, combined with a single-pass inference pipeline, makes it feasible to run the system on existing hospital workstations without the need for expensive GPU clusters.

In summary, the transition to a compositional framework allows for a more nuanced and accurate interpretation of complex chest X-rays. By explicitly modeling individual devices and their associated uncertainties, this approach provides a more robust tool for supporting clinical decision-making and improving patient safety in high-stakes environments like the ICU.
