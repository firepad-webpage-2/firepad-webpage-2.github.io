## The Rapid Transition of the Chinese Automobile Market

The global transition toward sustainable transportation has seen its most dramatic acceleration in China. Between 2015 and 2024, the market share of electric vehicles (EVs) in China surged from approximately 1% to over 45%. This shift did not occur in isolation but was driven by a complex interplay of rapid technological advancements, a massive expansion in product variety, declining battery costs, and substantial government interventions. 

Understanding this transition requires disentangling these often-complementary forces. For example, a reduction in battery costs is more effective when manufacturers can integrate that technology into a diverse range of vehicle body types that appeal to different consumer segments. Similarly, government subsidies might have a larger impact when the underlying product quality has already reached a certain threshold.

Research by Yu Hao and Jinge Li addresses these complexities by developing a structural equilibrium model of the Chinese automobile market. Their work provides a quantitative attribution of the EV market share increase and evaluates the welfare consequences of industrial policies. By analyzing a comprehensive dataset of nearly 480,000 product-market-year observations across 79 cities, the study highlights how different drivers shaped the adoption curve and how the benefits of these changes were distributed across society.

## Structural Modeling of Demand and Supply

To analyze the market dynamics, the researchers utilize a differentiated-products framework based on the Berry, Levinsohn, and Pakes (BLP) model. This approach allows for the estimation of consumer preferences and firm costs while accounting for the fact that prices and vehicle attributes are not assigned randomly but are determined by market equilibrium.

On the demand side, the utility a consumer $i$ receives from choosing vehicle $j$ in market $t$ is modeled as:

$$
u_{ijt} = \delta_{jt} + \mu_{ijt} + \epsilon_{ijt}
$$

In this equation, $\delta_{jt}$ represents the mean utility of the product, which is a function of its net price $p_{jt}$, driving range, and other physical characteristics. The term $\mu_{ijt}$ captures individual-level heterogeneity, particularly how price sensitivity varies with household income. The researchers find that lower-income households are significantly more sensitive to price, which is a critical factor when evaluating the impact of subsidies. The demand system is identified using instrumental variables that account for the endogeneity of price and range, ensuring that the estimated effects reflect true consumer preferences rather than unobserved demand shocks $\xi_{jt}$.

On the supply side, the model assumes that firms engage in multi-product Bertrand competition. By maximizing profits, firms set prices such that the margin reflects the price elasticity of demand. This allows the researchers to recover the marginal cost $mc_{jt}$ for every vehicle in the dataset. These costs are then decomposed to understand how specific components, such as battery pack prices, influence the total cost of production. A key finding is that a doubling of the EV driving range increases the marginal cost by roughly 28%, reflecting the significant expense of larger battery capacities.

## Decomposing the Drivers: The Shapley Value Approach

One of the primary challenges in attributing the growth of the EV share is that the contributing factors—quality, variety, cost, and policy—are non-separable. To solve this, the authors employ the Shapley value decomposition. This method calculates the contribution of each "block" of drivers by averaging its marginal effect across all possible sequences in which the blocks could have been introduced.

The researchers partition the 2015–2024 transition into six blocks:
1.  **Quality:** Improvements in vehicle attributes like range and power for existing models.
2.  **Variety:** The expansion of the choice set through the entry of new models and brands.
3.  **Battery:** Cost reductions specifically driven by falling battery prices.
4.  **Subsidy:** Changes in direct government purchase incentives.
5.  **Market:** Shifts in city demographics and overall market size.
6.  **Residual:** Unexplained shifts in demand and costs.

![shapley-waterfall-decomposition](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27924v1/figures/fig_shapley_waterfall_2024_2026_06_15.png)
*{fig:shapley-waterfall-decomposition}: The Shapley decomposition of the 43.64 percentage point increase in EV market share, highlighting Quality and Variety as the leading drivers.*

The results show that the "Quality" block is the single largest driver, accounting for 45.5% of the market share increase. This suggests that the EVs available in 2024 are fundamentally better products than those in 2015. "Variety" follows at 14.8%, emphasizing the importance of diverse offerings (e.g., SUVs, micro-cars, and luxury sedans). Interestingly, the "Subsidy" block shows a negative contribution of -13.6% for the aggregate period. This does not mean subsidies were ineffective; rather, it reflects the fact that subsidies were significantly phased down between 2015 and 2024. The market grew despite the reduction in direct financial support because the improvements in quality and costs more than compensated for the loss of subsidies.

## The Role of Learning and Dynamic Policy Effects

Beyond the direct impact of subsidies on a consumer's wallet, industrial policy often aims to stimulate "learning-by-doing." The paper incorporates this by linking cumulative EV production to future battery costs via Wright’s Law, which posits that costs drop at a consistent rate for every doubling of cumulative output. The authors calibrate a learning rate of 18% based on global battery price trends.

To test the long-term impact of policy, the researchers conduct a "No-Subsidy" counterfactual simulation. They ask: What would the market look like if China had never implemented national EV purchase subsidies?

![dynamic-quantity-channels](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27924v1/figures/fig_dynamic_quantity_channels.png)
*{fig:dynamic-quantity-channels}: Decomposition of the subsidy effect into static demand, learning-by-doing, and product entry channels.*

The simulation reveals that without subsidies, EV adoption would have been delayed by approximately three years. By 2024, annual sales would have been 1.8 million units instead of the actual 12.0 million. This massive difference is driven by three distinct channels:
*   **Static Demand:** The immediate loss of consumers who would only buy an EV if it were cheaper due to a subsidy.
*   **Learning Channel:** Lower early sales lead to higher cumulative costs. This channel accounts for a reduction of 1.2 million vehicles in 2024.
*   **Entry/Exit Channel:** Lower demand discourages firms from introducing new models. This accounts for a reduction of 1.0 million vehicles.

These results highlight that the primary value of early-stage subsidies is not just the immediate sales they generate, but the way they accelerate the entire technological ecosystem toward a tipping point of self-sustaining growth.

## Equity and the Distribution of Benefits

A critical question in public policy is who actually benefits from industrial subsidies. The paper finds that historical EV subsidies in China were largely regressive. Because early EVs were expensive and charging infrastructure was concentrated in wealthy metropolises, the majority of subsidy funds (54.5%) went to consumers in high-income, Tier 1 cities.

As the technology matured and cheaper models like the Wuling Mini EV entered the market, adoption spread to lower-tier cities. However, by the time EVs became widely accessible to middle- and lower-income consumers, the government had already begun phasing out the subsidies. This timing misalignment meant that the "early adopters" in wealthy cities captured a disproportionate share of the financial support.

![subsidy-equity-and-benefit](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.27924v1/figures/fig_subsidy_equity_benefit.png)
*{fig:subsidy-equity-and-benefit}: Comparison of historical subsidy incidence versus a counterfactual where 2015-level subsidies were maintained in the 2024 market, illustrating the regressive nature of the phase-out timing.*

The study also quantifies the welfare impact on firms. Under the "No-Subsidy" scenario, EV-native firms (such as Tesla, BYD, and newer startups) would have seen their sales collapse, while traditional manufacturers of internal combustion engine vehicles would have retained more market share. The research estimates that every 1 yuan of subsidy spent in 2024 generated approximately 3.38 yuan of private surplus (combined consumer and producer benefits), though a large portion of this gain (76%) accrued to producers rather than consumers.

## Implications for Industrial Policy

The research offers several high-level insights for the design of industrial policies in emerging technology sectors:

First, the impact of policy is highly dependent on the state of the technology. Direct subsidies are most powerful when they act as a bridge, allowing a technology to reach the scale necessary for learning-induced cost reductions. However, once the technology reaches a certain level of "Quality" and "Variety," market forces can sustain growth even as subsidies are withdrawn.

Second, the indirect effects of policy—such as fostering innovation and encouraging the entry of new firms—can be just as significant as the direct price effects. The decomposition shows that nearly half of the consumer welfare benefit from subsidies came from the indirect channel of cost reductions achieved through cumulative production.

Finally, the study underscores the importance of considering geographic and income-based heterogeneity. A uniform national policy may have vastly different outcomes across cities with different income levels and infrastructure densities. Future policies could benefit from more targeted approaches that account for these disparities to ensure a more equitable transition to new technologies.

By providing a rigorous, data-driven autopsy of the Chinese EV boom, this work demonstrates that while subsidies were a vital catalyst, the ultimate success of the transition relied on the industry's ability to transform those incentives into better, more diverse, and more affordable products.
