## Integrated Services in 6G: The Hybrid STAR-RIS Architecture

The transition toward sixth-generation (6G) wireless networks marks a fundamental shift in how we utilize electromagnetic waves. Beyond traditional data transmission, future networks are expected to provide high-precision sensing, localization, and wireless power transfer (WPT) simultaneously. This paradigm, often referred to as Integrated Sensing, Communication, and Power Transfer (ISCPT), requires infrastructure that can manage complex propagation environments while maintaining strict energy efficiency. One of the most promising technologies to address these challenges is the Simultaneously Transmitting and Reflecting Reconfigurable Intelligent Surface (STAR-RIS).

Traditional Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces (RIS) are limited by their half-space coverage, acting either as a mirror (reflection) or a window (transmission). STAR-RIS overcomes this by allowing incident signals to be both reflected and transmitted, enabling full-space 360-degree coverage. However, fully passive STAR-RIS designs suffer from "cascaded fading," where the signal strength drops significantly over the two-hop path (base station to surface to user). Conversely, fully active designs, which use amplifiers at every element, consume excessive power and introduce hardware noise. 

This research introduces a hybrid STAR-RIS architecture that dynamically switches between active and passive modes for its elements. By selectively activating only a subset of elements, the system achieves a balance between signal amplification and power consumption. This architecture is integrated into a framework that jointly supports communication users (CUs), energy-harvesting users (EUs), and target localization.

## Architecture and Element Functionality

The system consists of a Base Station (BS) with $M$ antennas serving $K_c$ communication users and $K_p$ energy users. A hybrid STAR-RIS with $N$ elements is deployed to bypass blockages between the BS and the users. Each element in the hybrid surface can be configured in two ways:

1.  **Active State:** The element utilizes an amplification layer, allowing for both phase control and amplitude gain. The amplification gain is denoted as $\rho$, where $\rho \leq \rho_{\max}$. This mode is energy-intensive but significantly boosts the signal-to-noise ratio.
2.  **Passive State:** The element bypasses the amplifier, providing phase-only control. In this mode, the element must satisfy the energy conservation law: $|\beta_{n,t}|^2 + |\beta_{n,r}|^2 \leq 1$, where $\beta_{n,t}$ and $\beta_{n,r}$ represent the transmission and reflection coefficients, respectively.

To facilitate localization, the STAR-RIS is further equipped with a small array of $N_s$ low-cost sensors. These sensors capture the echo signals reflected from a target (such as a UAV or a moving vehicle). The BS transmits a unified signal that includes data for communication, energy for power transfer, and a frequency-modulated continuous wave (FMCW) signal for sensing.

## Resolving the Channel Estimation Challenge

A major hurdle in RIS-assisted systems is acquiring accurate Channel State Information (CSI). Because the RIS elements are usually passive, they cannot process signals or perform traditional channel estimation. The problem is compounded in hybrid STAR-RIS systems due to the presence of both active and passive components and the dual-sided (transmission/reflection) nature of the surface.

The authors propose a tensor-based channel estimation approach to decouple the cascaded channels (BS-to-RIS and RIS-to-user). During an uplink training phase, users transmit orthogonal pilots while the STAR-RIS applies varying phase-shift configurations over $P$ time slots. The received signals at the BS are organized into a three-dimensional tensor $Z \in \mathbb{C}^{M \times K_{u} \times P}$, where $K_u = K_c + K_p$.

By applying a Parallel Factor Analysis with Alternating Least Squares (PARAFAC-ALS) algorithm, the researchers can decompose this tensor to estimate the individual channel components. This method is efficient because it requires relatively low overhead compared to exhaustive search methods and does not rely on the RIS having its own active radio chains for estimation.

## Localization, Communication, and Power Transfer

The system is designed to meet the distinct Quality of Service (QoS) requirements of three different types of tasks:

### target Localization
Localization is performed by analyzing the echo signals received at the STAR-RIS sensors. The target's location is determined by its 2D Angle-of-Arrival (AoA) and its range. The researchers utilize a 2D Multiple Signal Classification (MUSIC) algorithm to find the angular direction of the target. Range estimation is achieved by analyzing the "beat frequency" of the FMCW signal—the difference between the transmitted frequency and the reflected frequency. The accuracy of this process is measured using the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) and compared against the Cramér–Rao Bound (CRB), which represents the theoretical limit of estimation accuracy.

### Communication
For communication users, the goal is to maintain high spectral efficiency. The BS uses Zero-Forcing (ZF) precoding to ensure that data intended for one user does not interfere with others. The performance is quantified by the Signal-to-Interference-plus-Noise Ratio (SINR) at each user, which accounts for both multi-user interference and the noise introduced by the active elements of the STAR-RIS.

### Wireless Power Transfer
Energy-harvesting users require a stable supply of RF energy. This research employs a sigmoidal nonlinear energy harvesting model, which more accurately reflects real-world hardware than simple linear models. The BS uses Maximum-Ratio Transmission (MRT) for these users to concentrate signal power at their specific locations.

## Maximizing Energy Efficiency

The core objective of the optimization framework is to maximize the system's Energy Efficiency (EE), defined as the total communication data rate divided by the total power consumed:

$$\text{EE} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K_c} R_k}{P_{\text{BS}} + P_{\text{RIS}}^{\text{static}} + P_{\text{RIS}}^{\text{act}} + P_{\text{RIS}}^{\text{amp}}}$$

In this equation, $P_{\text{BS}}$ is the base station transmit power, $P_{\text{RIS}}^{\text{static}}$ is the basic circuit power for all elements, $P_{\text{RIS}}^{\text{act}}$ is the additional power consumed by elements in active mode, and $P_{\text{RIS}}^{\text{amp}}$ is the dynamic power used for signal amplification.

Optimizing this ratio is difficult because the variables—transmit power, STAR-RIS phase shifts, and the active/passive mode selection—are deeply coupled. The researchers solve this using an Alternating Optimization (AO) framework, which breaks the problem into three manageable steps:

1.  **Element Mode Selection:** A heuristic-enhanced multi-seed greedy (HE-MSG) algorithm determines which specific elements should be active to provide the most benefit for the least energy.
2.  **Power Allocation:** Dinkelbach’s method and Successive Convex Approximation (SCA) are used to find the optimal power levels for the BS.
3.  **Coefficient Design:** The transmission and reflection coefficients for every element are optimized to satisfy sensing, communication, and energy harvesting constraints.

## Performance Analysis and Results

Numerical evaluations demonstrate that the hybrid architecture provides significant advantages over both fully passive and fully active surfaces.

### Optimal Active Element Ratio
A critical finding of the study is that activating all elements is rarely the most efficient strategy. As shown in the researchers' analysis, the Energy Efficiency typically peaks when only 20% to 40% of the elements are in active mode (denoted by the ratio $\varrho$). Beyond this point, the gains in signal strength are offset by the high power consumption of the amplifiers. For larger surfaces (e.g., $N=100$), the optimal ratio is even lower, highlighting that a small amount of active control can go a long way in overcoming path loss.

### Localization Precision
The localization performance improves significantly with the number of snapshots and the size of the sensor array. As the sensing SINR increases, the 3D RMSE drops rapidly, approaching the theoretical CRB limit. Interestingly, even with smaller sensor arrays, high sensing SINR allows the system to maintain accurate localization, providing flexibility in hardware design.

### Robustness Against Sensing Demands
The study also examines the trade-off between communication and sensing. As the minimum sensing SINR threshold $\gamma_{s}^{\min}$ is increased, the system must redirect resources toward localization, which slightly reduces the communication EE. However, the proposed framework remains feasible even under strict sensing requirements where simpler, non-optimized schemes fail entirely.

## Significance for Future Networks

The hybrid STAR-RIS architecture represents a practical path forward for 6G infrastructure. By integrating sensing and power transfer directly into the communication fabric, and by solving the energy-performance trade-off through hybrid element switching, this work provides a blueprint for multifunctional wireless nodes. 

Key takeaways include:
*   **Selective Activation:** Hybrid designs can outperform fully passive surfaces by up to 6.8 times in terms of energy efficiency by intelligently choosing which elements to amplify.
*   **Full-Space Capability:** The STAR-RIS enables services to be provided to users on both sides of the surface, a feat impossible for standard RIS.
*   **Integrated Design:** The joint optimization of localization, communication, and power transfer ensures that no single service is sacrificed for the others, maintaining a balanced and sustainable network.

This research demonstrates that through sophisticated tensor-based estimation and alternating optimization, the complex interactions of 6G services can be managed effectively within a single, energy-aware hardware platform.
