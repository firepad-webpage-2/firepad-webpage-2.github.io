The rapid integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into social communication has created a recursive feedback loop. AI systems, such as large language models and recommendation algorithms, now actively shape the information that humans consume and share. Simultaneously, these AI systems are frequently retrained on the very data—social media posts, online articles, and user queries—generated by this AI-influenced society. This interaction creates a dynamic environment where informational distortions can propagate, accumulate, and potentially lead to systemic instability.

In "AI Contagion in Social Networks," researchers Olivier Bos and Stefano Bosi develop a formal framework to analyze this interaction. They investigate how informational errors, even small ones resulting from algorithmic bias or approximation, can spread through social networks and feedback into AI training processes. Their work identifies the conditions under which a society's collective knowledge remains stable versus when it faces "informational collapse."

## Quantifying Informational Distortion

To analyze the quality of collective knowledge, the study first establishes a mathematical measure for "distortion." Each agent $i$ in a society of $n$ individuals is assumed to hold a belief or a state of knowledge represented by a probability distribution $P_{i,t}$ at any given time $t$. The "truth" or ground truth distribution is denoted as $\hat{P}$.

The researchers use the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence to quantify the magnitude of distortion for each agent. Specifically, the error $e_{i,t}$ for agent $i$ is defined as:

$$
e_{i,t} := D_{KL}(P_{i,t} \| \hat{P})
$$

This information-theoretic approach is particularly suited for AI-mediated systems because modern AI generates probabilistic outputs. By using KL divergence, the model captures not just simple factual errors but also subtle distributional shifts, biases, and drifts in meaning that occur when information is repeatedly processed and re-shared.

## The AI-Social Communication Network

The model treats society as a network of $n$ agents connected by an attention network. This network is represented by a matrix $S = (s_{ij})$, where each entry $s_{ij}$ signifies the portion of attention agent $i$ pays to agent $j$. The matrix is row-stochastic, meaning the total attention of any agent sums to 1.

The researchers introduce three critical parameters to describe the system's architecture:

1.  **Systemic Amplification ($\alpha$):** This scalar parameter $\alpha > 0$ represents the intensity of information circulation. High values of $\alpha$ might correspond to environments with high algorithmic virality or frequent repetition. The effective social propagation operator is defined as $W := \alpha S$, where the spectral radius $\rho(W) = \alpha$ determines how much social interactions amplify existing beliefs.
2.  **AI Penetration ($\mu$):** This represents the degree to which individuals rely on AI for information. For each agent $i$, a fraction $\mu_i \in [0,1]$ of their information comes from AI, while the rest comes from social peers. The average AI penetration across the population is $\mu = \frac{1}{n} \sum \mu_i$.
3.  **AI-Adjusted Influence ($\tilde{W}$):** As AI penetration increases, the direct influence of human-to-human communication decreases. This is captured by the adjusted matrix $\tilde{W} = (1-\mu)W$.

## Recursive Dynamics: Contagion and Retraining

The core of the model is a two-way feedback loop consisting of the AI Contagion Channel and the AI Social Distortion Multiplier.

In the **AI Contagion Channel**, informational distortions $e_t$ (a vector of errors for all agents) evolve as follows:

$$
e_{t+1} = (1-\delta) \tilde{W} e_t + (1-\delta)\mu \mathbf{1} q_t
$$

Here, $\delta \in (0,1)$ represents the rate at which agents verify information or forget errors, and $q_t$ represents the current distortion present in AI-generated content. This equation shows that social error at time $t+1$ depends on previous social errors (transmitted through the network $\tilde{W}$) and new errors injected by the AI.

In the **AI Social Distortion Multiplier**, the AI system retrains on the data produced by the society it influences. The AI's distortion $q_t$ evolves according to:

$$
q_{t+1} = \gamma q_t + (1-\gamma)\bar{e}_t
$$

In this equation, $\bar{e}_t = \frac{1}{n} \mathbf{1}^\top e_t$ is the average distortion in the society at time $t$. The parameter $\gamma \in [0,1]$ represents the "memory" of the AI system—how much it relies on its previous training data versus new socially generated content. This creates a recursive loop: AI errors infect society, and society's errors are then "taught" back to the AI during retraining.

## Systemic Stability and the Regulatory Frontier

A significant contribution of this research is reducing the complex, high-dimensional system of $n$ agents and one AI into a manageable two-dimensional system. The authors show that because of the way social networks homogenize information over time, the long-run behavior of the entire system can be characterized by tracking only the average social distortion $\bar{e}_t$ and the AI distortion $q_t$.

The aggregate state $x_t := (\bar{e}_t, q_t)^\top$ follows the dynamics:

$$
x_{t+1} = A(\mu, \kappa) x_t
$$

The matrix $A(\mu, \kappa)$ is defined as:

$$
A(\mu, \kappa) = \begin{pmatrix} m & b \\ 1-\gamma & \gamma \end{pmatrix}
$$

Where $m := (1-\delta)(1-\mu)\alpha$ represents internal social diffusion and $b := (1-\delta)\mu(1-\kappa)$ represents the injection of AI distortion, adjusted by a regulatory filtering intensity $\kappa \in [0,1]$.

The stability of the collective knowledge depends on the spectral radius $\rho(A)$. If $\rho(A) < 1$, the system is stable: distortions eventually decay and vanish. If $\rho(A) > 1$, the system enters a state of "informational collapse," where errors grow exponentially through the feedback loop.

The researchers derive a **Regulatory Frontier** $\kappa^*(\mu)$, which is the minimum level of filtering required to keep the system stable for a given level of AI penetration:

$$
\kappa^*(\mu) = \Pi_{[0,1]} \left(1 - \frac{1 - \alpha(1-\delta)(1-\mu)}{(1-\delta)\mu}\right)
$$

This frontier shows that as a society relies more on AI (higher $\mu$), the regulator must work harder (higher $\kappa$) to filter out AI errors. If regulation fails to keep pace with AI penetration, the system crosses the threshold into runaway contagion.

## The Role of Network Topology

The researchers demonstrate that the structure of the social network significantly impacts how distortions persist, even if the absolute stability threshold remains unchanged.

### Homophily and Polarization
Homophily—the tendency of similar individuals to connect with one another—is measured by the second-largest eigenvalue of the attention matrix, $|\lambda_2(S)|$. High homophily indicates a polarized society where information travels slowly between groups. The study finds that while homophily doesn't change the long-term stability condition, it slows down the decay of errors. In practical terms, this means that in polarized networks, distortions linger much longer, potentially requiring even stricter regulation over finite time horizons to prevent societal harm.

### Core-Periphery Structures
Many modern information environments have a "core-periphery" structure, where a small number of influential "core" nodes (like major media outlets or high-reach influencers) broadcast to a "periphery" of many followers.

In such networks, the stationary distribution of influence concentrates on the core. This creates "directional informational fragility": if the core becomes distorted, that distortion quickly spreads to the periphery and is subsequently picked up by the AI's retraining process. This finding suggests that regulation should be asymmetric, focusing more heavily on high-centrality nodes and platforms that act as information hubs.

## Microfoundations: Individual Verification and Systemic Risk

The paper also addresses how individual behavior interacts with these macro-level dynamics. In a social environment, individuals must decide how much effort $a_i$ to invest in verifying the information they receive. This is modeled as a network game where agents face a tradeoff between the cost of verification and the loss associated with holding distorted beliefs.

Strategic complementarities—where an agent's incentive to verify information increases when their neighbors also verify—play a crucial role. The analysis reveals that the equilibrium verification effort $a^*$ for each agent is proportional to their Katz-Bonacich centrality in the network.

Crucially, these individual efforts aggregate into the society-wide verification rate $\delta_{LQ}$:

$$
\delta_{LQ} = \delta_0 + \eta \bar{a}^*
$$

If AI penetration $\mu$ becomes too high, or if the AI's distortion $q$ is too great, it may disincentivize individual verification, leading to a drop in $\delta_{LQ}$. This decrease in verification effectively lowers the barrier for informational collapse, making the system more vulnerable and requiring even more aggressive regulatory intervention.

## Conclusion and Policy Implications

The research by Bos and Bosi provides a theoretical foundation for understanding "AI contagion" as a systemic risk. Unlike traditional information risks that focus on individual malicious actors or specific false claims, this framework highlights how the structural feedback between AI training and social diffusion can create endogenous instability.

The findings have several direct implications for AI governance:
*   **Upstream Regulation:** Because distortions are amplified through retraining loops, intervention is most effective at the source—the training and output phases of AI models.
*   **Asymmetric Responsibility:** Regulatory efforts should be concentrated on high-centrality "core" platforms and influential models that have a disproportionate impact on aggregate social data.
*   **Dynamic Monitoring:** As AI systems become more integrated into social communication, the regulatory requirements for filtering and verification must adapt to the increasing penetration rate to prevent the system from crossing the stability frontier.

By framing informational quality as a stability problem, the paper suggests that maintaining a healthy information ecosystem requires a "macroprudential" approach similar to financial regulation, where the focus is on the resilience of the entire system rather than just the behavior of individual participants.
