## The Challenge of Powering Hyperscale Data Centers

The global digital infrastructure is currently undergoing a massive expansion, driven by the rapid growth of cloud computing and artificial intelligence. Hyperscale data centers, the massive facilities that house this infrastructure, require an immense and continuous supply of electricity. Projections suggest that in the United States alone, data center electricity consumption could account for nearly 12% of national usage by 2028. This demand places significant pressure on existing electrical grids and necessitates new solutions for firm, low-carbon power.

Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) have emerged as a potential solution to this energy challenge. Unlike traditional gigawatt-scale nuclear plants, SMRs are designed with smaller capacities that align better with the loads of large data center campuses. Furthermore, their potential for cogeneration—using waste heat for cooling—offers an attractive pathway toward higher energy efficiency. However, the economic viability of SMRs remains a subject of intense debate due to high capital costs and market uncertainties.

The research analyzed here provides a rigorous techno-economic boundary analysis to determine when colocating an SMR with a hyperscale data center becomes competitive with traditional grid procurement. By modeling a $200 \text{ MW}_e$ facility in the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT) region, the study identifies the specific thresholds for capital costs, market prices, and policy incentives required for nuclear cogeneration to be a viable path forward.

## Modeling Integrated Power and Cooling Systems

To understand the economic trade-offs, the study employs a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) that co-optimizes power supply and cooling dispatch over an 8760-hour annual horizon. This approach accounts for the hourly variability of electricity prices, IT loads, and outdoor weather conditions, which directly affect cooling efficiency.

The analysis compares four primary system configurations:
1.  **Grid-only (Case 0):** The baseline scenario where the data center purchases all electricity from the grid at locational marginal prices (LMP) and uses standard vapor-compression chillers (VCCs) for cooling.
2.  **SMR-only (Case 1):** An on-site BWRX-300 SMR provides the bulk of the power. The reactor is a continuous thermal source ($870 \text{ MW}_{th}$) generating $270 \text{ MW}_e \text{ gross}$ ($243 \text{ MW}_e \text{ net}$).
3.  **SMR + Absorption Cooling (Case 2):** This configuration adds a $160 \text{ MW}_c$ double-effect absorption chiller that uses steam extracted from the SMR’s turbine to provide cooling. This "cogeneration" reduces the electricity needed for cooling but incurs a penalty in the reactor's electrical output.
4.  **On-site Natural Gas (Case 3):** A natural gas combined cycle (NGCC) plant serves as the primary power source, operating in an islanded mode.

The technical modeling includes a "Willans line" for the turbine, which accurately captures how efficiency drops when the reactor operates at partial load. Crucially, the absorption chiller model accounts for an electrical penalty $\alpha_w = 0.20 \text{ MW}_e \text{ MW}_{th}^{-1}$, representing the lost electricity generation when steam is diverted for cooling, and a parasitic load $\beta_a = 0.035 \text{ kW}_e \text{ kW}_c^{-1}$ for internal pumps and fans.

## The Economic Threshold for Nuclear Power

The dominant factor in SMR economics is the initial capital expenditure (CAPEX). The study evaluates three capital cost trajectories: First-of-a-Kind (FOAK), Mid-range (ATB-Mid), and Nth-of-a-Kind (NOAK). 

Under 2023 market conditions, an SMR with mid-range capital costs of $\$7,615 \text{ kW}_e^{-1}$ is not yet competitive with the grid, showing a grid-cost margin of -49% to -62%. This means the nuclear option is significantly more expensive than simply buying power from the ERCOT market. FOAK units, costing roughly $\$14,700 \text{ kW}_e^{-1}$, face even steeper economic hurdles. 

However, the analysis identifies a clear boundary for grid parity. If capital costs can be reduced to approximately $\$5,000 \text{ kW}_e^{-1}$, SMRs begin to compete with the grid, provided they utilize the Section 45Y clean-electricity production tax credit (PTC). At NOAK costs of $\$2,250 \text{ kW}_e^{-1}$, nuclear power becomes highly lucrative, achieving grid parity even without tax incentives.

## The Hidden Costs of "Free" Heat

One of the most significant contributions of this work is the detailed evaluation of absorption cooling. While it is often assumed that using waste heat from a reactor is an efficient way to cool a data center, the economic reality is more complex. 

The study finds that in most scenarios, adding an absorption chiller actually increases the total annualized cost compared to an SMR-only configuration. In the baseline case, the absorption system increased costs by \$9.2 million per year. There are three primary reasons for this:
1.  **Capital Intensity:** Absorption chillers have high upfront costs ($450–1,200 \text{ kW}_c^{-1}$) compared to standard electric chillers.
2.  **Opportunity Cost of Steam:** Diverting steam to the chiller reduces the reactor's electrical output. When electricity prices are high, the value of the lost electricity exceeds the value of the cooling produced.
3.  **Policy Disincentives:** Incentives like the PTC are based on the amount of clean electricity generated. By extracting steam for cooling instead of using it to generate electricity, the facility forfeits a portion of its tax credits.

Absorption cooling only becomes economically viable if the alternative—electric cooling—is very inefficient (e.g., a Power Usage Effectiveness, or PUE, of 1.50 or higher) or if the cost of the absorption equipment falls below $\$60 \text{ kW}_c^{-1}$, which is far below current commercial prices. 

## Market Volatility and Carbon Pricing

The feasibility of on-site nuclear power is heavily dependent on the specific year and the corresponding electricity market dynamics. In 2022, characterized by high natural gas prices and high LMPs, the SMR-only case came within 14% of grid parity. Conversely, in 2024, a year with low market prices, the SMR options were deeply uncompetitive.

Carbon pricing acts as a significant bridge to parity. The study calculates that at mid-range SMR capital costs, a carbon price of $\$53 \text{ tCO}_2^{-1}$ would make the reactor-only case competitive with the grid. At $\$64 \text{ tCO}_2^{-1}$, the cogeneration case (with absorption cooling) also reaches parity. If carbon prices were to reach $\$100 \text{ tCO}_2^{-1}$, both nuclear options would be significantly cheaper than grid procurement, largely because the clean nuclear power can be exported to the grid to earn carbon credits.

## Strategic Implications for Stakeholders

The findings provide a roadmap for several key groups in the energy and technology sectors:

*   **SMR Developers:** The study establishes a clear target for capital cost reduction. To be competitive in moderate markets like ERCOT, SMRs must reach a cost level of approximately $\$5,000 \text{ kW}_e^{-1}$. Operational optimizations like thermal recovery are secondary to these fundamental capital cost reductions.
*   **Data Center Operators:** For facilities requiring high availability and low carbon, SMRs are a technical fit, but the economic risk is tied to long-term market LMPs. The study suggests that for data centers with highly efficient modern cooling ($\eta_{ch} = 0.90$, low PUE), the benefits of thermal integration are minimal compared to the complexity and cost of the systems.
*   **Policymakers:** The research highlights a potential conflict in current incentive structures. Because the PTC rewards electricity production, it can inadvertently discourage thermal efficiency measures like cogeneration. A more holistic policy might include credits for avoided thermal emissions to level the playing field for integrated energy systems.

In summary, while SMR cogeneration offers a promising vision for sustainable hyperscale computing, its success is not guaranteed by technical efficiency alone. The "boundary" for its success is defined primarily by the ability of the nuclear industry to lower construction costs and the willingness of markets or governments to assign a high value to carbon-free, firm energy.
