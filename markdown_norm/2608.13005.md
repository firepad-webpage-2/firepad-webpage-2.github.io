## Introduction to Wideband Angle Imaging

Modern sensing applications, such as millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar, sub-terahertz (sub-THz) imaging, and Integrated Sensing and Communication (ISAC), require the ability to reconstruct high-resolution angular responses of an environment. This process, known as angle imaging, involves identifying the strength of signals reflected from various directions within a specific field of view (FoV). Unlike sparse recovery techniques that assume only a few discrete targets exist, angle imaging aims to reconstruct dense responses, which is essential for perceiving extended objects or complex distributed reflectors.

Historically, angle imaging has relied on narrowband signal models and uniform linear arrays (ULAs). To achieve high resolution without spatial ambiguity (aliasing), these arrays typically require antennas spaced at half the wavelength of the carrier frequency. As the demand for finer resolution grows, the required physical aperture size increases, leading to a massive number of antennas and expensive radio-frequency (RF) chains. While sparse physical arrays can reduce hardware costs, they often introduce "grating lobes" or ambiguities in narrowband systems, making stable imaging difficult without assuming target sparsity.

This research shifts the perspective by leveraging the wideband nature of modern signals. In a wideband system, different frequency components induce distinct spatial phases across the same set of antennas. This phenomenon, often viewed as a nuisance called "beam squint," is utilized here as a form of frequency diversity. By treating each frequency-antenna pair as a unique virtual sensor, a small number of physical antennas can synthesize a much larger, dense "virtual array." This approach allows for high-resolution imaging with significantly reduced hardware, provided the resulting virtual sampling pattern is numerically stable.

## The Virtual Array Framework

The core principle of the proposed method is the interpretation of wideband measurements through a virtual array lens. Consider a wideband signal spanning a frequency range $[f_L, f_H]$ with $M_f$ discrete frequency samples. The receiving array consists of $M_a$ antennas located at positions $p_{m_a}$.

In a narrowband system, the steering vector depends only on the antenna positions and the single carrier frequency. However, in a wideband system, the steering phase for a signal arriving from an angle $\theta$ depends on both the frequency $f$ and the antenna position $p$. This relationship allows us to define a virtual spatial sample $v$ for every combination of frequency and antenna:

$$
v = \frac{f}{f_H} p
$$

The collection of all such points forms the virtual array $\mathbf{V}(F, P_a) = \left\{ \frac{f}{f_H} p : f \in F, p \in P_a \right\}$. Even if the physical antennas $P_a$ are sparse, the variation in $f$ "fills in" the gaps in the virtual domain. The effective resolution of this system is dictated by the highest frequency $f_H$ and the total array width $W$, leading to an angular pixel spacing of $\Delta_u = c / (W f_H)$.

### Frequency-Dependent Channel Model
The researchers extend this model to account for frequency-dependent scattering. Real-world targets do not always reflect all frequencies equally due to variations in radar cross-section or phase delays. They model the channel using a finite-dimensional Fourier-series expansion with $N_b$ basis functions. This results in a comprehensive linear system:

$$
\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{A}_{fd} \text{vec}(\mathbf{G})
$$

where $\mathbf{y}$ is the vector of all measurements, $\mathbf{A}_{fd}$ is the frequency-dependent system matrix, and $\mathbf{G}$ contains the coefficients representing the angular response across different frequencies. The imaging task is then solved as an inverse problem to recover $\mathbf{G}$.

## The Coverage Criterion (CC) for Numerical Stability

A major challenge in using sparse arrays for wideband imaging is ensuring that the synthesized virtual array provides enough information to reconstruct the image stably. If the virtual elements are clustered with large gaps between them, the system matrix $\mathbf{A}_{fd}$ becomes ill-conditioned, meaning that small amounts of noise can lead to massive errors in the recovered image.

The paper introduces the Coverage Criterion (CC) as a computationally efficient alternative to performing a Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) on the large system matrix. The CC is a geometric metric that determines the number of angular pixels $N_{cc,\theta}$ that a specific configuration can support based on the gaps in the virtual array.

The steps to calculate the CC are:
1.  **Define Active Sets**: For each channel basis component, identify the set of frequencies where the signal strength exceeds a threshold $\epsilon$.
2.  **Identify Gaps**: In the resulting virtual array for each component, find the maximum gap $d_{max,i}$ between adjacent virtual elements (including a "wrap-around" gap to account for the periodic nature of the spatial spectrum).
3.  **Determine Pixel Count**: The number of supportable angle pixels is then defined as:

$$
N_{cc,\theta} = \min \left( N_{\theta,\text{max}}, \frac{W}{d_{max,i}} \right)
$$

This criterion ensures that the virtual array samples the spatial field densely enough to satisfy the Nyquist sampling requirement for the desired angular resolution.

### Stability Guarantees
The authors provide a deterministic theoretical bound for the condition number of the system matrix. For a flat channel ($N_b = 1$), they show that the condition number $\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{fl})$ of a Voronoi-weighted version of the system matrix is bounded by:

$$
\kappa(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}_{fl}) \leq 2 \frac{W}{d_{max}} - 1
$$

This result is significant because it provides a mathematical guarantee that as long as the gaps in the virtual array are small relative to the aperture, the imaging process will be numerically stable.

## Designing Sparse Physical Arrays

Building on the Coverage Criterion, the paper proposes a recursive method for designing non-uniform sparse arrays. The goal is to place physical antennas such that the resulting wideband virtual array covers the entire aperture $[0, W)$ without any gaps exceeding the stability threshold.

The design uses a recursive formula to find the next antenna position $p_{m_a+1}$ based on the previous positions and the frequency range:

$$
p_{m_a+1} = \min_i \left( \frac{f_{i,H}}{f_{i,L}} p_{m_a} + \frac{c}{2 f_{i,L}} \right)
$$

This design strategy allows the physical array to be much sparser than a standard ULA. For instance, in a wideband scenario, the physical spacing between antennas can significantly exceed the half-wavelength limit $\Delta_u = c / (2 f_H)$ without causing aliasing in the final image.

## Evaluation and Results

The framework was tested across multiple frequency bands (C-, X-, K-, and W-bands). The results highlight several key findings:

### Efficiency Gains
The sparse array designs demonstrated an ability to support an angular dimension much larger than the number of physical antennas. In some simulations, only 10 antennas were needed to support over 110 angle pixels across the full field of view. This represents an order-of-magnitude reduction in hardware complexity compared to narrowband systems that would require over 100 antennas for the same resolution.

### Impact of Channel Complexity
The researchers found that as the frequency selectivity of the channel (modeled by $N_b$) increases, the number of supportable angle pixels generally decreases. This is because the system must "spend" its measurement degrees of freedom on resolving frequency variations as well as angular directions. 

Interestingly, different frequency bands react differently to this complexity. Bands with a larger ratio of carrier frequency to bandwidth, such as the W-band where $\alpha(1) = f_H / B$ is high, showed more stable performance under high $N_b$ values compared to the C-band. This suggests that higher-frequency bands are naturally more robust to frequency-dependent scattering in a wideband context.

### Numerical Stability
In all CC-guided designs, the condition numbers remained low (typically below 10 for unweighted matrices). Noise robustness tests confirmed that the recovered images maintained low error rates even at moderate signal-to-noise ratios (SNR), validating the theoretical stability bounds.

## Significance of the Work

This research provides a robust theoretical and practical framework for high-resolution sensing with minimal hardware. Its significance lies in several areas:

1.  **Hardware-Efficient Sensing**: By proving that sparse arrays can perform dense angle imaging using wideband signals, the work offers a pathway to reducing the cost and power consumption of radar and ISAC systems.
2.  **Practical Design Tools**: The Coverage Criterion offers a simple, geometric way for engineers to design and verify sensing systems without needing to perform massive matrix computations.
3.  **Stability in Complex Environments**: The inclusion of frequency-dependent scattering models makes this framework applicable to real-world scenarios where targets exhibit complex spectral signatures.
4.  **Optimal Antenna Placement**: The proposed recursive design provides a closed-form solution for antenna placement, moving beyond the trial-and-error or heuristic search methods common in sparse array design.

By turning the "problem" of wideband beam squint into a "solution" for frequency diversity, this framework enables a new generation of high-resolution imaging systems that are both computationally stable and hardware-efficient.
