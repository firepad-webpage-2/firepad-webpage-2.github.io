## Bridging the Gap Between Simulation and Speed

In quantitative finance, the ability to price options accurately and rapidly is a fundamental requirement. However, many sophisticated models that reflect real-world market behaviors—such as volatility clustering and asymmetric responses to shocks—lack analytical formulas. For these models, practitioners typically resort to Monte Carlo simulation, a process that involves generating millions of random paths to estimate a single price. While flexible, this approach is computationally expensive and introduces sampling noise, making it difficult to use in real-time environments or for massive portfolio valuations.

![surrogate-concept](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.15502v1/figures/return_density_compact.png)
*{fig:surrogate-concept}: Comparison between traditional Monte Carlo simulation and the neural network surrogate. The surrogate maps model parameters directly to a terminal return density, allowing for analytical pricing.*

This paper addresses this bottleneck by proposing a methodology for creating neural network surrogates that emulate the behavior of complex volatility models. Specifically, the research focuses on the GJR–GARCH (Glosten-Jagannathan-Runkle GARCH) model, a popular framework that accounts for the fact that market volatility tends to increase more following negative returns than positive ones. The work provides a blueprint for building "error-bounded" surrogates—models that are not only fast but also come with verifiable guarantees that they are as accurate as the simulations they replace.

## The Forward Density Operator

A common pitfall in applying machine learning to option pricing is attempting to regress the network directly on option prices. This can lead to violations of financial logic, such as negative prices or prices that do not change smoothly with strike levels (arbitrage). This work sidesteps these issues by using a "forward density operator." Instead of predicting prices, the neural network predicts the entire probability distribution of the asset's return at the option's maturity.

The paper employs a Mixture Density Network (MDN) that outputs the parameters of a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM). A GMM represents a complex probability density as a weighted sum of several bell curves (normal distributions):

$$
p(x) = \sum_{i=1}^M w_i N(x | \mu_i, \sigma_i^2)
$$

In this equation, $w_i$ are the weights, $\mu_i$ the means, and $\sigma_i$ the standard deviations of each component. This choice is mathematically convenient because the price of a standard European option is the expected payoff under this distribution:

$$
C = e^{-rT} \int g(F_0 e^x) p(x) dx
$$

Since each component of the GMM is lognormal, the total option price becomes a weighted sum of individual Black-Scholes prices. This architecture guarantees that the resulting prices are arbitrage-free by construction. Furthermore, a simple drift correction is applied to the means $\mu_i$ to ensure the no-arbitrage condition $E[F_T] = F_0 e^{rT}$ is satisfied exactly.

## Defining the Noise Floor

Perhaps the most significant contribution of the paper is the formal definition of the "Monte Carlo noise floor." When training a neural network on simulation data, the targets themselves contain random errors due to the finite number of paths used in the simulation. These errors act as a fundamental limit on how accurate the surrogate can become.

The author derives a distribution-free accuracy limit for any surrogate trained on Monte Carlo targets. When evaluating the Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) error, the sampling noise at a specific quantile $q$ has a standard deviation of $\sqrt{q(1-q)/N}$. By averaging this across a uniform grid of quantiles, the paper establishes the expected root-mean-square CDF noise floor:

$$
L_{min} = \sqrt{1/(6N)}
$$

For a typical simulation budget of $N=10^7$ paths, this floor is approximately $1.29 \times 10^{-4}$. This value provides a clear, quantitative target for model developers: once a neural network's error reaches this level, it is statistically indistinguishable from a fresh Monte Carlo simulation. Attempting to train further would simply mean the network is fitting the random noise of the training data.

## Decomposing Error into Actionable Design Rules

The paper presents a systematic framework for understanding why a surrogate might deviate from the true model. The total test error $\epsilon_{test}$ is decomposed into four approximately independent terms:

$$
\epsilon_{test} \approx \sqrt{L_{min}^2 + e_{cap}^2 + e_{cov}^2 + e_{opt}^2}
$$

1.  **Label Noise ($L_{min}$):** The fundamental sampling error from the Monte Carlo process.
2.  **Capacity Bias ($e_{cap}$):** The error occurring if the neural network is too small or the mixture model is too simple to represent the true density.
3.  **Coverage Variance ($e_{cov}$):** The error resulting from gaps in the training data across the parameter space.
4.  **Optimizer Temperature ($e_{opt}$):** The residual jitter caused by the stochastic nature of the training process itself.

Based on this decomposition, the author provides explicit design rules. For a desired precision $\epsilon$, one must use at least $N \geq 1/(6\epsilon^2)$ paths per simulation to push label noise below the target. To handle coverage, one should add parameter cases (using low-discrepancy Sobol sequences) until the test error converges to the training error. Finally, to minimize optimizer noise, the learning rate must be annealed to a final value $\eta^\star \approx (B/c_{opt}) L_{min}^2$, where $B$ is the batch size.

## Training on the GJR–GARCH Model

The methodology was applied to the GJR–GARCH(1,1) model with innovations following a skewed Student-t distribution. To make the learning task easier for the network, the author introduced a Dimensionless Reduced Form (DRF). This transformation removes scale and shift symmetries (like the level of the asset price or the long-run average volatility) so that the network only needs to learn the "shape" of the dynamics rather than the absolute scale.

The training objective used is the "CDF-matching loss." Instead of maximizing the likelihood of individual data points (which can focus too much on the peaks of the distribution), the network is trained to minimize the difference between its predicted CDF and the simulated CDF over a uniform grid of quantiles:

$$
L_{cdf} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{Q} \sum_{q=1}^Q (CDF_{GMM}(x_q) - CDF_{MC}(x_q))^2}
$$

This loss is directly linked to option pricing accuracy, as a small error in the CDF implies a small error in the price across all possible strike prices.

## Results and Performance Benchmarks

The resulting surrogate achieved a mean out-of-sample error of $1.38 \times 10^{-4}$, which is within $7\%$ of the theoretical noise floor of $1.29 \times 10^{-4}$. This demonstrates that the methodology successfully extracted nearly all available information from the training simulations.

![cdf-fitting](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.15502v1/figures/cdf_fitting.png)
*{fig:cdf-fitting}: The neural network's ability to fit the target CDF. The error residuals (bottom panels) show that the surrogate's discrepancy is almost entirely composed of random Monte Carlo noise.*

Key performance findings include:
*   **Speedup:** The surrogate is approximately $4 \times 10^5$ times faster than a comparable Monte Carlo simulation. It can price an entire option chain in roughly $0.1$ milliseconds on a CPU, or at a rate of tens of millions of options per second on a GPU.
*   **Data Efficiency:** Using Sobol sequences for parameter sampling proved more efficient than random sampling, especially when the training set was small.
*   **Input Engineering:** Adding specific features, such as the log of the distance to the non-stationarity boundary ($\kappa \to 1$), halved the residual error in difficult regions of the parameter space.
*   **Error Distribution:** Most of the residual error was concentrated at very short maturities ($T \in \{1, 2, 3\}$) or near the stability limits of the GARCH process, providing clear directions for future architectural refinements.

## Economic Consistency and Sensitivities

Despite being a neural network, the surrogate produces surfaces that are consistent with economic theory. The predicted implied-volatility surfaces show a characteristic "smile" or "skew" where out-of-the-money puts are more expensive than calls, reflecting the market's fear of sharp crashes.

![iv-surface](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.15502v1/figures/iv_surface.png)
*{fig:iv-surface}: A predicted implied volatility surface and corresponding return density. The surface exhibits the standard negative skew and flattening term structure observed in equity markets.*

The model also correctly captures parameter sensitivities. For example, decreasing the degrees-of-freedom parameter ($\nu$) thickens the tails of the return distribution and raises the "wings" of the volatility smile. Increasing the leverage parameter ($\gamma$) steepens the skew, as it makes volatility more sensitive to price drops. Because the network is differentiable, these sensitivities (Greeks) can be calculated analytically and instantaneously, which is a significant advantage for risk management.

## Summary of Contributions

This work moves neural network surrogates beyond being just "black box" approximations. By anchoring the training process to the theoretical Monte Carlo noise floor, the author provides a verifiable way to claim that a machine learning model is as good as a gold-standard simulator. The use of the forward density operator ensures that the output is always a valid probability distribution, preventing financial non-sense like negative prices. For practitioners, this methodology offers a path to using complex, realistic volatility models in high-speed trading and risk management systems that previously had to rely on much simpler, less accurate models.
