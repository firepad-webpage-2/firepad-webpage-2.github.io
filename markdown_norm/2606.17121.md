Predicting how a ship moves through water is a fundamental requirement for both design and safety. Regulatory bodies, such as the International Maritime Organization (IMO), mandate specific manoeuvring standards to ensure that vessels can navigate safely in restricted waters and respond effectively to steering commands. Traditionally, engineers determine the hydrodynamic coefficients—the parameters that describe the forces and moments acting on the hull—through physical experiments or empirical formulas. However, these methods are either logistically expensive or limited in their predictive accuracy. 

System Identification (SI) offers a more flexible alternative by extracting these coefficients from observed ship trajectories. This study investigates how high-fidelity synthetic data generated by Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) can be combined with regularized machine learning techniques to build robust manoeuvring models. By focusing on the KVLCC2 tanker, a common benchmark in naval architecture, the researchers demonstrate how to overcome numerical instabilities and improve the generalization of ship steering models.

## The Abkowitz Mathematical Framework

To simulate ship motion, the study utilizes the Abkowitz model, a three-degree-of-freedom (3-DOF) system that describes movement in the horizontal plane: surge, sway, and yaw. The ship's state is defined by its surge velocity $u$, sway velocity $v$, and yaw rate $r$, while the control input is the rudder angle $\delta$.

The hydrodynamic forces $X$ (longitudinal), $Y$ (transverse), and the yaw moment $N$ are expressed as polynomial expansions of these kinematic variables. For example, the surge equation of motion can be simplified to:

$$(m - X_{\dot{u}}) \dot{u} - m(vr + x_G r^2) = X$$

where $m$ is the ship mass, $x_G$ is the longitudinal center of gravity, and $X_{\dot{u}}$ represents the added mass derivative. The term on the right, $X$, encompasses all hydrodynamic damping and steering forces. In the Abkowitz formulation, these forces are expanded into Taylor series (up to third order) involving terms like $\Delta u$, $v^2$, $r^2$, and $vr$. Identifying these coefficients accurately is essential for a model that can predict a ship's response to various helm commands.

## Generating Synthetic Data via Computational Fluid Dynamics

Instead of relying on physical model tests, which require specialized towing tanks, the authors generate "synthetic" trajectories using Unsteady Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (URANS) simulations. This CFD approach allows for a "virtual free-running test" where the ship's six-degree-of-freedom motion is resolved in a digital environment.

The simulations employ a Volume-of-Fluid (VOF) method to track the water-air interface and a $k$-omega SST turbulence model. To manage the propeller's effect without the extreme computational cost of resolving individual blades, an actuator disk model is used. The ship is subjected to standard manoeuvres, such as zig-zags (where the rudder is toggled between port and starboard angles) and turning circles. These simulations are validated against experimental data from the SIMMAN 2014 workshop, ensuring the synthetic data accurately reflects real-world physics before being used for system identification.

## The Challenge of Multicollinearity in Hydrodynamic Models

A significant hurdle in ship system identification is multicollinearity. This occurs when two or more predictors in the regression model (e.g., $v$ and $r$) are highly correlated. During a standard manoeuvre, sway and yaw often change in tandem, making it difficult for a mathematical solver to distinguish which variable is responsible for a specific change in force. 

When multicollinearity is present, ordinary least squares (LSQ) estimators often produce "cancellation effects," where two coefficients take on unnaturally large, opposing values that cancel each other out during training. While the resulting model might fit the training data perfectly, it often fails catastrophically when asked to predict a different type of manoeuvre. The researchers quantify this using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF):

$$\text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}$$

A high $\text{VIF}_j$ for a predictor $X_j$ indicates that it is almost entirely explained by other variables, signaling that the identified coefficient $x_j$ is likely unstable. The study shows that full Abkowitz models (with up to 68 parameters) suffer from extreme VIF values, necessitating either a reduction in the number of parameters or the use of regularization.

## Regularized Machine Learning Estimators

To address the instability caused by multicollinearity, the study compares four different regression techniques. Each is formulated as a convex Quadratic Programming (QP) problem to ensure a unique global solution.

1. **Least-Squares Regression (LSQ):** The baseline method, which minimizes the sum of squared errors between the predicted and actual forces:
   $$\min_{x} \|Ax - b\|_2^2$$
   While simple, LSQ is highly sensitive to correlated data, leading to poor generalization.

2. **Lasso Regression:** Introduces an L1-norm penalty ($\lambda \|x\|_1$) that forces unimportant coefficients to zero. This acts as an automated feature selection tool:
   $$\min_{x} \left( \|Ax - b\|_2^2 + \lambda \|x\|_1 \right)$$

3. **Ridge Regression:** Introduces an L2-norm penalty ($\lambda \|x\|_2^2$) that shrinks all coefficients toward zero. This stabilizes the model by preventing any single coefficient from becoming excessively large due to correlation:
   $$\min_{x} \left( \|Ax - b\|_2^2 + \lambda \|x\|_2^2 \right)$$

4. **Support Vector Regression (SVR):** Uses an epsilon-insensitive loss function ($\epsilon$), which ignores errors smaller than a certain threshold. This makes the model more robust to noise and outliers in the CFD data.

The results indicate that **Ridge Regression** provides the best balance between numerical stability and predictive accuracy, effectively mitigating the cancellation phenomena that plague standard LSQ methods.

## Determining Optimal Training Length and Data Variety

A practical question for researchers is: *How much CFD data is enough?* Generating high-fidelity CFD results is computationally expensive, so minimizing the simulation time is critical. The study finds that for a zig-zag manoeuvre, a minimum of four overshoots (the points where the ship crosses its original heading) is required for the coefficients to converge to stable values. Using fewer overshoots leads to models that cannot accurately predict turning circles.

Furthermore, the study highlights that a model trained on a single manoeuvre—even a complex one like a 35°/35° zig-zag—often fails to generalize to low-amplitude motions or steady turning. To achieve a "global" model capable of predicting a wide range of ship behaviors, it is necessary to train on a combination of manoeuvres, such as a large-angle zig-zag, a small-angle zig-zag, and a turning circle.

## Comparative Performance and Generalization

The researchers tested the identified models on "unseen" data—manoeuvres that were not used during the training phase. Standard LSQ models trained on single manoeuvres showed significant errors when predicting steady-state turning diameters, often deviating by nearly 30%. 

In contrast, regularized models, particularly those using Ridge regression with multiple input manoeuvres, maintained high fidelity across different dynamic regimes. By applying a penalty to the coefficient magnitudes, the Ridge estimator preserved the underlying physical relationships between velocity and force, preventing the mathematical "overfitting" that occurs when the solver exploits correlations in the training data.

## Practical Implications for Naval Architecture

This research provides a clear roadmap for using machine learning to enhance ship design. By using synthetic CFD data, designers can identify hydrodynamic characteristics early in the development cycle, before physical models are even built.

The primary takeaway is that **regularization is not just a statistical luxury but a necessity** for Abkowitz-type models due to the inherent correlation between ship motion variables. Ridge regression, specifically, emerges as a computationally efficient and robust tool for this purpose. The study also establishes that a diverse training set—combining transient zig-zags with steady turning circles—is essential for creating a model that remains accurate across the full operational envelope of the vessel. These findings allow for safer, more reliable ship performance predictions, ultimately contributing to more efficient maritime transport.
