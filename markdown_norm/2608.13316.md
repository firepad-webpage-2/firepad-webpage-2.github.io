## The Shift to Foundation Models in Movement Data

In recent years, the paradigm of foundation models (FMs) has transformed artificial intelligence, moving from task-specific models to large-scale architectures pretrained on vast, unlabeled datasets. While these models have seen significant adoption in natural language processing and computer vision, their application to biomedical movement data—specifically data collected from wearable accelerometers—is a developing frontier. Human movement data provides a continuous window into health, enabling applications like human activity recognition (HAR), fall detection, sleep monitoring, and stress assessment.

Current research explores whether these FMs can outperform traditional supervised learning methods, which often require large amounts of carefully labeled data. However, the field has lacked a systematic evaluation of these models across varied tasks and deployment conditions. This research addresses that gap by conducting a comprehensive benchmarking study of four prominent open-source FMs for movement data. The study moves beyond simple performance metrics to analyze computational efficiency, robustness to sensor placement, and the internal representations learned by these models.

## The Benchmark: Foundation Models vs. Baselines

The study evaluates four diverse foundation models, each characterized by different architectures and pretraining strategies:
- **NormWear:** A Transformer-based model pretrained using a Masked Autoencoder (MAE) approach on a large-scale wearable dataset.
- **UniMTS:** A Graph-Transformer architecture trained through multi-task self-supervision.
- **Oxford SSL:** A ResNet-based model utilizing contrastive learning on triaxial acceleration data.
- **ElderNet:** An architecture similar to Oxford SSL but specifically tailored and further pretrained for older populations.

These models, ranging from 5 million to 136 million parameters, are compared against four strong supervised baselines: TinyHAR, XResNet1D, Inception1D, and S4. These baselines represent a mix of convolutional neural networks (CNNs), state-space models, and attention-based architectures, trained from scratch on target datasets. The comparison aims to identify if the pretraining phase of FMs confers a measurable advantage over models specifically optimized for a single dataset.

## Evaluation Modes and Training Methodology

A critical contribution of this work is the use of three distinct evaluation modes to assess how FMs adapt to new tasks:
1. **Finetuning:** All model weights are updated. This represents the scenario where practitioners have enough labeled data to adjust the entire model.
2. **Linear Evaluation:** The FM is frozen, and only a linear head is trained on its final output. This tests the quality of the model's fixed representations.
3. **Frozen Evaluation:** The FM remains frozen, but a learnable query-attention head is used. This allows for non-linear read-out from the frozen features, providing a middle ground between linear probing and full finetuning.

The evaluation spans 10 public datasets and 19 tasks, categorized into HAR, fall detection, sleep staging, stress detection, and demographic regression. All models were trained using a unified pipeline where input data consisted of triaxial acceleration windows. FMs maintained their native sampling frequencies $f_s$ and input transformations. Performance was measured using $\text{AUROC}$ for classification tasks and $\text{MAE}$ for demographic regression (age, weight, height). Statistical significance was determined through pairwise bootstrapping to ensure that differences in rankings were not due to chance.

## Performance Analysis Across Health Tasks

The results indicate that FMs do not consistently outperform supervised baselines in standard HAR tasks. For instance, on the RealWorld and PAMAP2 datasets, supervised models like Inception1D and S4 performed competitively with, and sometimes better than, FMs. However, FMs showed clear advantages in more specialized or critical health tasks.

In fall detection (KFall and SisFall datasets), Oxford SSL emerged as the strongest performer, followed by UniMTS. This suggests that the features learned during self-supervised pretraining are particularly well-suited for identifying the high-impact, anomalous patterns characteristic of a fall. Similarly, in stress and affect detection, Oxford SSL and ElderNet led the rankings, although absolute performance for all models remained relatively low, highlighting the inherent difficulty of detecting psychological stress using only accelerometer data.

Sleep staging proved to be the most challenging category for all models, with performance hovering near chance levels ($\text{AUROC}$ between 0.5 and 0.6). In this domain, supervised baselines often matched or slightly exceeded FM performance, indicating that the general movement features learned by current FMs may not adequately capture the subtle physiological shifts associated with sleep cycles.

## Efficiency and Robustness to Sensor Placement

Practical deployment of wearable AI requires more than just high accuracy; models must be efficient and robust to real-world variability. The study found significant differences in computational overhead. Oxford SSL and ElderNet were the most efficient, with high throughput ($17,500\text{ samples/s}$) and low latency ($< 0.1\text{ ms}$). Conversely, NormWear, despite its high parameter count, was approximately 180 times slower, making it less suitable for real-time applications on resource-constrained devices.

Robustness to sensor placement is another vital factor, as users may wear devices on different parts of the body (e.g., wrist vs. waist). ElderNet demonstrated superior adaptability across seven different sensor positions in the RealWorld dataset, consistently outperforming supervised baselines. This suggests that the scale of FM pretraining helps the model learn invariant features that are less sensitive to the specific orientation or location of the sensor.

## Probing Internal Representations

To understand why certain models perform better, the researchers analyzed the similarity of internal representations using Concept-Based Alignment (CBA) and Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA). These metrics quantify how similar the features are between different layers of a model or between different models.

One unexpected finding was the high degree of similarity between layers in UniMTS, suggesting a lack of hierarchical feature differentiation. In contrast, Oxford SSL and ElderNet exhibited more distinct block-diagonal patterns, where early layers $L_i$ and late layers $L_j$ represent different "concepts." This hierarchical structure is typically associated with better generalization in deep learning models. Interestingly, when using frozen representations, UniMTS performed exceptionally well as a feature extractor, provided it was paired with a non-linear readout head.

## Concept Discovery and Activity Profiling

Beyond pre-defined labels, the study explored whether FMs could discover intrinsic "concepts" in movement data using the NLMCD framework. By applying dimensionality reduction and density-based clustering to the models' hidden states, the researchers identified 34 distinct concepts in the RealWorld dataset.

The analysis showed that FMs form very clear, non-overlapping concepts for dynamic activities like jumping or running. However, for more ambiguous activities like walking up or down stairs, or for sedentary behaviors like sitting and standing, the discovered concepts showed significant overlap. This suggests that while FMs are excellent at capturing the physics of high-energy motion, they struggle to differentiate between low-energy activities that produce similar acceleration profiles. This finding opens the door for using FMs to create data-driven activity profiles that might be more nuanced than simple human-annotated labels.

## Deployment Recommendations for Practitioners

Based on the extensive benchmarking, the study provides evidence-based guidance for selecting models in real-world scenarios:

1. **For General HAR with Ample Data:** Strong supervised baselines like Inception1D or S4 remain highly effective and are often more computationally efficient to train and deploy than large FMs.
2. **For Critical Health Monitoring (Falls/Stress):** ResNet-based FMs like Oxford SSL and ElderNet are recommended due to their superior detection capabilities and high inference speed.
3. **For Low-Data or Rapid Prototyping:** UniMTS is a powerful frozen feature extractor. Practitioners can use its frozen representations with a simple non-linear head to achieve high performance without the need for extensive finetuning.
4. **For Diverse Sensor Placements:** ElderNet is the most robust choice when the exact location of the wearable device cannot be guaranteed.
5. **For Personalization/Demographics:** NormWear, despite its computational cost, provides the most reliable features for inferring user-specific characteristics like age and weight when used in a frozen evaluation mode.

In conclusion, while foundation models for movement data are not a universal replacement for supervised learning, they offer specific advantages in robustness and transferability. By understanding the computational trade-offs and representational strengths of each model, practitioners can better leverage these architectures for advanced health monitoring applications.
