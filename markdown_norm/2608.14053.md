The study of numerical semigroups and the Frobenius Coin Problem represents a foundational area within additive number theory. At its core, the classical Frobenius Problem asks a deceptively simple question: given a set of positive integers with no common divisor greater than 1, what is the largest integer that cannot be formed by adding those numbers together in non-negative quantities? This value is known as the Frobenius number.

The paper "p-NUMERICAL SEMIGROUP OF THE SEQUENCE OF CONSECUTIVE ODD INTEGERS" by Komatsu, Hyun, and Song expands upon this classical problem by investigating the $p$-Frobenius number. This generalization moves beyond simple representability to consider the number of ways an integer can be formed. Specifically, the authors focus on two families of consecutive odd integers, verifying theoretical conjectures that describe their $p$-Frobenius numbers.

## The Foundations of Numerical Semigroups

A numerical semigroup is a subset of the non-negative integers that includes zero, is closed under addition, and has a finite complement in the set of natural numbers. Formally, for a set of positive integers $A = \{a_1, a_2, \dots, a_k\}$ where the greatest common divisor $\text{gcd}(A)=1$, the semigroup generated by $A$ consists of all integers $n$ that can be expressed as:
$$
n = x_1 a_1 + x_2 a_2 + \dots + x_k a_k
$$
where each $x_i$ is a non-negative integer. The classical Frobenius number, denoted $g(A)$, is the largest integer that does not belong to this semigroup.

The $p$-Frobenius problem is a higher-order extension of this concept. Instead of checking if a representation exists, we define $r_A(n)$ as the number of distinct ways to represent the integer $n$ as a sum of elements in $A$. We then define the set $S_p(A) = \{n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} : r_A(n) > p\}$. This set contains all integers that have more than $p$ representations. The $p$-Frobenius number $g_p(A) = \max \{ \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \setminus S_p(A) \}$ is the largest integer that has $p$ or fewer representations. When $p=0$, this reduces to the classical Frobenius number, as $S_0(A)$ is the semigroup itself, and $g_0(A)$ is the largest integer not in the semigroup.

## Consecutive Odd Integers and Existing Research Gaps

While formulas for the Frobenius number are well-known for certain sets—such as the simple case of two integers $\{a, b\}$ where $g(a, b) = ab - a - b$—the problem becomes significantly more difficult as the number of generators increases or as we move to the $p$-Frobenius variant. Previous research established explicit formulas for $g_p(A)$ when $A$ consists of consecutive integers (e.g., $\{a, a+1, \dots, a+k\}$). However, the transition from consecutive integers to consecutive odd integers introduces structural complexities that prevent direct application of those earlier results.

The authors of this paper specifically target two families of consecutive odd integers:
1.  $A^-_a = \{2a+1, 2a+3, \dots, 4a+1\}$
2.  $A^+_a = \{2a+1, 2a+3, \dots, 4a+3\}$

For both sets, the smallest element is $b = 2a+1$. These families were the subject of Conjectures 7.1 and 7.5 in a 2025 study by Komatsu and Pandey. This paper provides the formal proofs necessary to verify those conjectures, filling a specific gap in the literature regarding arithmetic progressions in numerical semigroups.

## The Methodology of the p-Apéry Set

The primary tool used to solve the $p$-Frobenius problem is the $p$-Apéry set. For a chosen element $b$ in the set $A$ (usually the smallest), the $p$-Apéry set is defined as:
$Ap_p(A; b) = \{m^{(p)}_0, m^{(p)}_1, \dots, m^{(p)}_{b-1}\}$
where $m^{(p)}_i$ is the smallest integer in $S_p(A)$ that is congruent to $i \pmod b$.

The significance of this set lies in a established lemma which states that the $p$-Frobenius number can be calculated directly from the maximum value in this set:
$$
g_p(A) = \max Ap_p(A; b) - b
$$
By identifying the smallest integer in each residue class modulo $b$ that has more than $p$ representations, the authors can pinpoint the exact threshold where integers transition from having "few" representations to "many." This reduces a complex search across all integers to a targeted analysis of $b$ residue classes.

## Combinatorial Tools: Restricted Partitions

To determine the number of representations $r_A(n)$, the authors employ the theory of partitions. A partition of $n$ is a way of writing $n$ as a sum of positive integers. In this context, the authors use the bounded restricted partition function $p^{(\le L)}_{\le r}(n)$, which counts the number of ways to partition $n$ into at most $r$ parts, where each part is no larger than $L$.

The generating function for this partition function is the Gaussian binomial coefficient:
$$
\left[\begin{smallmatrix} L+r \\ r \end{smallmatrix}\right]_q
$$
The coefficients of this polynomial are symmetric and unimodal. Symmetry implies that the number of ways to partition a small integer is reflected in the number of ways to partition a corresponding large integer within the bounded range. Unimodality means that the number of partitions increases up to a central point and then decreases.

By leveraging these properties, the authors establish inequalities that bound the number of representations $r_A(n)$. For example, they introduce the sequence $M_k = p_{\le 4}(k)$, representing the number of partitions of $k$ into at most 4 parts. This sequence is used to define the boundaries for $p$. In the theorems, $p$ is constrained such that $M_m \le p < M_{m+1}$ (for $A^-_a$) or $M_j \le p < M_{j+1}$ (for $A^+_a$), allowing the authors to relate the value of $p$ to a specific index $m$ or $j$.

## Analysis of the First Family: $A^-_a$

The first major result of the paper concerns the set $A^-_a = \{2a+1, 2a+3, \dots, 4a+1\}$ for $a \ge 4$. The authors consider $p$ in the range $1 \le p \le \lfloor(a+1)/2\rfloor$.

The proof strategy involves identifying a critical value $N_0 = 4b+2m$. Through a series of lemmas, the authors demonstrate that for this specific value, the number of representations is $r_A(4b+2m) = M_m$. Since $M_m \le p$ by definition, $N_0$ does not belong to $S_p(A)$. However, they then show that for every residue class modulo $b$, the element $5b+2m$ (or an equivalent value) does belong to $S_p(A)$.

By analyzing the structure of $r_A(n)$ in terms of partition functions, they establish that $m^{(p)}_{2m} = 5b+2m$ is the maximum element in the $p$-Apéry set. Applying the relationship between the Apéry set and the Frobenius number leads to Theorem 1.2:
$g_p(A^-_a) = 8a + 2m + 4$

This formula explicitly links the $p$-Frobenius number to the growth of the partition function $M_k$. It shows that as $p$ increases, the $p$-Frobenius number moves in discrete steps determined by the number of ways to partition integers into four parts.

## Analysis of the Second Family: $A^+_a$

The second family, $A^+_a = \{2a+1, 2a+3, \dots, 4a+3\}$, is slightly larger and exhibits different behavior depending on the value of $p$. The authors split the analysis into two cases: $p=1$ and $p \ge 2$.

For the case $p=1$, the authors find that the 1-Frobenius number is:
$g_1(A^+_a) = 6a + 5$
This is established by showing that while most elements in the sequence have multiple representations, $3b+2$ is a critical point that only has a single representation (i.e., $r_A(3b+2) = 1$), which prevents it from being in $S_1(A^+_a)$.

For the case $p \ge 2$ and $a \ge 3$, the authors use a similar strategy to the first family, involving the index $j$ where $M_j \le p < M_{j+1}$. They identify that $4b+2j \notin S_p(A^+_a)$ while $5b+2j \in S_p(A^+_a)$. The maximum element of the $p$-Apéry set is determined to be $5b+2j$, resulting in Theorem 1.3:
$g_p(A^+_a) = 8a + 2j + 4$

The fact that the formula for $p \ge 2$ mirrors the structure of the $A^-_a$ formula suggests a shared underlying combinatorial architecture for these odd-integer sequences, even though the sets themselves differ by one element.

## Significance of the Results

The verification of these conjectures is significant for several reasons. First, it provides exact mathematical descriptions for a problem that is often only solvable through exhaustive computation for small sets. By providing formulas that depend only on $a$ and the partition index $m$ or $j$, the authors allow for the immediate calculation of $g_p(A)$ for any $a$ and $p$ within the specified bounds.

Second, the work demonstrates the power of combining numerical semigroup theory with partition theory. The use of Gaussian polynomials to prove the properties of $r_A(n)$ provides a rigorous framework that can likely be adapted to other arithmetic progressions, such as sequences of integers with a common difference $d > 2$.

Finally, the paper clarifies the limits of current knowledge. The constraints on $a$ (such as $a \ge 4$ or $a \ge 3$) and $p$ (up to $\lfloor(a+1)/2\rfloor$) define the "stable" region where these formulas hold. Outside these bounds, the representation counts $r_A(n)$ may behave less predictably, suggesting areas for future investigation. The transition from $p=1$ to $p \ge 2$ in the $A^+_a$ family also highlights that the $p$-Frobenius number does not always follow a uniform rule as $p$ scales, necessitating careful case-by-case analysis.

In summary, this research successfully resolves open conjectures regarding the $p$-numerical semigroups of consecutive odd integers. By utilizing the $p$-Apéry set and the properties of restricted partition functions, the authors have provided a complete characterization of the $p$-Frobenius numbers for these specific sequences, advancing the broader understanding of additive representation problems.
