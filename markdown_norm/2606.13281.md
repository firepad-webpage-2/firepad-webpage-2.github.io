In the field of causal discovery, researchers aim to identify the underlying mechanisms that govern how variables interact in complex systems. A common framework for this is the Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG), where arrows represent direct causal influences. However, most real-world datasets are "causally insufficient," meaning that some relevant variables are unobserved or latent. When these latent variables are ignored, the resulting causal models often yield incorrect conclusions due to confounding—where an unobserved factor influences both a cause and its effect.

This paper, authored by Marco Borriero, Monia Lupparelli, Giovanni M. Marchetti, and Veronica Vinciotti, addresses this fundamental challenge by extending the principle of causal invariance to models with latent variables. Causal invariance suggests that while the marginal distributions of variables might change across different environments (e.g., different experimental conditions or demographic groups), the causal mechanism itself—how a target variable $Y$ reacts to its direct parents $X_{PA}$—remains stable. 

## Causal Discovery and the Latent Variable Problem

Traditional methods like Invariant Causal Prediction (ICP) rely on the assumption that all relevant predictors are observed. Under this assumption, if we find a set of variables $S$ such that the conditional distribution $p(Y|X_S)$ is invariant across environments, then $S$ is a candidate for the set of true causal parents. 

The presence of latent variables $H$ disrupts this logic. If a latent variable is a mediator (appearing in the path between an observed cause and the target) or a confounder (influencing both), the standard invariance tests may fail or lead to the selection of the wrong variables. The authors shift the focus from simple DAGs to Acyclic Directed Mixed Graphs (ADMGs). Unlike DAGs, ADMGs allow for bi-directed edges ($\leftrightarrow$), which represent the presence of unobserved common causes (confounders) between observed variables.

The core contribution of this work is the formalization of how the "induced graph"—the graphical structure that remains after unobserved variables are marginalized out—can still be used to identify causal parents through invariance testing.

## Causal Invariance in Induced Graphical Models

A Structural Causal Model (SCM) defines a variable as a function of its parents and an independent noise term. For a target variable $Y$, the model is typically written as:

$$
Y = f(X_{PA}, \delta_Y)
$$

where $X_{PA}$ are the causal parents and $\delta_Y$ is the error term. Causal invariance requires that both the function $f$ and the distribution of $\delta_Y$ remain the same across all environments $e \in \mathcal{E}$. Additionally, the noise $\delta_Y$ must be independent of the parents $X_{PA}$.

When latent variables $H_{PA}$ are present, the authors distinguish between two primary scenarios:
1.  **Non-confounding latents:** Latent variables that act as mediators ($X \to H \to Y$) or sources ($H \to Y$) but do not create confounding paths between observed predictors and the target.
2.  **Hidden confounders:** Latent variables that influence both a predictor and the target ($X \leftarrow H \to Y$).

In the first scenario, the authors prove in **Proposition 1** that the marginal model for $Y$ given its "augmented" observed parents $X_{gPA}$ (the original parents plus the observed parents of latent mediators) remains a valid SCM. This means the standard conditional distribution $p(Y|X_{gPA})$ is invariant, allowing researchers to recover the causal structure using modified versions of existing tools.

## Testing for Invariance without Confounders

For the widely used case of multivariate Gaussian target variables, the authors provide a specific testable condition in **Theorem 1**. They consider a linear SCM:

$$
Y = B_{PA}^T X + \epsilon_Y
$$

where $\epsilon_Y \sim N(0, \Sigma)$. They show that a subset of variables $X_S$ represents the augmented parent set if and only if the covariance of the residuals is invariant across all environments:

$$
E[(Y - B^T X)(Y - B^T X)^T] = \Sigma
$$

To put this into practice, the authors propose a two-step statistical procedure:
1.  **Box's M Test:** Use this test to check if the residual covariance matrices are equal across all environments for a given subset $X_S$.
2.  **BIC Selection:** Among the subsets that pass the invariance test, select the one that minimizes the average Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which balances model fit with complexity.

In numerical simulations, this approach correctly identified the parent sets with 92% accuracy, demonstrating that even with unobserved mediators, the causal signal can be reliably recovered if no confounding is present.

## Addressing Hidden Confounders: Weak Invariance

The most significant hurdle in causal inference is the "hidden confounder." If a latent variable $H$ influences both $X$ and $Y$, then the noise term in the marginal model becomes correlated with the predictors. This violates the standard requirement for causal invariance ($\delta_Y \perp \perp X_{PA}$).

To solve this, the authors introduce the concept of **Weak Invariance** (Definition 2). A model is weakly invariant if the functional relationship $f$ and the distribution of the noise $\epsilon_Y$ (which now includes the latent effect) are stable across environments, even if $\epsilon_Y$ is not independent of the predictors $X$. 

The authors demonstrate through **Proposition 3** that induced ADMGs are superior to other representations like Ancestral Graphs for this task. While Ancestral Graphs are often used to represent marginal independences, they may fail to preserve the weak invariance property when confounding occurs. ADMGs, by contrast, maintain the structural integrity of the causal mechanism.

To identify parents in this confounded setting, the authors utilize **Instrumental Variables (IVs)**. An IV is a variable $Z$ that influences the predictors $X$ but has no direct effect on the target $Y$ except through $X$.

For the Gaussian case with confounders, **Theorem 2** provides the necessary and sufficient conditions. The identification relies on adjusted expected likelihood score equations. These equations account for the fact that $E[X \epsilon_Y^T] \neq 0$ due to the latent $H$. By finding a set $X_S$ that satisfies these adjusted scores and maintains a stable residual covariance, researchers can identify the true causal parents even in the presence of unobserved confounders.

## Empirical Application: Flow Cytometry Data

The authors apply their methodology to a well-known dataset in biology: the flow cytometry data from Sachs et al. (2005). This dataset measures the expression of various proteins in human T-cells under different chemical interventions (environments). 

The goal was to identify the causal parents of the bivariate target $\{P38, Erk\}$. In one experiment, they treated the protein Jnk as an unobserved variable. Biologically, Jnk is known to confound the relationship between the protein PKC and the target P38.

Using the procedure derived from **Theorem 1** (which assumes no confounding), the method failed to identify the correct invariant set when Jnk was hidden. However, by applying **Theorem 2** and using other proteins like PIP2 and Raf as instrumental variables, the procedure successfully identified the remaining invariant parent set. This result highlights the necessity of the proposed framework: if researchers use tools designed for causally sufficient systems on data with hidden confounders, they will likely reach incorrect conclusions.

## Significance of the Work

This paper advances the theoretical foundations of causal discovery by bridging the gap between invariance-based methods and the reality of latent variables. The unique contributions include:

*   **Formalization of Induced SCMs:** Proving that marginal models of ADMGs retain a structural causal interpretation under specific latent configurations.
*   **The Weak Invariance Principle:** Providing a framework to test for stable causal mechanisms even when the errors are correlated with the predictors—a common occurrence in observational science.
*   **Practical Statistical Tests:** Developing Theorem-based conditions for Gaussian data that can be implemented using standard statistical tests like Box's M and BIC.

The implications for research are broad. In fields like genomics, economics, and social science, where many variables are unmeasurable, these methods provide a pathway to discover robust causal relationships. By shifting the focus to "weakly invariant" sets and utilizing instrumental variables within the ADMG framework, scientists can move closer to identifying the true drivers of complex systems without needing to observe every single component. 

Future research sparked by this work may focus on extending these principles to non-Gaussian or non-linear systems, as well as developing more efficient algorithms to search through the large space of possible parent subsets in high-dimensional data.
