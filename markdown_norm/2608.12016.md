## Term Structure Dynamics in the Hull–White Model

The term structure of interest rates, often visualized as the yield curve, is a primary indicator of economic health and expectations. Its shape—defined by its level, slope, and curvature—contains information about future inflation, monetary policy, and potential recessions. For instance, an inverted yield curve, where short-term rates exceed long-term rates, is frequently viewed as a precursor to economic contraction. While simple shapes like "normal" (upward sloping) or "inverse" (downward sloping) are common, market data often reveal more complex patterns with multiple peaks and troughs.

In the research paper "Term Structure Shapes in the Hull–White Model with Svensson–Parameterized Initial Yield Curves," Felix Sachse investigates the mathematical capacity of the Hull–White model to replicate these diverse shapes. While the Hull–White model is inherently flexible, allowing it to fit any initial curve, the study focuses on what happens when the model is initialized with the widely used Svensson family of curves. By applying tools from Tchebycheff systems and envelope theory, the author provides a rigorous classification of the shapes the model can produce, how these shapes transition over time, and what patterns emerge as the time horizon approaches infinity.

## The Hull–White Framework and Initial Parametrization

The study centers on the Hull–White model, which describes the evolution of the short-term interest rate $r(t)$ under a risk-neutral measure. The short rate follows an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process defined by the stochastic differential equation:

$$dr(t) = (b(t) - \lambda r(t)) dt + \sigma dW(t)$$

In this equation, $\lambda$ represents the speed of mean reversion, $\sigma$ is the volatility, and $W(t)$ is a standard Brownian motion. The parameter $b(t)$ is a time-dependent drift term specifically chosen to ensure the model perfectly matches the initial forward curve observed in the market. 

To analyze the model's behavior systematically, the paper restricts the initial forward curve $f_0$ to the Svensson family. The Svensson parametrization is a popular choice among central banks because of its ability to capture complex curvatures. The forward curve in this family is defined as:

$$f_0^S(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 e^{-x/\tau_1} + \frac{\beta_2}{\tau_1} x e^{-x/\tau_1} + \frac{\beta_3}{\tau_2} x e^{-x/\tau_2}$$

Here, $x$ is the maturity, and the parameters $\beta_i$ and $\tau_j$ control the level, slope, and curvature components. By varying these parameters, the Svensson family can represent several subfamilies:
- **Nelson–Siegel:** A simplification where $\beta_3 = 0$.
- **Bliss:** A variation where $\beta_2 = 0$ and $\tau_1 \neq \tau_2$.
- **Monotone:** A two-factor version where $\beta_2 = \beta_3 = 0$.
- **Flat:** A one-factor version where all $\beta_i$ except $\beta_0$ are zero.

The research aims to determine how the interaction between these initial parameters and the Hull–White model's parameters ($\lambda, \sigma$) dictates the number of local extrema (peaks and valleys) in the resulting forward and yield curves over time.

## Mathematical Methodology: Tchebycheff Systems and Envelopes

Determining the shape of a yield curve requires identifying the number and types of its local extrema. Mathematically, this involves counting the zeros of the derivative of the forward curve $f(x, t, r)$ or yield curve $y(x, t, r)$ with respect to maturity $x$. 

The author employs the theory of **Extended Complete Tchebycheff (ECT) systems**. These systems provide a framework for bounding the number of zeros a linear combination of functions can have. By showing that the derivatives of the interest rate curves are polynomials of an ECT system, the author establishes strict upper bounds on the number of possible local extrema for each initial curve family. For example, if a curve's derivative belongs to a system with $n$ basis functions, it can have at most $n-1$ zeros.

Beyond counting extrema, the paper uses **envelope theory** to segment the state space—the set of all possible time $t$ and interest rate $r$ combinations—into regions corresponding to specific shapes. For a fixed maturity $x$, the "zero set" $\zeta_x$ is the set of points $(t, r)$ where the derivative of the curve is zero. The "envelope" $\eta$ is formed by the points where the first and second derivatives with respect to $x$ vanish simultaneously:

$$\partial_x f(x, t, r) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \partial_{xx} f(x, t, r) = 0$$

These envelopes act as boundaries in the $(t, r)$ plane. Crossing an envelope boundary signifies a change in the number of extrema, essentially "creating" or "destroying" a hump or a dip in the curve.

## Classification of Attainable Shapes

The paper provides a comprehensive list of attainable shapes, which are classified by the sign sequence of their derivatives (e.g., a "humped" curve increases then decreases). A central result is the derivation of upper bounds for local extrema across different initial curve families:

| Initial Curve Family | Max Local Extrema (Hull–White) |
| :--- | :--- |
| One-factor (Flat) | 1 |
| Two-factor (Monotone) | 2 |
| Nelson–Siegel | 3 |
| Bliss | 4 |
| Svensson | 5 |

Interestingly, the Hull–White model can generate at most two additional local extrema compared to the initial curves themselves. This suggests that while the model adds complexity, it does not allow for infinite oscillations.

### Flat and Monotone Initializations
For flat initial curves, the model only produces three shapes: normal, inverse, and humped. The state space segmentation is straightforward, with the shape determined by the level of the short rate $r$ relative to thresholds defined by $\beta_0, \lambda,$ and $\sigma$.

![figure-1-state-space-segmentation-for-the-forward-curve-with-flat-initial-yield](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12016v1/img-1.jpeg)

For monotone initial curves, the behavior becomes more sensitive to the relationship between the mean reversion speed $\lambda$ and the decay parameter $\tau$ of the initial curve. Under certain conditions, such as $2\lambda < 1/\tau$, the model can produce a "dip-humped" (dh) shape, which is a curve with two local extrema.

### Nelson–Siegel Initializations
The analysis of the Nelson–Siegel family is significantly more complex. The author identifies seven distinct regimes for the forward curve and nine for the yield curve. Using a "winding number" formula, the research characterizes the segments of the parameter space. One notable finding is that while the "hump-dip-hump" (hdh) shape is attainable, the "dip-hump-dip" (dhd) shape is mathematically impossible when starting from a Nelson–Siegel initialization.

## Long-Term Asymptotic Behavior

A critical question addressed is whether the complexity of yield curve shapes persists as time progresses. The paper analyzes the limits of the tracking functions and envelopes as $t \to \infty$. 

The results show a significant simplification in the long term. For the yield curve, the probability of observing complex shapes vanishes asymptotically. Regardless of the initial curve parameterization, as $t \to \infty$, the yield curve almost surely adopts one of only three shapes: normal, inverse, or humped. 

The author calculates the asymptotic relative frequencies for these shapes under the risk-neutral measure. Specifically, as $t \to \infty$:
- The probability of an inverse shape converges to $0.5$.
- The probability of a normal shape converges to $\Phi\left(-\frac{3\sigma}{\sqrt{8\lambda^3}}\right)$.
- The probability of a humped shape converges to $0.5 - \Phi\left(-\frac{3\sigma}{\sqrt{8\lambda^3}}\right)$.

This means that in the long run, the Hull–White model behaves identically to the simpler Vasicek model in terms of the variety of shapes it produces. The richness provided by the Svensson initialization is a transient phenomenon that dominates the short-to-medium term but fades over long horizons.

## Significance of the Findings

This research provides a rigorous mapping between model parameters and the resulting geometry of the interest rate term structure. Its significance lies in several areas:

**1. Model Expressiveness:** It clarifies the limitations of the Hull–White model. While it is a "perfect fit" model for initialization, its internal dynamics constrain the evolution of the curve's shape. Knowing that a "dip-hump-dip" shape cannot be generated from a Nelson–Siegel starting point allows practitioners to understand when the model might fail to capture specific market configurations.

**2. Practical Segmentation:** The state-space segmentations (like those shown in the figures) serve as "maps" for traders and risk managers. By observing the current short rate and time, one can determine the qualitative shape of the forward curve expected by the model without performing full re-calibrations.

**3. Theoretical Consistency:** By showing that the Hull–White model converges to Vasicek-like shape frequencies asymptotically, the paper provides a bridge between simple and complex affine models. It highlights that the "memory" of the initial market state (captured by the time-dependent drift $b(t)$) eventually dissipates, leaving the long-term dynamics to be governed by the constant mean-reversion and volatility parameters.

By combining the descriptive power of the Svensson family with the stochastic dynamics of the Hull–White model, this work offers a detailed technical look at how interest rate expectations evolve, providing a foundation for more accurate economic forecasting and derivative pricing.
