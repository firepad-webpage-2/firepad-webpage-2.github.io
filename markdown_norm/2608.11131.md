In multivariate statistics, the lack of a natural ordering for data points in higher dimensions poses a significant challenge for researchers. Unlike univariate data, where one can easily define medians and quantiles, points in $\mathbb{R}^d$ do not follow a canonical linear order. Statistical depth functions were introduced to solve this by providing a center-outward ordering. Among these, simplicial depth is a classic measure that quantifies the centrality of a point $x$ by looking at how many random simplices formed by the data contain $x$.

While simplicial depth is a powerful tool for nonparametric inference, its practical application is complicated by the varying rates at which its sample estimates converge to the true value. Depending on the symmetry of the underlying distribution, this rate can change, making it difficult to construct accurate confidence intervals or perform hypothesis tests without prior knowledge. This work introduces an adaptive resampling framework designed to estimate these convergence rates directly from data, enabling robust inference even when the degree of distribution symmetry is unknown.

## Simplicial Depth and the U-Statistic Framework

The simplicial depth of a point $x$ with respect to a distribution $P$ is defined as the probability that $x$ is contained within a simplex whose vertices are $d+1$ independent observations from $P$. Formally, if $X_1, \dots, X_{d+1}$ are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables from $P$, the simplicial depth is $\text{SD}(x; P) = P(x \in \Delta(X_1, \dots, X_{d+1}))$.

When we have a finite sample $X_n = \{X_1, \dots, X_n\}$, the sample simplicial depth $\text{SD}_n(x; X_n)$ is computed as:

$$\text{SD}_n(x; X_n) = \binom{n}{d+1}^{-1} \sum_{1 \leq i_1 < \dots < i_{d+1} \leq n} \mathbb{I}(x \in \Delta(X_{i_1}, \dots, X_{i_{d+1}}))$$

where $\Delta(\cdot)$ denotes the convex hull (simplex) of its arguments. This estimator is a U-statistic, a class of statistics used extensively in nonparametric theory. The asymptotic behavior of a U-statistic is determined by its "degeneracy." For most points $x$, the statistic is non-degenerate, meaning it converges to the true depth at a rate of $\sqrt{n}$ and follows a normal distribution. However, if $x$ is a center of symmetry for the distribution, the statistic becomes degenerate. In this case, the convergence rate accelerates to $n$, and the limiting distribution becomes a weighted sum of chi-squared random variables.

The core problem for practitioners is that the center of symmetry is rarely known beforehand. If one assumes a $\sqrt{n}$ rate for a point that is actually a center of symmetry, the resulting confidence intervals will be unnecessarily wide and theoretically incorrect. Conversely, assuming an $n$ rate for a non-symmetric point leads to intervals that fail to cover the true value.

## Characterizing Degeneracy in the Plane

A major theoretical contribution of this research is the complete characterization of degeneracy for simplicial depth in two dimensions ($d=2$). While it was previously established that symmetry implies degeneracy, the authors proved the converse: in the plane, the sample simplicial depth U-statistic at a point $x$ is degenerate if and only if $x$ is the center of angular symmetry for the distribution $P$ (provided the depth is non-zero).

Angular symmetry is a specific type of symmetry where, for any line passing through $x$, the probability mass is distributed such that the probability of a random point falling in any half-plane defined by the line is exactly $1/2$. This finding is significant because it links the abstract statistical property of U-statistic degeneracy to a concrete geometric property of the data distribution. This characterization serves as the foundation for the adaptive methods developed in the paper, as it confirms that in bivariate settings, the convergence rate parameter $\gamma$ is either $1/2$ (non-degenerate) or $1$ (degenerate).

## The Adaptive Subsampling Procedure

To handle the unknown rate of convergence, the authors propose a two-step adaptive subsampling procedure. The goal is to estimate the rate parameter $\gamma$ and the distribution of the centered and scaled statistic $R_n = n^\gamma (\text{SD}_n(x; X_n) - \text{SD}(x; P))$ simultaneously.

The estimation of $\gamma$ involves taking "nested" subsamples. First, two different subsample sizes are chosen, $m_\ell$ (lower) and $m_u$ (upper), both smaller than the full sample size $n$. By comparing how the variability of the simplicial depth changes between these two subsample sizes, one can back-calculate the rate of convergence. Specifically, the method computes the median absolute differences $T_u$ and $T_\ell$ between the subsampled depths and the full sample depth:

$$T_u = \text{median}\{| \text{SD}_{m_u,s}(x) - \text{SD}_n(x; X_n) | : s = 1, \dots, S\}$$
$$T_\ell = \text{median}\{| \text{SD}_{m_\ell,s}(x) - \text{SD}_n(x; X_n) | : s = 1, \dots, S\}$$

The raw estimator for the rate is then derived from the log-ratio of these differences:

$$\hat{\gamma} = \frac{\log T_\ell - \log T_u}{\log m_u - \log m_\ell}$$

### Refined Estimators and Bias Correction
Since theory dictates that $\gamma$ should be either $1/2$ or $1$ in the bivariate case, the authors introduce trimmed and rounded versions of the estimator. The trimmed estimator $\hat{\gamma}_t = \max(\min(\hat{\gamma}, 1), 1/2)$ ensures the result stays within the theoretically plausible range, while the rounded estimator $\hat{\gamma}_r$ forces the estimate to exactly $1/2$ or $1$ based on a threshold.

Furthermore, they address a common issue in subsampling: the "upward bias" that occurs when the subsample size $m$ is a significant fraction of $n$. Because the full sample statistic $\text{SD}_n(x; X_n)$ is itself an estimate, the variance of the difference $\text{SD}_m - \text{SD}_n$ is slightly smaller than the true variance of $\text{SD}_m$. To correct this, they introduce bias correction factors like $\hat{\gamma}_{bc,1}$ based on the term $n/(n-m)$, which significantly improves performance in non-degenerate cases and for smaller sample sizes.

## Estimating the Distribution for Inference

Once the rate $\hat{\gamma}$ is estimated, the procedure approximates the entire distribution of the simplicial depth. This is achieved by drawing many subsamples of size $m$ and observing the empirical distribution of the scaled differences. This data-driven distribution can then be used for two primary tasks:

1.  **Confidence Intervals:** Constructing intervals for $\text{SD}(x; P)$ that adapt to whether $x$ is at the center of the distribution or in the tails.
2.  **Classification Certainty:** In supervised learning, the "maximum depth classifier" assigns a point $x$ to the class where it has the highest depth. However, a simple class label doesn't convey how sure the classifier is. The authors use a Hall-type estimator $\hat{p}_H$ to estimate the probability $p(x) = P(\text{SD}_n(x;X_n) > \text{SD}_n(x;Y_n))$. This provides a measure of certainty that accounts for the specific geometry and convergence rates of the classes.

## Experimental Results and Practical Use

Extensive simulations using Gaussian and Cauchy distributions validated the consistency of the rate estimators. As the sample size $n$ increases, the estimated $\hat{\gamma}$ reliably converges to the true value of either $1/2$ or $1$. The study found that the $\hat{\gamma}_{bc,1}$ bias correction was particularly effective, especially when combined with the rounded estimator $\hat{\gamma}_r$.

In classification tasks, the proposed method proved superior to standard "percentile-like" bootstrap approaches. Standard methods often struggle near the boundaries of the data (the convex hull), where simplicial depth frequently hits zero in subsamples. By contrast, the adaptive procedure using the Hall-type estimator provides much more decisive and stable classification probabilities, even near the centers of symmetric distributions where the depth distribution is highly skewed.

The authors also provided an R package, "SD," which includes efficient C++ implementations of these algorithms. This is critical because simplicial depth is computationally intensive to calculate in dimensions $d > 2$; the efficient bivariate implementation makes intensive resampling techniques like this procedure feasible for real-world datasets.

## Conclusion

This research bridges a long-standing gap in multivariate nonparametric statistics. By providing both a theoretical characterization of simplicial depth degeneracy and a practical, adaptive resampling method, the work allows for robust statistical inference without requiring unrealistic assumptions about distribution symmetry. The two-step subsampling approach effectively "learns" the correct asymptotic rate from the data, leading to more accurate confidence intervals and better-calibrated classification models. While the theoretical characterization of degeneracy is currently limited to the plane, the adaptive resampling framework itself is general and paves the way for advanced multivariate analysis in higher dimensions.
