## The Challenge of Long-Term Helioseismology

Helioseismology is the study of the Sun's interior through the observation of acoustic waves that propagate through its gaseous body. By measuring the precise radial velocities of the solar surface, researchers can detect oscillation modes that reveal the internal structure, rotation, and dynamics of our star. The Birmingham Solar Oscillations Network (BiSON) has been a leader in this field since 1992, maintaining a global network of ground-based observatories to provide continuous monitoring of "Sun-as-a-star" oscillations.

![bison-ng-system-architecture-at-iza-a](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12033v1/izana_block_diagram.png)
*{fig:bison-ng-system-architecture-at-iza-a}: A block diagram illustrating the BiSON:NG system architecture as deployed at the Observatorio del Teide, Izaña. It shows the integration of the solar telescope, instrumentation, and the microservice-based control network.*

Because the Sun's oscillation modes have very long periods and low amplitudes, scientific success depends on the continuity of data. Any gaps in observation—caused by sunset at a single site or local weather—can introduce artifacts into the power spectrum of the data, making it difficult to resolve closely spaced frequency modes. To minimize these gaps, BiSON operates a geographically distributed network. However, maintaining this infrastructure over decades presents significant challenges. Legacy systems, often designed for manual operation, become increasingly expensive to staff and difficult to maintain as hardware components and software libraries reach their end-of-life. The BiSON:NG (Next Generation) platform was developed to address these issues by automating legacy sites and modernizing their technical foundations.

## The BiSON:NG Platform: A Modular Architecture

The transition from the original BiSON systems to BiSON:NG represents a fundamental shift in how observatory control software is designed. Historically, astronomical instruments often relied on monolithic software—single, large programs that managed everything from telescope movement to data logging. While functional, these systems are fragile; a bug in one component can crash the entire system, and upgrading one part often requires rewriting the whole program.

BiSON:NG adopts a microservice-based architecture. In this model, the system is broken down into small, independent services that perform specific tasks, such as:
- **Thermal Control:** Managing the temperature of the spectrophotometers.
- **Attitude Control:** Managing the movement and guiding of the telescope mount.
- **Data Acquisition:** Recording scientific measurements from the sensors.
- **Monitoring and Alerting:** Checking system health and notifying operators of issues.

These services communicate using the MQTT (Message Queuing Telemetry Transport) protocol. MQTT is a lightweight messaging standard designed for the Internet of Things (IoT). It uses a "publish-subscribe" model where services send data to a central broker under specific topics (e.g., `bison/telemetry/temperature`). Other services, such as a logging database or a live dashboard, subscribe to these topics to receive the information. This decoupling ensures that if one service fails, the others continue to operate, and new features can be added without disrupting existing operations.

## Hardware Modernization and Optical Feeds

A key physical component of the BiSON:NG upgrade is the use of fibre-fed instrumentation. In older solar observatories, the large spectrophotometer instruments were often mounted directly to the telescope or placed in a way that required complex optical paths involving multiple mirrors. By using optical fibres, the light collected by a small telescope can be transmitted to a stable, climate-controlled room. This simplifies the mechanical requirements for the telescope mount and isolates the sensitive instruments from environmental vibrations and temperature swings.

For the optical feed, the researchers utilized modified Sky-Watcher AZ-GTi Alt-Az mounts. While these are inexpensive, commercially available units, they were customized for scientific use. Because the standard mounts lack absolute encoders (they do not "know" exactly where they are pointing after a power cycle), the team added custom 3D-printed brackets and mechanical limit switches to allow the system to perform an automated "homing" routine every morning.

![modified-sky-watcher-az-gti-mount](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12033v1/skywatcher1.png)
*{fig:modified-sky-watcher-az-gti-mount}: The modified Sky-Watcher AZ-GTi mount used for the automated optical feed. Custom limit switches were added to enable automated homing and position recovery.*

Tracking the Sun with high precision is achieved through a guide camera system. A Sony IMX477 sensor paired with an $80 \text{ mm}$ focal length objective lens acts as the "eyes" of the system. An onboard computer processes these images to keep the solar disc centered on the $1 \text{ mm}$ core diameter optical fibre.

## Automation at Observatorio del Teide, Izaña

The Izaña site in Tenerife represents the most complete implementation of the BiSON:NG automation suite. Previously, this site required an on-site operator to open the roof and start observations every day. To automate this, the researchers installed a "treasure chest" robotic enclosure (a DML DOME Sky Treasure Chest v3).

![the-treasure-chest-enclosure-at-iza-a](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12033v1/dml_dome_stcv3.png)
*{fig:the-treasure-chest-enclosure-at-iza-a}: Schematic of the DML DOME Sky Treasure Chest v3 robotic enclosure. The design allows for a small footprint while providing full protection for the automated solar telescope.*

This enclosure is a self-contained system with its own weather sensors for rain, wind, and humidity. It is controlled via a REST API, allowing the BiSON:NG software to schedule opening and closing times. The system includes critical fail-safes:
- **Autonomous Closure:** If the rain sensor detects moisture or wind speeds exceed a safety threshold, the enclosure closes immediately without waiting for a command from the main computer.
- **Watchdog Timer:** If the main control computer loses its network connection to the enclosure for more than a few minutes, the enclosure assumes a system failure has occurred and closes itself to protect the telescope.
- **Redundant Monitoring:** System status is logged to InfluxDB, a time-series database, and visualized using Grafana, allowing researchers in the UK to monitor the site in real-time.

## Integration at Mount Wilson Observatory

Automating the Mount Wilson site in California presented a different challenge: integrating modern technology into a century-old historical landmark. The 60-foot Solar Tower at Mount Wilson is a massive vertical telescope structure. Unlike the new installation at Izaña, the team had to place their automated optical feed inside the existing tower infrastructure.

![the-60-foot-solar-tower-at-mount-wilson](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12033v1/tower1.jpg)
*{fig:the-60-foot-solar-tower-at-mount-wilson}: The historic 60-foot Solar Tower at Mount Wilson Observatory. The BiSON:NG optical feed was integrated into this structure to modernize its helioseismology capabilities.*

One significant hurdle at Mount Wilson is the legacy dome enclosure. The motor drive for the tower's dome is a simple DC motor without any electronic feedback to indicate the dome's position. While a complete replacement of the dome drive was not feasible, the team developed a pragmatic automation strategy using optical sensors placed at key locations to detect when the dome is fully open or closed.

Despite these environmental constraints, the guiding performance at Mount Wilson has been highly successful. During commissioning tests, the system achieved the following tracking results:
- **Azimuth Axis:** A root mean square (rms) pixel error of only $1.63 \text{ px}$, which translates to an angular error of $6.6 \text{ arcseconds}$.
- **Altitude Axis:** An rms error of $0.74 \text{ px}$, or roughly $3 \text{ arcseconds}$.

To put this in perspective, these errors correspond to a physical drift of just $5.8 \mu\text{m}$ on the face of the $1 \text{ mm}$ fibre. This level of precision ensures that the light collection remains efficient and stable throughout the day. The maximum daily variation in solar position within the guide camera's view was approximately $\pm 5 \text{ pixels} (21 \text{ arcseconds})$, well within the tolerances required for helioseismic data.

## Software Stack and Data Management

The sustainability of the BiSON network relies as much on software as it does on hardware. By using standardized tools, the researchers ensure that the system can be maintained by future generations of scientists.
- **Programming:** The high-level control logic is written in Python, running on $\text{x86\_64}$ hardware. For lower-level tasks requiring real-time response, such as motor control on microcontrollers, $\text{C++}$ is used.
- **Database:** All telemetry (voltages, temperatures, tracking errors) and scientific data are stored in InfluxDB. This allows for rapid querying of historical performance.
- **Security and Archiving:** The sites use a segregated network approach. An internal network connects the instruments and mounts, while a secure "Edge Host" handles the connection to the outside world. Every night, the data is automatically replicated via SSH to a central archive at the University of Birmingham and then to a High-Performance Computing (HPC) facility for permanent storage.

## Impact on Helioseismology Research

The modernization of these two sites is a significant step toward the long-term viability of the BiSON network. By reducing the need for on-site personnel and moving toward a modular, easily maintainable architecture, the project mitigates the risks of both human resource fluctuations and equipment obsolescence.

Furthermore, the successful deployment of BiSON:NG at both a new, custom enclosure (Izaña) and a historic solar tower (Mount Wilson) demonstrates the flexibility of the platform. This modular approach lowers the barrier to entry for adding new sites to the network. Smaller, cheaper, and fully automated stations can now be deployed to fill remaining gaps in global coverage, potentially providing the most continuous and highest-quality data set of solar oscillations ever recorded. This work ensures that BiSON will continue to contribute to our understanding of the Sun's internal life for decades to come.
