## Bridging the Gap Between Simulation and Reality

The design of wireless communication receivers is undergoing a transition from traditional, manually engineered signal processing pipelines to data-driven architectures powered by deep learning. In standard 5G and prospective 6G systems, Orthogonal Frequency-Division Multiplexing (OFDM) is the dominant waveform. Traditional receivers for these systems typically use modular components for tasks like channel estimation, equalization, and bit demapping. Recently, "learned receivers" have emerged, replacing some or all of these components with neural networks.

A significant hurdle in this field is the reliance on synthetic data. Most research utilizes mathematical channel models to train these networks because simulations provide an infinite supply of data with perfect "ground truth" labels. However, real-world Over-the-Air (OTA) transmissions involve complex hardware impairments—such as phase noise, I/Q imbalance, and non-linearities—that are difficult to capture accurately in simulations. This creates a "reality gap," where models trained in simulation may underperform when deployed on actual hardware.

This research investigates whether incorporating real-world OTA measurements into the training process can bridge this gap. Specifically, it examines a fundamental asymmetry: does the benefit of real-world data depend on what the neural network is trying to learn? The study compares a modular neural channel estimator against an end-to-end neural receiver to determine how different supervision targets—explicit channel labels versus decoded bits—respond to the inherent noise present in real-world measurements.

## Architecture Design: Modular vs. End-to-End Approaches

To isolate the impact of training data, the researchers implemented two distinct neural receiver architectures with similar model capacities (number of parameters). Both models process received OFDM signals but target different stages of the receiver chain.

The first model, the **Axial ConvMixer Estimator (ACM-E)**, is a modular neural channel estimator. Its specific task is to take the received signal $Y$ and the known pilot symbols as input and output an explicit estimate of the communication channel $\hat{H}$. This estimate is then passed to a conventional Zero-Forcing (ZF) equalizer and a soft demapper to produce bit log-likelihood ratios (LLRs). The ACM-E architecture uses "axial factorization," a technique borrowed from image processing that processes the time and frequency dimensions of the OFDM grid separately to reduce computational complexity while maintaining effectiveness. The network is trained using a Mean Square Error (MSE) loss function:

$$L_{ACM-E} = \frac{1}{N_s N_{eff}} \sum_{i,j} |\hat{H}_{ij} - H_{gt,ij}|^2$$

Here, $H_{gt,ij}$ represents the supervision target, which is the "ground truth" channel for a specific subcarrier $i$ and symbol $j$.

The second model, **ConvMixer DeepRx (CM DeepRx)**, is an end-to-end receiver. Instead of producing an intermediate channel estimate, it maps the raw received signal $Y$ directly to bit LLRs. This approach jointly optimizes channel estimation, equalization, and demapping within a single neural network. It is trained using Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss:

$$L_{DeepRx} = - \frac{1}{N_b} \sum_k [b_k \log \hat{p}_k + (1-b_k) \log(1-\hat{p}_k)]$$

where $b_k$ is the actual transmitted bit and $\hat{p}_k$ is the probability predicted by the network. This architecture allows the network to learn to compensate for hardware impairments without needing an explicit model of those impairments.

## The Challenge of Over-the-Air Supervision

In a simulated environment, training is straightforward because the "genie" channel $H_{ij}$ and the transmitted bits $b_k$ are known perfectly. However, in an OTA setting, the true channel $H_{ij}$ is never known; it can only be estimated from the noisy received signal.

To create training labels for the modular ACM-E model, the researchers performed a measurement campaign. They captured transmissions, synchronized them, and performed error correction. For data blocks that passed a Cyclic Redundancy Check (CRC), the transmitted bits were assumed to be correctly recovered. These bits were then re-modulated to reconstruct the exact transmitted symbols $x_{ij}$. Using these symbols and the received signal $y_{ij}$, a dense "ground truth" channel label was generated via a Least-Squares (LS) approach:

$$H_{gt,ij} = y_{ij}/x_{ij}$$

While this method provides labels for every point in the OFDM grid, the resulting label $H_{gt,ij}$ is inherently noisy because $y_{ij}$ contains thermal noise and hardware interference. Crucially, because the neural network's input $Y$ and the supervision target $H_{gt}$ are both derived from the same physical measurement $y_{ij}$, the noise in the input and the noise in the label are mathematically related.

In contrast, the end-to-end CM DeepRx model is supervised by the transmitted bits $b_k$. Once a CRC passes, these bits are effectively error-free. Thus, even if the input signal $Y$ is noisy, the supervision target $b_k$ remains "clean." This difference in the nature of the training targets is the core of the phenomenon explored in this paper.

## Understanding Input-Correlated Supervision Noise

The most significant theoretical contribution of this work is the identification of **input-correlated supervision noise**. In traditional machine learning, "label noise" is often assumed to be independent of the input. If label noise is independent, a neural network can eventually learn to average it out given enough data. However, if the noise in the label is correlated with the noise in the input, a persistent bias is introduced.

For the neural channel estimator (ACM-E), the received signal is $y_{ij} = H_{ij}x_{ij} + n_{ij}$, where $H_{ij}$ is the channel, $x_{ij}$ is the transmitted symbol, and $n_{ij}$ is the noise. The LS estimate used for supervision is:

$$\hat{H}_{LS,ij} = y_{ij}/x_{ij} = H_{ij} + n_{ij}/x_{ij} = H_{ij} + \tilde{n}_{ij}$$

The neural network takes $Y$ (which includes $y_{ij}$) as input and tries to minimize the MSE relative to $\hat{H}_{LS,ij}$. Because $\tilde{n}_{ij}$ is a component of the input signal itself, the network cannot distinguish between the true channel $H_{ij}$ and the noise $\tilde{n}_{ij}$. As a result, the network does not converge to the true channel $E[H_{ij} | Y]$ but rather to the noisy target $E[H_{gt,ij} | Y]$.

This correlation creates a "noise floor" for the learning process. No matter how many OTA samples are collected, the network will be biased toward the specific noise realizations present in the training data that match the input features. This provides a theoretical explanation for why modular estimators might struggle when trained exclusively on raw real-world data.

## Experimental Findings: The Value of Real-World Data

The researchers tested their hypothesis by training models with varying proportions of OTA data, denoted by $p_m$. A value of $p_m = 0\%$ means the model was trained only on synthetic data, while $p_m = 100\%$ means it was trained exclusively on measurements.

For the **end-to-end receiver (CM DeepRx)**, the results were clear: performance improved monotonically as $p_m$ increased. By using 100% real-world data, the model achieved its best performance, effectively learning to navigate the specific impairments of the radio hardware. Because its supervision target (the bits) was not correlated with the input noise, it did not suffer from the bias affecting the channel estimator.

For the **neural channel estimator (ACM-E)**, the behavior was non-monotonic. At lower fractions (e.g., $p_m = 40\%$), the model showed improvements over the simulation-only baseline, likely because it learned to handle some hardware effects. However, at $p_m = 100\%$, the performance degraded significantly, eventually becoming worse than the model trained only on simulations. This degradation was most pronounced at high signal-to-noise ratios (SNR), where the input-correlated noise in the labels became the dominant source of error for the network.

## Mitigating Noise: The Role of Label Denoising

To confirm that input-correlated noise was indeed the culprit, the researchers conducted a "denoising" experiment. They took the raw OTA channel labels ($y_{ij}/x_{ij}$) and applied a classical time-frequency smoothing filter—the same type used in standard non-learned receivers—to reduce the noise before training the ACM-E model.

The results validated the hypothesis. When trained with these "denoised" labels, the ACM-E model no longer suffered from the performance collapse at $p_m = 100\%$. In fact, it performed better across all OTA fractions compared to the raw-label version. However, even with denoised labels, the modular ACM-E could not match the performance of the end-to-end CM DeepRx.

This comparison highlights two key advantages of end-to-end learning in wireless systems:
1. **Target Purity:** Using bits for supervision avoids the bias of input-correlated noise that plagues intermediate channel labels.
2. **Joint Compensation:** The end-to-end model can learn to ignore certain channel estimation errors if they don't impact the final bit recovery, whereas a modular estimator is forced to minimize MSE on a potentially flawed target.

## Implications for Next-Generation Wireless Systems

This research provides practical guidance for engineers and researchers developing AI-based physical layer components for 5G-Advanced and 6G systems.

First, it suggests that **end-to-end architectures** are naturally more robust when training on real-world measurements. Developers using such architectures should strive to collect as much diverse OTA data as possible, as the bit-level supervision effectively filters out measurement noise.

Second, for those who prefer **modular architectures** (perhaps for better interpretability or to reuse existing components), the study warns against using raw LS estimates from OTA data as training targets. Instead, these targets must be carefully refined or denoised. Alternatively, a "mixed" training strategy—using a large amount of synthetic data supplemented by a smaller, carefully curated portion of OTA data—may be more effective than going "all-in" on raw measurements.

Finally, the work underscores the importance of the "supervision quality" in wireless AI. While big data is a cornerstone of deep learning, in the physical layer, the *way* that data is labeled is just as critical as the volume of data itself. Understanding the mathematical relationship between inputs and targets is essential for building reliable, high-performance receivers that can operate in the complex reality of the air interface.
