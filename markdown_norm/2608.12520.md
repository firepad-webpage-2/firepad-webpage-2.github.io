## Hierarchical Control in Autonomous Driving

Autonomous vehicles (AVs) operate in complex environments governed by a structured hierarchy of priorities, often referred to as a "rulebook." These priorities dictate that certain objectives must not be compromised for the sake of others; for instance, avoiding a collision (safety) is strictly more important than maintaining a specific speed limit (legal), which in turn is more important than smooth braking (comfort). In the mathematical framework of Model Predictive Control (MPC), this is typically modeled as a lexicographic multi-objective optimization problem. 

In such a system, the controller attempts to find a trajectory $z \in \mathcal{Z} \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ that minimizes a sequence of violation functions $V_1, V_2, \dots, V_L$ and a performance cost $J(z)$. The hierarchy implies that we first minimize $V_1$. Once the minimum possible value for $V_1$ is found, we minimize $V_2$ within the set of solutions that achieve that minimum $V_1$, and so on. This process ensures that high-priority rules are never sacrificed for low-priority ones.

However, solving this "lexicographic cascade"—which requires solving $L+1$ separate optimization problems every few milliseconds—is computationally expensive for real-time systems. Engineers often bypass this by using a "weighted-sum scalarization," where all objectives are combined into a single function $J_w(z) = J(z) + \sum_{i=1}^L w_i V_i(z)$ where $w_i > 0$. While much faster, this approach relies on heuristic weight tuning (e.g., $w_1 \gg w_2 \gg \dots \gg w_L$). There is no standard guarantee that a specific set of weights will yield the same result as the true lexicographic hierarchy. This paper addresses the gap by geometrically characterizing the set of weights that ensure equivalence and providing a method to certify them.

## Geometric Characterization of Equivalence

The core theoretical contribution of the work is the definition of the "equivalence region." To understand this, we consider the "upper image" $P$ of the achievement map. If we map every possible trajectory $z$ to a point in $\mathbb{R}^{L+1}$ representing its violations and performance cost, the set $P$ is the collection of all points "above and to the right" of these mapped values.

$$
P = \{ (v_1, \dots, v_L, j) \in \mathbb{R}^{L+1} \mid \exists z \in \mathcal{Z}, V_i(z) \leq v_i, J(z) \leq j \}
$$

Under the assumption of a convex optimization problem, $P$ is a convex set. The lexicographic optimum $p^* = (V_1(z^*_{lex}), \dots, V_L(z^*_{lex}), J(z^*_{lex}))$ sits on the boundary of this set. The paper proves that a weight vector $w$ produces the same solution as the lexicographic cascade if and only if the vector $(w, 1)$ is a supporting normal to the set $P$ at the point $p^*$. 

This defines the normalized equivalence region $\Omega(p^*)$ as the slice of the outward normal cone at $p^*$ where the coefficient for the performance cost $J$ is normalized to 1. 

$$
\Omega(p^*) = \{ w \in \mathbb{R}^L_{>0} \mid (w, 1) \in N_P(p^*) \} \cap \mathcal{B}
$$

This geometric view shifts weight tuning from a heuristic search to a problem of finding weights within a specific cone. If the weights are chosen from $\Omega(p^*)$, the single-solve optimization is mathematically guaranteed to produce the lexicographically optimal trajectory $z^*_{lex}$.

## The $\ell_1$ vs. $\ell_2$ Penalty Dichotomy

The paper highlights a critical distinction between two common types of penalty functions used in MPC: $\ell_1$ (hinge) penalties and $\ell_2$ (squared-hinge) penalties.

In the $p = 1$ regime ($\ell_1$ penalties), the violation function is defined as:
$$
V_i(z) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{R}_i} \max(0, g_{i,j}(z))
$$
In the $p = 2$ regime ($\ell_2$ penalties), it is defined as:
$$
V_i(z) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{R}_i} (\max(0, g_{i,j}(z)))^2
$$

The research proves that these two choices have fundamentally different properties regarding lexicographic equivalence. For $\ell_1$ penalties, if the underlying problem is polyhedral (e.g., linear constraints and piecewise linear costs), the upper image $P$ is also polyhedral. This means that $p^*$ is a vertex of $P$, and there exists a "fat" cone of weights that achieve exact equivalence. This allows for a robust choice of finite weights.

In contrast, for $\ell_2$ penalties, the violation functions are continuously differentiable, and their gradients vanish as the violation $g_{i,j}(z)$ approaches zero. The authors demonstrate that if a constraint is binding (active) at the lexicographic optimum with a non-zero Lagrange multiplier, then no finite weight $w_i$ can achieve exact equivalence. Instead, the violation only vanishes asymptotically. Specifically, as the weight $w_i$ increases, the raw violation $g_{i,j}(z)$ decays as $O(1/w_i)$ and the functional violation $V_i(z)$ decays as $O(1/w_i^2)$. This "foreclosure" of exactness suggests that $\ell_2$ penalties are fundamentally unsuitable for tasks requiring strict adherence to a priority hierarchy via a single weighted solve.

## Constructing $\ell_1$ Weight Certificates

For systems using $\ell_1$ penalties, the paper provides a constructive method (Algorithm 1) to find an interior weight within the equivalence region. The process involves examining the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) stationarity conditions at the lexicographic solution.

By introducing auxiliary variables $\beta_{i,j} = w_i \alpha_{i,j}$ (where $\alpha_{i,j} \in [0, 1]$ represents the subgradient of the hinge function), the stationarity condition can be expressed as:
$$
\nabla J(z) + \sum_i \sum_j \beta_{i,j} \nabla g_{i,j}(z) + \sum_k \lambda_k \nabla h_k(z) = 0
$$
where $h_k(z)$ represents the dynamics and other equality constraints. The set of valid weights $w$ is the projection of the feasible set of this linear system onto the $w$ coordinates. 

The algorithm identifies the Chebyshev center of this polyhedral region—essentially the point furthest from the boundaries of the equivalence region. This provides a "certified" weight with a safety margin, ensuring that small perturbations in the problem parameters are less likely to push the weight out of the equivalence cone.

## Persistence and the Guarded Architecture

While a weight might be certified for a specific time step (a "pointwise" certificate), the receding-horizon nature of MPC means the optimization problem changes at every tick as the vehicle moves and the environment evolves. A key question is how long a certified weight remains valid.

The authors evaluated this "persistence" using the nuPlan simulator. They found that the intersection of equivalence regions across multiple ticks often becomes empty very quickly. The median lifetime of a certificate was found to be only one control interval (0.1s at the native rate). This lack of persistence is primarily driven by "active-set turnover"—the frequent changing of which constraints are active as the vehicle navigates.

To address this, the researchers proposed a "guarded weighted-solve architecture." In this setup, the controller typically runs a single weighted-sum solve using calibrated weights. However, it simultaneously monitors the compliance pattern. If the pattern of rule violations does not match what is expected for the lexicographic optimum (indicating the certificate has expired), the system falls back to the full (but slower) lexicographic cascade for that tick.

## Empirical Results and Performance

The methodology was tested on a 25-rule rulebook within the nuPlan simulator, comparing several configurations: the legacy heuristic, $\ell_1$ vs. $\ell_2$ penalties, the full cascade reference, and the new calibrated/guarded approaches.

![rss-compliance-rates](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12520v1/fig_rss_per_scenario.png)
*{fig:rss-compliance-rates}: Per-cell Responsibility-Sensitive Safety (RSS) compliance rates across different driving scenarios for various planner configurations ($C_0$ to $C_4$). The full cascade ($C_4$) generally shows the highest compliance.*

Key findings from the experiments include:
1.  **Calibration Utility:** Using calibrated weights $w^\dagger$ (C5) significantly improved event-level fidelity to the lexicographic ideal compared to heuristic weights. For example, precision for legal-tier rule violations improved from 0.232 to 0.463.
2.  **Guarded Performance:** The guarded architecture (C6) was highly effective. It achieved lexicographic Pareto-dominance over the full cascade in 9 out of 11 scenarios. This counter-intuitive result (a surrogate beating its reference) is attributed to the receding-horizon effect, where slightly different choices in the surrogate solve can lead to more favorable states in future ticks.
3.  **Computational Savings:** The calibrated single-solve was approximately 9.5 times faster than the full cascade. Even with the fallback mechanism active on roughly 35% of ticks, the guarded architecture remained about 1.8 times faster than always running the cascade.
4.  **$\ell_2$ Regressions:** As predicted by theory, the $\ell_2$ penalty configuration resulted in significantly higher violations of comfort rules compared to $\ell_1$, as the solver struggled to zero out higher-priority violations exactly.

## Conclusion

This work provides a rigorous mathematical bridge between high-speed weighted-sum MPC and high-fidelity lexicographic optimization. By proving that $\ell_2$ penalties foreclosure exact equivalence and providing a constructive geometry-based calibration for $\ell_1$ penalties, the paper offers a practical toolkit for AV engineers. While weight certificates are transient in dynamic environments, the combination of principled calibration and a guarded fallback architecture allows for efficient, real-time control that maintains the semantic integrity of complex safety rulebooks.
