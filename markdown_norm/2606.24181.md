## Fare Evasion and Enforcement Strategies

Fare evasion is a major operational challenge for public transport providers, resulting in substantial revenue losses and complicating the management of transit networks. To combat this, operators employ ticket inspectors who verify passenger credentials. A fundamental decision in these enforcement strategies is whether to deploy inspectors in uniform (visible) or in plainclothes (covert). 

The theoretical tradeoff between these two modes is centered on the concepts of deterrence and detection. Visible enforcement, rooted in economic theories of crime, suggests that the presence of uniformed inspectors increases the "perceived probability of detection." This is intended to deter potential offenders from evading fares in the first place. However, visibility also introduces an information advantage for the passenger. Savvy riders can adapt their behavior—for example, by alighting at the next stop or avoiding specific vehicle sections—upon spotting a uniformed official. Covert enforcement eliminates this adaptation opportunity, potentially leading to higher detection rates of those who have already chosen to evade.

Despite the operational importance of this choice, empirical evidence on which strategy is more effective has been limited, largely due to the difficulty of establishing a clear causal link. Simple comparisons between detection rates can be misleading because operators might strategically deploy uniformed inspectors in areas where evasion is already known to be high. This study addresses this challenge by applying causal machine learning and policy learning to a massive, multi-source dataset from PostAuto, the largest regional bus operator in Switzerland. By controlling for a wide array of contextual factors, the research quantifies how much more (or less) efficient one strategy is compared to the other.

## Data Foundations: Merging Institutional and Operational Data

The strength of this analysis lies in its granular and multifaceted dataset. The primary source consists of 21,727 inspection records from PostAuto. These records track individual inspection events, capturing the start and end times, the specific bus line, the number of passengers checked, and the number of detected fare evaders. The researchers defined the primary outcome of interest as "inspection efficiency," calculated as the ratio of detected fare evaders to total inspection time.

To ensure that the comparison between uniformed and plainclothes inspections is "apples-to-apples," the researchers merged this operational data with several external datasets. This allowed them to account for the socio-economic and geographic characteristics of the areas where the bus lines operate. These factors include:
*   **Demographics**: Population size, age distribution, the share of foreign residents, and social assistance rates at the municipality level.
*   **Public Transport Infrastructure**: Accessibility scores, boarding volumes recorded by PostAuto, and the prevalence of travelcards like the Swiss "General Abonnement" (GA) and the "Half Fare Travelcard."
*   **Economic Context**: Cantonal-level unemployment rates.
*   **Line Geography**: Route topography and the degree of urbanization of the stops served.

By aggregating these data points, the researchers constructed a high-dimensional feature set that describes the environment of each inspection. This allows the causal models to "adjust" for the fact that uniformed inspectors might be deployed in different environments than their plainclothes counterparts, satisfying the critical assumption that once these factors are accounted for, the choice of inspection mode is effectively random for the purpose of the model.

## Methodological Framework: Causal Forests and Policy Learning

Traditional statistical methods often struggle with two aspects of complex policy questions: identifying the true causal effect in observational data and understanding how that effect changes across different contexts. This study moves beyond simple regression by using a "Causal Forest" approach.

### The Causal Model
The central goal is to estimate the Average Treatment Effect (ATE), denoted as $\tau$, which represents the difference in the expected outcome if every inspection were uniformed versus if every inspection were plainclothes. Let $D$ be the treatment variable, where $D = 1$ indicates a uniformed inspection and $D = 0$ indicates a plainclothes inspection. The ATE is defined as:

$$\tau = E[Y(1) - Y(0)]$$

In this framework, $Y(1)$ and $Y(0)$ are the potential outcomes—the efficiency we would observe under each strategy. Because we never see both outcomes for the same inspection, the model uses the rich set of covariates $X$ to estimate these values. The causal forest effectively grows thousands of decision trees to find subgroups within the data where the treatment and control groups are as similar as possible.

### Addressing Treatment Heterogeneity
A key contribution of the paper is the analysis of the Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE). Instead of assuming that visible inspections have a single, uniform effect across all of Switzerland, the CATE allows the effect to vary based on the characteristics of the bus line:

$$\tau(x) = E[Y(1) - Y(0) | X = x]$$

This captures whether uniformed inspections are, for instance, more effective in densely populated urban centers than in rural alpine villages. The researchers used the variable importance scores from the causal forest to identify which factors—such as population size or travelcard ownership—were the most influential drivers of these differences.

## Quantifying the Detection Gap: Results of the Causal Forest

The analysis reveals a significant disparity in detection efficiency. When looking at the sample as a whole, plainclothes inspections were consistently more efficient at detecting fare evaders than uniformed inspections.

![distribution-of-treatment-effects](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.24181v1/img-2.jpeg)
*{fig:distribution-of-treatment-effects}: Distribution of estimated individual (conditional) treatment effects. The majority of the distribution lies below zero, indicating that uniformed inspections generally result in lower detection efficiency compared to plainclothes inspections across most contexts.*

The estimated ATE for uniformed inspections relative to plainclothes was approximately $\tau \approx -0.173$ incidents per hour. Given that the average detection rate in the sample was 0.67 incidents per hour, this represents a 26% decrease in detection efficiency when inspectors are visible. This finding strongly supports the "evasion avoidance" hypothesis: when passengers see a uniform, those without a ticket are often able to avoid being checked.

Furthermore, the study examined whether this gap was driven by specific circumstances. Using the Sorted Group Average Treatment Effects (GATES), the researchers divided the sample into five groups based on how strongly the model predicted the treatment would affect them. Interestingly, the effect was negative across all five groups. Even in the context where uniformed inspections performed best, they were still less efficient than plainclothes ones at detecting offenders. The magnitude of this "visibility penalty" was most pronounced on bus lines serving areas with high passenger volumes and specific demographic profiles, suggesting that in busy environments, the opportunities for avoiding visible inspectors are even greater.

## Designing an Optimal Strategy: Insights from Policy Trees

After establishing the causal effects, the final stage of the study was to translate these results into practical advice for transport operators. This was achieved through "policy learning," specifically by training a policy tree. A policy tree is a shallow decision-making tool designed to maximize a specific goal—in this case, inspection efficiency—by assigning an optimal strategy ($\pi$) based on local characteristics.

The objective is to find a mapping $\pi: X \to \{0, 1\}$ that maximizes the expected welfare:

$$E[Y(\pi(X))]$$

The resulting tree (shown in {fig:optimal-policy-tree}) provides a simple, three-way classification for bus lines.

![optimal-policy-tree](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.24181v1/img-3.jpeg)
*{fig:optimal-policy-tree}: Optimal policy tree for maximizing detection efficiency. The tree recommends plainclothes inspections for the vast majority of scenarios, identifying only one specific subgroup where uniformed inspections might be optimal.*

The model suggests the following rules:
1.  **High Foreign Resident Share**: For bus lines where the share of foreign residents in the served municipalities is above 21.9%, plainclothes inspections are recommended regardless of other factors.
2.  **Low Foreign Resident Share / Low Population**: In areas with a lower share of foreign residents and a total population below approximately 6,676, plainclothes inspections are also recommended.
3.  **Low Foreign Resident Share / High Population**: The only group where uniformed inspections are recommended is characterized by a below-median share of foreign residents combined with an above-median population size.

This targeted approach recommended plainclothes inspections for roughly 83% of the observed scenarios. This prescriptive model allows PostAuto to move away from a "one-size-fits-all" deployment and toward a data-driven allocation of their staff.

## Implications for Public Transport Policy

The results of this research provide a clear empirical signal: if the goal is to maximize the detection of fare evaders, plainclothes inspections are superior in almost all contexts. The 26% drop in efficiency associated with uniforms highlights a significant limitation of visible enforcement. 

However, the authors carefully note that "efficiency" in this study is defined solely by detection numbers. There is a broader welfare perspective to consider. Uniformed inspectors serve multiple roles beyond ticket checking: they provide a sense of security for passengers, offer orientations and assistance, and may have a long-term deterrent effect that this short-term detection study cannot fully capture. If a uniformed presence convinces 100 people to buy a ticket who otherwise wouldn't have, the fact that they detect fewer evaders per hour might actually be a sign of a successful deterrence strategy.

For transport operators, this means the choice of inspector visibility should depend on their primary objective. If the priority is to catch and fine existing offenders to recoup immediate revenue and reinforce the rules, plainclothes teams should be the backbone of the operation. If the priority is the public's perception of safety and long-term prevention, a visible presence remains necessary. The causal machine learning framework presented here provides the tools for operators to balance these competing goals with evidence-based precision, ensuring that limited resources are deployed where they can be most effective.
