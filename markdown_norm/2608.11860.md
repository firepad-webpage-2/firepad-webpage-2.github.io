## Understanding Inverse Design in Nanophotonics

Nanophotonics is a field of study focused on the behavior of light on the scale of nanometers, typically involving interaction with subwavelength structures. A prominent application in this domain is the development of metal–insulator–metal (MIM) absorbers. These devices consist of a metallic ground plane, a dielectric spacer, and a top layer of patterned metallic resonators. The specific geometry of these resonators—their shape, size, and arrangement—dictates how the device absorbs electromagnetic radiation at different wavelengths.

Designing these structures involves two primary processes. "Forward design" starts with a known geometry and calculates its optical response (the absorption spectrum) using electromagnetic solvers like finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) simulations. While accurate, this is computationally expensive and slow when exploring a vast design space. "Inverse design," the focus of this research, attempts the reverse: given a target optical spectrum, find the specific geometry that produces it.

The inverse problem is fundamentally difficult because it is non-unique; different geometries can yield nearly identical spectra. Furthermore, the relationship between geometry and spectrum is highly nonlinear. Small structural changes, such as the width of a gap or the sharpness of a corner, can lead to significant shifts in resonant peaks. Most current machine learning approaches use standard convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to generate these designs. However, standard CNNs operate on fixed, rectangular grids, which can struggle to accurately reconstruct the irregular and fine-scale features required for high-performance nanophotonic absorbers.

## The Role of Deformable Convolutional Networks

Standard convolutional layers use a rigid sampling grid to extract features from an image. For instance, a $3 \times 3$ kernel always samples from the nine pixels immediately surrounding a central point. While effective for general image tasks, this rigidity can be a bottleneck when the features of interest—like the thin arms of a resonator or narrow apertures—do not align perfectly with a Cartesian grid.

This research proposes using "deformable convolution" within the generator (the decoder) of the inverse design model. Deformable convolution introduces flexibility by adding learned offsets and modulation coefficients to the sampling grid. For each location $\mathbf{p}$ in the output feature map, the operation can be described as:

$$
y(\mathbf{p}) = \sum_{k=1}^K w_k \cdot m_k \cdot x(\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{p}_k + \Delta\mathbf{p}_k)
$$

where $\mathbf{p}_k$ is the standard offset (e.g., $(-1, -1)$), $\Delta\mathbf{p}_k$ is the learned displacement, $m_k$ is a scalar modulation weight, and $w_k$ represents the filter weights. This allows the network's receptive field to "warp" or adapt to the actual shape of the resonator elements it is trying to reconstruct.

The generator takes an 80-dimensional input spectrum $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^{80}$ and maps it to a $64 \times 64$ geometry mask $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = G_{\theta}(\mathbf{s})$. The architecture starts with a linear projection that reshapes the spectrum into a small spatial representation, which is then progressively upsampled through four layers. In the proposed model, these layers are all deformable convolutional layers, allowing for spatially adaptive sampling during the reconstruction of the geometry.

## A Two-Stage Training Strategy

Training a generative model to solve the inverse problem directly can be unstable. If a model is trained solely using an adversarial loss (like a GAN), it may fail to find the correct global mapping between the spectrum and the shape. Conversely, training only with a reconstruction loss (like Mean Squared Error) often results in "blurry" outputs where the network averages across multiple possible solutions to the non-unique problem.

To address this, the researchers implemented a two-stage training strategy:

1.  **Stage 1: Supervised Pre-training:** The generator is first trained using a pixel-wise reconstruction loss $\mathcal{L}_{rec} = \|\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}\|^2_2$, where $\mathbf{x}$ is the ground truth geometry. This stage establishes the fundamental relationship between spectral features and the overall structural topology of the resonator.
2.  **Stage 2: Adversarial Refinement:** The model then undergoes refinement using a Least-Squares Generative Adversarial Network (LSGAN) framework. A discriminator network is introduced to distinguish between "real" ground truth geometries and "fake" generated ones. To maintain stability, the total generator loss is defined as:

$$
\mathcal{L}_G = \mathcal{L}_{rec} + \alpha_e \mathcal{L}_{adv}
$$

Initially, the adversarial weight $\alpha_e = 0$ for a "warm-start" period, ensuring the pre-trained mapping isn't immediately disrupted. Later, it is increased to $\alpha_e = 0.1$, pushing the generator to produce sharper boundaries and more physically realistic features that the discriminator cannot easily flag as synthetic.

## Evaluating Reconstruction Performance

The researchers compared their deformable convolutional model against several other spatial operators, including standard convolution, "involution," and dynamic convolutions (Dynamic Conv and ODConv). The results consistently showed that the deformable approach outperformed the others in terms of both image fidelity and physical consistency.

In terms of image metrics, the proposed model achieved a Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) of $20.79 \pm 0.31 \text{ dB}$ and a Structural Similarity Index (SSIM) of $0.8501 \pm 0.0082$. When the generated images were binarized (to represent actual physical material vs. void), the model achieved a Dice coefficient of $0.9623$, indicating very high overlap with the intended designs.

The deformable convolutional approach (Proposed) shows superior ability in reconstructing sharp corners and thin features compared to standard or dynamic convolutions.*

One of the most critical tests was "spectral consistency"—verifying if the reconstructed geometry actually produces the desired spectrum. Since full FDTD simulations are slow, the researchers used an independently trained high-accuracy "forward surrogate" model to evaluate the generated shapes. The spectral RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error) was $0.0805$, and the dominant absorption peak wavelength error was roughly $0.42 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$ across the $4\text{--}12 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$ range. This confirms that the model preserves the essential optical behavior of the requested design.

## Insights from Learned Offsets

A unique contribution of this work is the analysis of the learned offsets $\Delta\mathbf{p}_k$ and modulation weights $m_k$. By visualizing these, the researchers could see how the model "attends" to different parts of the geometry at different stages of reconstruction.

![analysis-of-deformable-offsets](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.11860v1/deformable_offset_multiscale_example.png)
*{fig:analysis-of-deformable-offsets}: Visualization of learned deformable offsets and modulation maps at different spatial resolutions ($4 \times 4$, $16 \times 16$, and $64 \times 64$).*

The analysis revealed a scale-dependent behavior:
*   **Early Stages ($4 \times 4$ and $16 \times 16$):** The offsets showed high "enrichment" around boundaries, corners, and gaps. This indicates that in the coarse stages of reconstruction, the deformable mechanism is actively warping the sampling grid to define the global layout of the resonator.
*   **Late Stage ($64 \times 64$):** The offsets were much smaller and more distributed. At this final resolution, the network performs fine-grained pixel adjustments rather than large-scale structural warping.

This behavior confirms that deformable convolution provides a specific benefit for nanophotonic design by allowing the network to focus its parameters on the most physically sensitive regions of the structure—the interfaces and narrow apertures—where optical resonances are often concentrated.

## Significance of the Work

The research demonstrates that the choice of spatial operator in deep learning decoders is a significant factor in the success of inverse design for physical systems. While most work focuses on the high-level framework (e.g., GANs vs. Diffusion), this study shows that making the internal sampling mechanism adaptive—via deformable convolution—leads to measurable improvements in geometric and spectral accuracy.

The two-stage training protocol provides a stable pathway for optimizing these models, avoiding the pitfalls of mode collapse or blurry outputs common in single-stage approaches. For engineers, this provides a tool for "one-shot" design: a user can input a desired absorption curve and receive a high-fidelity resonator mask in milliseconds, potentially bypassing hours of iterative simulation.

While this work focused on $64 \times 64$ grayscale masks for MIM absorbers, the underlying principles are applicable to a wider range of metamaterials and photonic crystals. Future research may look into integrating topology-aware loss functions or extending these adaptive operators to 3D voxel-based reconstructions to handle even more complex nanophotonic devices.
