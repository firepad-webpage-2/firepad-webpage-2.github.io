The study of strongly correlated electron systems frequently relies on Dynamical Mean-Field Theory (DMFT), a framework that maps a complex lattice problem onto a simpler single-impurity model. While effective for systems in equilibrium, extending DMFT to non-equilibrium scenarios—such as those involving ultrafast laser pulses or steady-state quantum transport—introduces significant computational hurdles. The central challenge lies in the "impurity solver," the component responsible for calculating the local dynamics of the system.

![spectral-function-of-the-photodoped-hubbard-model-on-a-bethe-lattice-showing-comparisons-between-different-approximation-orders](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13146v1/dmft_tfla_broadening.png)

In non-equilibrium settings, traditional solvers often fail. Quantum Monte Carlo methods suffer from the "sign problem," which causes computational costs to grow exponentially with time. Hamiltonian-based methods, while accurate, are limited by finite system sizes. Consequently, researchers often turn to perturbative methods like the strong-coupling expansion. However, these expansions involve high-dimensional, time-ordered integrals that become prohibitively expensive at higher orders. This paper addresses this bottleneck by utilizing Quantics Tensor Trains (QTT) and Tensor Cross Interpolation (TCI) to accelerate these calculations, enabling more accurate and systematically improvable non-equilibrium simulations.

## The Challenge of Time-Ordered Integrals

In the strong-coupling expansion, physical quantities like the pseudo-particle self-energy $\Sigma$ or correlation functions $C$ are expressed as a sum of diagrammatic terms. In a non-equilibrium steady state (NESS), these terms involve integrals over the Keldysh contour. By parameterizing the contour using real times $t_j$ and Keldysh indices $\sigma_j \in \{+1, -1\}$, these expressions are converted into sums over $2^D$ configurations of real-time integrals.

The general structure of such a $D$-dimensional time-ordered integral is:

$$
I = \int_0^T dt_1 \int_0^{t_1} dt_2 \dots \int_0^{t_{D-1}} dt_D \mathcal{F}(t_1, \dots, t_D)
$$

As the diagrammatic order $D$ increases, the complexity of evaluating these integrals using standard grid-based methods scales as $O(N_t^D)$, where $N_t$ is the number of time steps. For third-order diagrams (TOA), $D$ can be as high as 6 or more, making direct integration impossible for dense grids. Prior work has often been forced to stop at lower-order approximations like the Non-Crossing Approximation (NCA) or the One-Crossing Approximation (OCA), which may lack the precision needed to capture complex physical phenomena like the Mott transition or dynamic screening.

## Tensor Trains and Quantics Representation

To bypass the exponential scaling of $D$, the authors employ Tensor Trains (TT), a method for representing a high-dimensional tensor as a product of low-rank matrices. For a tensor $\mathcal{A}$ with $D$ indices, the TT representation is:

$$
\mathcal{A}(i_1, i_2, \dots, i_D) \approx \sum_{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_{D-1}} A_1(i_1, \alpha_1) A_2(\alpha_1, i_2, \alpha_2) \dots A_D(\alpha_{D-1}, i_D)
$$

The efficiency of this representation depends on the bond dimension $\chi$, which determines the amount of information shared between the "cores" of the train. If $\chi$ remains small, the storage and manipulation costs scale linearly with $D$ rather than exponentially.

The researchers use Tensor Cross Interpolation (TCI) to construct these trains. TCI is a "black-box" algorithm that samples only a small fraction of the tensor's entries to build the approximation, with a complexity of $O(\chi^3 N n_{\text{rook}} D)$. To further enhance efficiency, they use the Quantics (QTT) representation. QTT factorizes each physical index into its binary bits, transforming a grid of size $N$ into $\log_2(N)$ dimensions. This allows functions to be represented on extremely dense grids with logarithmic scaling in $N$, provided the function has a low-rank structure in the bit representation.

## Four Strategies for Efficient Integration

The paper introduces and compares four different ways to parameterize and evaluate these integrals using TCI and QTT:

1.  **Time Decomposition (TD):** The integrand is sampled on a rectangular domain of times. The time-ordering constraint is treated as a mask. This method is straightforward but requires a larger bond dimension $\chi$ because the mask introduces sharp discontinuities that are difficult for tensor networks to represent efficiently.
2.  **Quantics Time Decomposition (QTD):** Similar to TD, but uses the bit-factorized QTT representation. This allows for very dense grids, but the bond dimension remains a limiting factor due to the time-ordering mask.
3.  **Time Difference Decomposition (TDD):** This approach leverages the time-translational invariance of steady states. The integrand is expressed in terms of time differences $\tau_d$. The resulting integrals are retarded convolutions, which can be evaluated efficiently in the frequency domain using Fast Fourier Transforms (FFT) with $O(N_t \log N_t)$ scaling.
4.  **Quantics Time Difference Decomposition (QTDD):** This is the most advanced strategy presented. It combines the time-difference parameterization with QTT. Crucially, the authors develop a direct real-time convolution algorithm within the QTT framework, avoiding the need for Fourier transforms entirely.

## A Direct Real-Time QTT Convolution Algorithm

A significant contribution of this work is the algorithm for performing retarded convolutions directly in the QTT format. A retarded convolution follows the form:

$$
(f * g)(t) = \int_0^t f(t - \tau) g(\tau) d\tau
$$

In a standard QTT representation, performing such an operation is non-trivial because the integration limit $t$ depends on the coordinate. The authors solve this by introducing an auxiliary "carry bit" that tracks the integration state across the binary hierarchy of the QTT. This carry bit is formulated as a Matrix Product Operator (MPO). While this doubles the bond dimension $\chi$, it allows the entire convolution to be performed in the compressed format, maintaining the logarithmic scaling of QTT while preserving high accuracy through trapezoidal integration rules.

## Benchmarks and Performance

To validate these methods, the researchers first tested them against a Gaussian scalar benchmark where the exact result is known. The results showed that Time Difference (TDD) methods reached machine precision ($10^{-13}$) with very small bond dimensions ($\chi \approx 16$). The QTDD methods saturated at a slightly higher error ($10^{-7}$ to $10^{-9}$), which is still more than sufficient for most physical applications and comparable to standard quadrature errors.

More importantly, the TCI-based methods exhibited the expected linear or logarithmic scaling with the number of grid points $N_t$. For example, at $N_t = 2^{10}$, the QTCI methods outperformed the non-quantics versions in terms of total runtime, even with the overhead of TCI sampling. The study also highlighted the importance of using proper quadrature weights (like trapezoidal or Gregory weights) rather than simple Riemann sums to ensure the hermiticity of the resulting physical operators.

## Application to DMFT and EDMFT

The solver was then integrated into self-consistent DMFT calculations for the Hubbard model. In equilibrium, the solver correctly reproduced the spectral function $A(\omega)$ and satisfied the fluctuation-dissipation relation. In the non-equilibrium case of a photodoped Mott insulator (at $U=8$), the authors were able to perform calculations at the third order (TOA).

The researchers then extended the solver to Extended Dynamical Mean-Field Theory (EDMFT) on a square lattice. EDMFT is more complex because it includes retarded density-density interactions $V$, requiring the evaluation of over 3,400 diagrams for a third-order calculation.

In the photodoped regime ($\gamma=2.2$), higher-order diagrams revealed a significant increase in carrier density, suggesting that lower-order approximations might overestimate the stability of the Mott insulating state under excitation. The bosonic response $-\text{Im } W_{\text{loc}} / \pi$ also showed sensitive dependence on the expansion order, emphasizing the need for the systematically improvable approach enabled by this QTT solver.

## Significance of the Work

This paper provides a robust framework for overcoming the computational limits of non-equilibrium strong-coupling expansions. By reducing the cost of high-dimensional integration from exponential to linear or logarithmic, it allows researchers to check the convergence of diagrammatic series that were previously inaccessible.

The primary significance lies in:
*   **Controllable Accuracy:** Users can systematically increase the expansion order to ensure their results are converged.
*   **Efficiency:** The direct QTT convolution algorithm enables calculations on grids as large as $2^{17}$ points, providing high frequency resolution without a corresponding increase in compute time.
*   **Versatility:** The solver is applicable to a wide range of models, including those with retarded interactions like EDMFT and potential GW+EDMFT applications.

Ultimately, this work advances the ability to simulate quantum systems far from equilibrium, providing the tools necessary to investigate complex phenomena like transient superconductivity, quantum transport in molecular junctions, and the ultra-fast control of correlated materials.
