## The Challenge of Three-Dimensional Galaxy Morphology

Understanding the intrinsic three-dimensional (3D) shape of a galaxy is fundamental to reconstructing its history. The geometry of a stellar system—whether it is a thin, rotating disk, a boxy bar, or a triaxial spheroid—encodes the signatures of its formation, including gas accretion, internal instabilities, and past mergers. However, a significant barrier exists: astronomers can only observe galaxies as two-dimensional projections on the sky.

In the observational realm, this projection effect makes it difficult to disentangle a galaxy's true shape from its orientation relative to Earth. While statistical methods can infer the general shapes of large populations, characterizing the radial 3D structure of an individual, multi-component galaxy remains a challenge. To bridge this gap, researchers turn to cosmological hydrodynamical simulations like IllustrisTNG and EAGLE. These simulations provide direct access to the 3D coordinates of billions of stellar particles, offering a laboratory to study morphology without projection bias.

Characterizing this simulated data, however, is not trivial. Galaxies are complex, often featuring central bulges, extended disks, elongated bars, and diffuse stellar halos. Traditional methods often oversimplify these structures, assuming they are perfect ellipsoids. This research introduces **Gal3D**, a new framework designed to move beyond these simplifications. By using superellipsoid modeling, Gal3D captures higher-order features like "boxiness" and "diskiness," providing a more nuanced map of galaxy structure across different cosmic environments and simulation models.

## Limitations of Traditional Shape Tensors

Historically, the most common way to measure the shape of a simulated galaxy or dark matter halo is the iterative inertia-tensor method. In this approach, the mass distribution is used to calculate a shape tensor, which is then diagonalized to find the principal axes ($a, b, c$). By iterating through shells of particles, researchers can estimate how the flattening or elongation of a galaxy changes with distance from its center.

While useful, this method has three primary drawbacks:
1.  **Computational Cost:** For high-resolution simulations with millions of particles per galaxy, performing iterative calculations on discrete particles is computationally expensive.
2.  **Noise Sensitivity:** In the low-density outskirts of galaxies, discreteness noise (the random spacing of individual particles) can lead to unstable results and inaccurate axis orientations.
3.  **The Ellipsoidal Constraint:** Most importantly, the inertia tensor assumes an ellipsoidal geometry. Real galaxies frequently exhibit features that violate this assumption. For example, a "box/peanut" bulge or a highly "pointed" disk cannot be accurately described by a standard ellipsoid.

Gal3D addresses these issues by first converting discrete particle data into a smooth density field and then fitting a more flexible geometric model—the superellipsoid.

## From Particles to Continuous Density Fields

The first step in the Gal3D framework is the construction of a continuous stellar mass density field using **Adaptive Kernel Density Estimation (AKDE)**. Instead of treating stars as points, AKDE smooths the mass of each particle over a local volume. 

The degree of smoothing is adaptive: in the dense centers of galaxies, the smoothing length $h(x)$ is small to preserve fine detail, while in the sparse outer regions, it is larger to suppress noise. The local density $\rho(x)$ is determined by the contribution of the $k=32$ nearest neighbors. This process produces a smooth, differentiable map of the galaxy's mass distribution, allowing the researchers to evaluate the density at any arbitrary point in space.

Once the density field is established, the framework uses a spherical Fibonacci lattice to sample the galaxy. This lattice provides $N=1024$ nearly uniform directions (rays) originating from the galaxy's center. By tracing these rays and identifying where the density drops to specific target levels, the framework identifies "iso-density surfaces"—3D shells where the stellar density is constant.

## The Superellipsoid: Beyond Simple Ovals

The core contribution of Gal3D is the use of the **superellipsoid** to model these iso-density surfaces. A superellipsoid is a generalization of the standard ellipsoid that introduces three additional parameters: the shape indices $S_a, S_b, S_c$. 

In a standard ellipsoid, these indices are all equal to 1. By allowing them to vary, Gal3D can describe a much wider variety of shapes:
*   **Boxiness ($S > 1$):** High indices create shapes with flatter sides and sharper corners, characteristic of certain galactic bars and box/peanut bulges.
*   **Diskiness/Pointedness ($S < 1$):** Low indices create "diamond-like" or cuspy shapes, which better represent the vertical profile of thin stellar disks.

![superellipsoid-shape-indices](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12933v1/var_shape_index_sab_c_examples.png)
*{fig:superellipsoid-shape-indices}: Examples of how varying the superellipsoid shape indices $S_a, S_b, S_c$ allows the model to transition between pointed, ellipsoidal, and boxy geometries.*

For every density level, Gal3D fits a superellipsoid by optimizing a set of parameters: the semi-axes ($a, b, c$), the shape indices ($S_a, S_b, S_c$), the center offsets ($\Delta x_c, \Delta y_c, \Delta z_c$), and the orientation (Euler angles). The fitting process minimizes an objective function, $\chi^2_D$, which measures the radial difference between the sampled density points and the model surface. This method proves to be significantly more stable than previous approaches, especially for capturing the vertical shape of disks.

## Quantifying Radial Structure and Components

By applying this fitting process across the entire radial range of a galaxy, Gal3D generates "radial shape profiles." These profiles act as a fingerprint for the galaxy's internal components. The researchers defined two primary axis ratios to describe flattening and elongation:

$$\n\epsilon_{ac} = 1 - \frac{c}{a}\n$$
$$\n\epsilon_{ab} = 1 - \frac{b}{a}\n$$

Here, $\epsilon_{ac}$ represents the overall flattening (large values indicate a thin, disk-like structure), and $\epsilon_{ab}$ represents elongation in the plane (large values indicate a bar-like structure).

The utility of this approach is evident when looking at individual galaxies. For instance, in a galaxy with a **box/peanut bulge**, Gal3D detects a specific signature: a central increase in $\epsilon_{ab}$ (the bar) combined with an increase in the shape index $S_c > 1$ (the boxiness). In galaxies with **warps**, the framework detects a gradual shift in the orientation angles $\theta_{c-z}$ as the minor axis of the fitted shells tilts away from the galaxy's main rotation axis at larger radii.

## Comparing the Universes of TNG and EAGLE

The paper applies Gal3D to thousands of galaxies from two of the most prominent simulation suites: **IllustrisTNG** (TNG50 and TNG100) and **EAGLE**. While both simulations aim to reproduce the observed universe, they use different numerical methods and "subgrid" physics (models for star formation and black hole feedback). Gal3D reveals that these choices lead to distinct morphological outcomes.

### Disk Flattening and Resolution
A key finding is that galaxies in TNG tend to be thinner and more radially extended than those in EAGLE. Within the TNG suite itself, resolution plays a major role. Galaxies in the high-resolution TNG50-1 run show significantly higher flattening ($\epsilon_{ac}$) and lower vertical shape indices ($S_c \sim 0.5$) compared to the lower-resolution TNG50-2 and TNG100 runs. This suggests that as simulation resolution improves, galaxies are better able to maintain thin, cuspy disk structures.

### Bar Strength and Bulges
TNG galaxies also exhibit more prominent bars and box/peanut bulges compared to EAGLE galaxies of the same mass. In EAGLE, the bar-related elongation signal ($\epsilon_{ab}$) is systematically weaker. Furthermore, the signature of box/peanut-shaped bulges (elevated $S_a$ and $S_c$) is much more pronounced in TNG. These differences likely stem from how the simulations handle the heating of stellar disks and the feedback from supermassive black holes.

### Massive Ellipticals and Triaxiality
For the most massive galaxies ($M_{*30} > 10^{11} M_{\odot}$), both simulations show a transition from disk-dominated to pressure-supported systems, likely due to a history of mergers. However, EAGLE's massive ellipticals tend to be more triaxial or prolate in their outer regions than TNG's. Gal3D identifies a resurgence of elongation ($\epsilon_{ab}$) in the outskirts of massive EAGLE galaxies, a feature not seen in TNG, pointing to potential differences in their late-stage assembly histories.

## Numerical Robustness and Open Science

To ensure that the results were not artifacts of the methodology, the authors performed extensive sensitivity tests. They found that the recovered shape profiles were remarkably stable against changes in the number of neighbors ($k \geq 16$) used for density estimation and the number of sampling rays ($N_{ray} = 64$ was often sufficient). The specific choice of the $\chi^2_D$ objective function was also critical; simpler functions often failed to converge or produced physically unrealistic "diamond" shapes in the vertical direction.

By releasing Gal3D as an open-source tool, the authors provide the community with a standardized way to quantify 3D morphology. This is particularly important for comparing results across different research groups who might otherwise use disparate, non-comparable methods.

## Significance of the Work

Gal3D provides a sophisticated lens through which to view simulated galaxies. By moving beyond the simple ellipsoid, it allows researchers to:
1.  **Map specific physical components** (like bars, warps, and box/peanut bulges) to quantitative geometric parameters.
2.  **Highlight systematic differences** between simulation models, providing a benchmark for refining subgrid physics.
3.  **Prepare for future observational comparisons.** Although Gal3D works in 3D, its models can be projected into 2D to simulate what future telescopes might see, helping to interpret the complex light distributions found in real-world surveys.

In summary, this work establishes a flexible and robust framework for galaxy morphology, demonstrating that the shape of a galaxy is a continuous, radially varying property that contains the keys to understanding how these massive stellar structures came to be.
