## The Challenge of Interest Rate Model Calibration

In the financial world, predicting the movement of interest rates is essential for valuing complex derivatives, managing risk, and meeting regulatory requirements like Solvency II. A standard tool for this task is the G2++ model, a two-factor stochastic model that describes how the "short rate" (the rate at which money can be borrowed for a very short period) evolves over time. The G2++ model is favored because it is mathematically tractable—meaning we can derive clear formulas for the prices of bonds and options—and flexible enough to capture complex market behaviors, such as the "humped" volatility often seen in interest rate markets.

However, using such a model requires "calibration": adjusting its five parameters to match observed market data, such as the prices of interest rate caps (which act like insurance against rising rates). Traditionally, this has been treated as a simple numerical optimization problem. The goal is to minimize the difference between the model's predicted prices and the actual market prices, typically measured by the Root Mean Squared Relative Error (RMSRE).

![model-prices-projection](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.20420v1/fig01_model_prices_projection.png)
*{fig:model-prices-projection}: Geometric interpretation of calibration as a projection of market data onto the curved surface defined by the model's price functions.*

The research by Mahler and Ruckdeschel highlights a significant gap in this standard practice. A low calibration error (a low RMSRE) does not necessarily mean the model is stable or that the parameters are reliable. If the market data changes slightly, will the parameters stay the same? Which specific market quotes are driving the results? If a single data point is an outlier, how much does it skew the entire model? The authors argue that calibration should be viewed not just as an optimization task, but as a statistical estimation problem that requires rigorous diagnostic tools to ensure robustness and transparency.

## Statistical Foundations: From Optimization to Regression

To bridge the gap between numerical optimization and statistical analysis, the authors first establish a formal link between the common RMSRE objective and the theory of Weighted Least Squares (WLS) regression.

The RMSRE for a set of $m$ market instruments is defined as:

$$\text{RMSRE}(\Pi) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{m} \sum_{k=1}^{m} \left( \frac{y_{k}^{\text{Mkt}} - g_{k}(\Pi)}{y_{k}^{\text{Mkt}}} \right)^{2}}$$

where $y_{k}^{\text{Mkt}}$ is the market price, $g_{k}(\Pi)$ is the model-implied price, and $\Pi$ is the vector of five parameters. The authors show that minimizing this error is mathematically equivalent to a WLS problem where the objective function is:

$$Q(\Pi) = \sum_{k=1}^{m} w_{k} (y_{k}^{\text{Mkt}} - g_{k}(\Pi))^{2} \text{ where } w_{k} = \frac{1}{(y_{k}^{\text{Mkt}})^{2}}$$

This equivalence is crucial because it justifies using weights $w_{k}$ that are inversely proportional to the squared market prices. Statistically, this corresponds to an assumption that the pricing errors are multiplicative rather than additive, which is appropriate for financial data where prices can span several orders of magnitude. By framing calibration as WLS, the authors can apply a suite of diagnostic tools from non-linear regression to understand the "geometry" of the calibration—how the model surface relates to the observed data points.

## The Jacobian Factorization: Efficiency and Exactness

Diagnostics require understanding how sensitive the model prices are to changes in parameters. This is captured by the Jacobian matrix $J$, which contains the partial derivatives of all model prices with respect to all parameters. In many practical applications, these derivatives are calculated numerically (by slightly changing a parameter and seeing how the price moves), which can be slow and prone to error.

The G2++ model parameters include two mean-reversion speeds $a_{x}$ and $a_{y}$, two volatilities $\sigma_{x}$ and $\sigma_{y}$, and a correlation $\rho_{xy}$. A unique contribution of this paper is the derivation of an exact analytical factorization for the Jacobian of G2++ cap prices. They show that the derivative of a cap price with respect to any parameter $p$ can be broken down into:

1.  **Price-to-Variance Sensitivity:** How the price changes relative to the total variance of the interest rate process.
2.  **Variance-to-Parameter Sensitivity:** How that variance changes relative to the specific G2++ parameter.

This analytical approach is not only more precise than numerical methods but also significantly faster, making it feasible to run complex diagnostics daily in a production environment.

## Measuring Data Impact: Leverage and Influence

One of the primary questions the authors address is: which data points have the most "leverage" over the model? In regression, leverage refers to the potential of a single observation to pull the fitted model toward itself.

The authors utilize the **Weighted Hat Matrix** ($H_{w}$):

$$H_{w} = W^{1/2} J (J^{\top} W J)^{-1} J^{\top} W^{1/2}$$

The diagonal elements of this matrix, called leverage scores, range from 0 to 1. A score close to 1 for a specific cap maturity (e.g., the 30-year cap) means the model is almost forced to fit that price exactly, potentially at the expense of other data points.

![leverage-scores](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.20420v1/fig03a_leverage_scores.png)
*{fig:leverage-scores}: Leverage scores across different cap maturities for Euro ATM data. The 3-year and 30-year caps consistently show the highest leverage.*

Empirical analysis reveals that leverage in the Euro interest rate market is "boundary-dominated." The very short-term (3-4 years) and very long-term (30 years) maturities exert the most influence on the model's geometry. While leverage shows potential influence, the **Influence Score** measures realized influence—how much the parameters actually moved because of a specific observation. The influence vector is calculated as:

$$\text{IF}(y_{k}; \hat{\Pi}) = (y_{k}^{\text{Mkt}} - g_{k}(\hat{\Pi})) \cdot ((J^{\top} W J)^{-1} J_{k}^{\top} w_{k})$$

This combines the geometric leverage with the actual residual (the error in fitting that specific point). The authors find that shocks in short-maturity prices, when combined with their high leverage, are the most frequent causes of large parameter displacements.

![influence-scores](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.20420v1/fig03b_influence_scores.png)
*{fig:influence-scores}: Influence scores on a log scale, showing that while geometry is boundary-dominated, realized influence is spread across maturities, with occasional spikes at the short end.*

## Assessing Identifiability and Degrees of Freedom

A common problem in complex models is "over-parameterization," where the data isn't rich enough to uniquely determine all parameters. The authors use the **Effective Degrees of Freedom (EDoF)**, defined as the trace of the hat matrix, to diagnose this. For a five-parameter model like G2++, the EDoF should ideally be 5.

![effective-degrees-of-freedom](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.20420v1/fig04_edof_trace.png)
*{fig:effective-degrees-of-freedom}: The trace of the hat matrix over time. Drops below 5 indicate periods where parameters become locally unidentifiable, often because a constraint (like correlation hitting -1) is active.*

The study shows that while the EDoF is usually 5, it frequently drops to 4 or 3. This typically happens when parameters hit their boundaries—for instance, when the correlation $\rho_{xy}$ hits its limit of $-1$. These drops signal to risk managers that the model has lost a "dimension" of flexibility, making the remaining parameters less certain. Crucially, the authors demonstrate that the RMSRE might stay low even when EDoF drops, proving that error metrics alone cannot detect these structural weaknesses in calibration.

## Regime Shifts in the Euro Interest Rate Market

By applying Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to the daily calibrated parameter sets from 2016 to 2025, the authors identified a major shift in the calibration environment.

![pca-regimes](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.20420v1/fig06_pca_regimes.png)
*{fig:pca-regimes}: PCA of calibrated parameters showing a clear regime shift. The market moved from a stable horizontal band (2016-2021) to a more volatile vertical trajectory during the 2022-2024 interest rate transition.*

Between 2016 and 2021 (a period of negative interest rates in Europe), the calibration was very stable, with variations mostly limited to the volatility parameters $\sigma_{x}$ and $\sigma_{y}$. However, starting in 2022, as the European Central Bank began raising rates, the calibration regime expanded significantly. The model began to see much larger fluctuations in the mean-reversion speeds $a_{x}$ and $a_{y}$. This suggests that the "information content" of market data changed, requiring the model to adjust its structural parameters more aggressively to maintain a good fit.

## Robust Parameter Uncertainty

Finally, the paper addresses how to measure the precision of the calibrated parameters. Standard methods often produce confidence intervals that are either too wide (due to outliers) or physically impossible (e.g., suggesting a volatility could be negative).

The authors solve this by using two advanced techniques:
1.  **Median Absolute Deviation (MAD):** Instead of using the standard variance of residuals, which is easily skewed by one bad data point, they use the MAD to estimate the noise level. This provides a more stable estimate of "typical" error.
2.  **Variance Stabilizing Transformations (VST):** To ensure confidence intervals respect parameter boundaries, they transform the parameters into an unconstrained space (using logarithms for positive values and the inverse hyperbolic tangent for correlation). They then calculate the uncertainty and map it back to the original space.

![robust-confidence-intervals](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.20420v1/fig07_robust_ci_ay.png)
*{fig:robust-confidence-intervals}: Robust confidence intervals for the mean-reversion parameter $a_{y}$. The bands become significantly wider and more volatile during the 2024-2025 period, reflecting increased market uncertainty.*

These "boundary-respecting" intervals are often asymmetric, providing a much more realistic picture of parameter risk than the symmetric intervals used in traditional statistics.

## Significance and Practical Significance

The primary contribution of this work is the provision of a transparent, theoretically grounded "dashboard" for interest rate model calibration. Rather than trusting a single "optimal" parameter set, practitioners can now evaluate:
- **Identifiability:** Are all parameters actually being determined by the data?
- **Stability:** How sensitive is the calibration to a single market quote?
- **Uncertainty:** What is the statistical "margin of error" for each parameter?

For insurance companies and banks, this framework is a vital component of model governance. It allows them to detect when a calibration is becoming fragile—even if the price fit looks perfect—and provides a clear mathematical basis for explaining why parameters might be fluctuating during periods of market stress. By moving from simple optimization to a full diagnostic suite, the authors provide the tools necessary for more reliable risk management and financial valuation.
