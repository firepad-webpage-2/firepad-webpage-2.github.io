## The Geometric Foundation of Semiparametric Inference

Semiparametric efficiency theory (SET) provides the mathematical scaffolding for modern statistical inference, particularly in the fields of causal inference and missing data analysis. It serves as the basis for developing estimators that are both robust and efficient, such as one-step estimators, targeted minimum loss estimators (TMLE), and debiased machine learning procedures. Despite its widespread utility, SET is often perceived as a dense and disparate collection of concepts—scores, tangent spaces, and influence functions—whose interrelationships can be difficult to grasp.

The central thesis of this work is that SET can be viewed as a systematic application of differential calculus to a space of probability distributions. By treating probability distributions as "points" in an infinite-dimensional space and statistical parameters as "functions" defined on that space, the complex machinery of semiparametric theory becomes an intuitive geometric framework. This perspective allows researchers to understand how model restrictions and parameter definitions interact to determine the limits of statistical precision.

The significance of this geometric approach lies in its ability to unify disparate concepts. For instance, the "tangent space" is not just a technical construct; it is the statistical equivalent of a tangent plane, defining the allowable directions in which a distribution can move within a given model. Similarly, "influence functions" act as gradients, representing how a parameter changes in response to these movements. By organizing these concepts into a "Calculus–Statistics Dictionary," the theory becomes more accessible to those familiar with basic multivariable calculus.

## The Calculus–Statistics Dictionary: A Unified Framework

To bridge the gap between ordinary calculus and semiparametric theory, we establish a systematic correspondence. In standard multivariable calculus, we operate in a Euclidean space where a point $x$ can be shifted along a curve $x_\epsilon$ to explore the behavior of a function $f$. In semiparametric theory, the space of interest is the set of all possible probability distributions $P$. A statistical functional $\psi(P)$ (the parameter of interest) plays the role of the function $f(x)$.

Movement in this space is described by a "path," which is a one-dimensional submodel $P_\epsilon$ indexed by a scalar $\epsilon$. At $\epsilon = 0$, the path passes through the true, baseline distribution $P_0$. Just as the velocity vector $v$ represents the direction and speed of movement along a curve $x_\epsilon$, the "score function" $S$ represents the direction of infinitesimal movement along a path of distributions.

| Calculus Concept | Statistics Analogue | Description |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Point $x$ | Distribution $P$ | The fundamental unit of the space. |
| Function $f(x)$ | Functional $\psi(P)$ | The quantity we wish to estimate. |
| Curve $x_\epsilon$ | Path $P_\epsilon$ | A local movement in the space. |
| Velocity $v$ | Score $S$ | The infinitesimal direction of the path. |
| Tangent Plane | Tangent Space $\mathcal{T}$ | The set of all allowable directions. |
| Gradient $\nabla f$ | Influence Function $\phi$ | The representative of the derivative map. |
| Projected Gradient | Efficient Influence Function $\phi_{\text{eff}}$ | The unique, optimal gradient within the model constraints. |

This dictionary provides the conceptual structure for the remainder of the theory. Every complex construction in SET corresponds to a familiar operation in the calculus of surfaces.

## Perturbations and the Statistical Score

In Euclidean space, a direction is a vector $v$. In the space of distributions, a direction is a function $S(O)$ of the observed data $O$. This is because changing a distribution requires specifying how probability mass is redistributed across the entire sample space. If we move along a path $P_\epsilon$, the "velocity" of this movement is captured by the score function:

$$S(O) = \left. \frac{\partial}{\partial \epsilon} \log p_\epsilon(O) \right|_{\epsilon=0}$$

The score $S(O)$ identifies which values of $O$ gain probability mass (where $S(O) > 0$) and which values lose mass (where $S(O) < 0$) as we move away from $P_0$. Because the total probability must always sum to one, all scores must have a mean of zero under $P_0$. These scores live in a Hilbert space $L^2_0(P_0)$ of mean-zero, square-integrable functions, where the "inner product" of two directions is their covariance.

## The Tangent Space: Defining Allowable Model Movement

Not all directions of movement are permissible. A statistical model $\mathcal{M}$ represents a subset of all possible distributions, often defined by constraints (e.g., independence assumptions, linear relationships). The tangent space $\mathcal{T}$ is the collection of all score functions $S$ that correspond to paths remaining within the model $\mathcal{M}$.

Geometrically, $\mathcal{T}$ is analogous to a tangent plane on a curved surface. It defines the "allowable" infinitesimal directions of movement for $P_0$. In a nonparametric model, where no assumptions are made, $\mathcal{T}$ is the entire space $L^2_0(P_0)$. In a parametric or semiparametric model, $\mathcal{T}$ is a smaller subspace. The tangent space depends strictly on the model $\mathcal{M}$ and the baseline distribution $P_0$; it does not depend on the parameter we are trying to estimate.

## Pathwise Differentiability and the Influence Function

Once we have defined the space and the allowable directions, we ask: how does the parameter $\psi(P)$ change as we move? We define the pathwise derivative $\dot{\psi}(S)$ as the derivative of $\psi(P_\epsilon)$ with respect to $\epsilon$ at $\epsilon = 0$. This is the statistical equivalent of a directional derivative $Df_x(v)$.

A parameter is "pathwise differentiable" if there exists a function $\phi(O)$ that represents this derivative through an inner product:

$$\dot{\psi}(S) = E_{P_0}[\phi(O) S(O)]$$

This function $\phi$ is called an "influence function." In multivariable calculus, the gradient $\nabla f$ is the unique vector such that the directional derivative is $\nabla f(x)^\top v$. However, in statistics, if the tangent space $\mathcal{T}$ is a proper subspace of $L^2_0(P_0)$, the influence function $\phi$ is not unique. Any function $\phi$ that satisfies the equation above for all $S$ in $\mathcal{T}$ is a valid influence function. Geometrically, this is because adding any vector perpendicular to the tangent space (a vector in $\mathcal{T}^\perp$) to the gradient does not change the result of the inner product with vectors inside the tangent space.

## The Efficient Influence Function as an Orthogonal Projection

Among the many possible influence functions, there is one that is uniquely important: the efficient influence function $\phi_{\text{eff}}$. This function is the statistical analogue of the "projected gradient" in constrained optimization. It is the unique influence function that lies entirely within the tangent space $\mathcal{T}$.

We obtain the efficient influence function by taking any valid influence function $\phi$ and projecting it onto the tangent space:

$$\phi_{\text{eff}} = \Pi(\phi | \mathcal{T})$$

This projection is fundamental to efficiency. The "nuisance tangent space" $\mathcal{T}_\eta$ consists of all directions $S$ in $\mathcal{T}$ along which the parameter $\psi$ does not change ($\dot{\psi}(S) = 0$). The efficient influence function is the only representative that is orthogonal to these nuisance directions. Specifically, it lies in the subspace $\mathcal{T} \cap \mathcal{T}_\eta^\perp$, the "informative" part of the tangent space.

## Statistical Applications: Bounds and Estimators

The geometric properties of the efficient influence function have immediate consequences for statistical practice.

First, the efficient influence function defines the fundamental limit of precision. The squared norm (variance) of the efficient influence function, $\text{Var}(\phi) = E_{P_0}[\phi(O)^2]$, is the semiparametric efficiency bound. This bound is the lowest possible asymptotic variance that any regular, asymptotically linear estimator can achieve under the given model $\mathcal{M}$. Because projection always reduces the length of a vector, $\phi_{\text{eff}}$ (the projected gradient) always has a variance less than or equal to any other influence function $\phi$.

Second, influence functions provide a first-order approximation of the parameter, known as a von Mises expansion:
$$\psi(P) - \psi(P_0) = -E_{P_0}[\phi(O)] + \text{remainder}$$
For an empirical distribution $P_n$, this approximation leads to the construction of estimators. Since $E_{P_0}[\phi(O)] = 0$, the expansion suggests that an estimator $\hat{\psi}$ can be improved by adding the empirical average of the influence function. This is the logic behind one-step estimation:
$$\hat{\psi}_{os} = \psi(\hat{P}) + \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \phi(O_i)$$
where $\hat{P}$ is an initial (possibly biased) estimate. The influence function effectively "de-biases" the initial estimator, leading to an estimator that converges at a fast $n^{-1/2}$ rate, even when the initial components (like machine learning models) converge more slowly.

In summary, re-framing semiparametric efficiency theory as differential calculus clarifies that efficiency is not an abstract concept but a result of geometric projection. The statistical model determines the tangent space, the parameter determines the gradient, and their intersection defines the best possible way to extract information from data.
