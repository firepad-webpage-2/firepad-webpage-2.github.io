## Integrated Bayesian Hierarchical Forecasting

Hierarchical forecasting involves making predictions for a set of related time series organized into a multi-level structure. A common example is retail demand, where individual product sales at specific stores (bottom level) must aggregate to total sales for a region, a state, and finally the entire country (top levels). A critical requirement for these systems is coherence: the forecasts for the lower levels must sum up to the forecasts for the higher levels. Traditional approaches typically handle this as a post-processing step known as reconciliation. After generating base forecasts independently for each level, a reconciliation algorithm adjusts them to ensure the summation constraints are met. While methods like Minimum Trace (MinT) are effective for point forecasts, they often struggle with probabilistic calibration and do not naturally incorporate the hierarchical structure into the model's initial training phase.

This research proposes a fully Bayesian framework that shifts coherence from a post-processing correction to an integral part of parameter estimation. Instead of forcing perfect coherence after the fact, the framework uses a soft penalty to guide the model toward coherent solutions during the learning process. This approach allows for a flexible balance between fitting the data and satisfying the aggregation constraints. Furthermore, the framework introduces a "level-focusing" mechanism, enabling users to prioritize the accuracy of specific hierarchical levels that are most relevant to their decision-making needs. By leveraging Bayesian hierarchical priors, the method also facilitates partial pooling, where information is shared across related series to improve the stability of parameter estimates, especially for series with sparse or noisy data.

## Theoretical Foundations: The Bayesian Predictive Distribution

At the heart of this framework is the Bayesian treatment of uncertainty. For any given time series, the goal is to estimate a parameter vector $\theta_i$ based on observed data $y$. In a standard Bayesian setup, we define a likelihood $p(y|\theta)$ and a prior distribution for the parameters. The posterior distribution $p(\theta|y)$ is then updated as new data arrives.

To generate a one-step-ahead forecast $\hat{y}_{T+1}$, the framework uses the posterior predictive distribution:
$$
p(\hat{y}_{T+1}|y) = \int p(\hat{y}_{T+1}|\theta) p(\theta|y) d\theta
$$
This integration accounts for both the inherent noise in the data and the uncertainty in the estimated parameters. For multi-step-ahead forecasts $\hat{y}_{T+h}$, the framework iteratively integrates over predicted future values. This propagation of uncertainty is a significant advantage over frequentist reconciliation methods, which often require the estimation of complex covariance matrices for multi-step forecast errors—a task that is computationally demanding and prone to estimation error.

## Information Sharing via Hierarchical Priors

In many hierarchical datasets, individual series at the same level share common characteristics. For instance, the demand for different types of soft drinks in a single region might follow similar seasonal patterns. The proposed framework exploits this by using a Bayesian hierarchical prior. Instead of assuming the parameters $\theta_i$ for each series $i$ are independent, they are treated as being drawn from a common population distribution characterized by hyperparameters $\phi$:
$$
p(\theta, \phi) = p(\phi) \prod p(\theta_i|\phi)
$$
This structure allows for partial pooling: the estimate for $\theta_i$ is "shrunk" toward the group mean $\phi$. This is particularly powerful for bottom-level series with limited historical data; their parameters are effectively informed by the behavior of their "peers" in the hierarchy. The standard deviation of the hyperprior can be learned from the data, determining how much influence the group mean should have on individual series.

## Soft Coherence Penalization

Rather than enforcing coherence as a hard constraint (e.g., $y_{\text{total}} = \sum y_{\text{bottom}}$), the framework introduces a soft penalty term into the likelihood. Let $d_t$ represent the difference (incoherence) between the aggregated values of lower-level series and their corresponding upper-level series at time $t$. The framework penalizes this incoherence by multiplying the likelihood $\prod p(y_{t,i}|\theta_i)$ by a Gaussian density function centered at zero:
$$
p(d|\sigma^2_{\text{coh}} I_T) = \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_{\text{coh}} I_T)
$$
The parameter $\sigma_{\text{coh}}$ controls the stringency of the penalty. A small value for $\sigma_{\text{coh}}$ acts as a "tight" penalty, forcing the model to find parameters that yield near-perfect coherence. A larger value allows for more flexibility. This soft approach is more robust to model misspecification—if a perfectly coherent solution is highly improbable given the data, the model will not be forced into an unrealistic prediction just to satisfy the constraint.

## Level-Weighted Forecasting

A common challenge in organizational forecasting is that different levels of the hierarchy have different levels of importance. A national manager might care most about the accuracy of the total country forecast, while a local manager focuses on specific regional sales. Current reconciliation methods generally treat all levels equally during the optimization process.

The proposed framework addresses this through a level-weighting mechanism. The likelihood contribution for specific series $y_{1,i}, \dots, y_{T,i}$ or an entire hierarchical level is raised to a power $\lambda_i > 1$:
$$
p(y_{t,i}|\theta_i)^{\lambda_i}
$$
This effectively increases the relative importance of that data during parameter estimation. When $\lambda_i$ is applied to a specific level, the global model update prioritizes fitting the data at that level. Because of the hierarchical prior and the coherence penalty, improvements at the focused level can propagate to neighboring levels, potentially enhancing the overall performance of the system while ensuring the results remain relevant to the primary decision-maker.

## Performance Evaluation on Australian Tourism Data

The framework was tested on a five-level hierarchy of Australian domestic tourism data, categorized by geographical regions and purpose of travel (e.g., holiday, business). The base model used was an Exponential Smoothing (ETS) model. The study compared several variants: independent Bayes (no hierarchy), hierarchical Bayes (with pooling), Bayesian models with different coherence penalties, and level-weighted models. These were benchmarked against the standard MinT reconciliation method.

The results demonstrated that level-weighting successfully improved accuracy at the targeted levels. For example, when the top level (Australia total) was up-weighted, the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) at that level decreased significantly compared to the unweighted hierarchical model. Interestingly, this improvement often extended to the next level down (States), showing that the hierarchical structure allows focused improvements to "leak" beneficial information to related series.

![crps-performance](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.23009v1/Figures/CRPS_line.png)
*{fig:crps-performance}: Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS) relative to independent Bayes. Similar to RMSE, the level-weighted approaches provide the best probabilistic accuracy at the focused levels.*

In terms of probabilistic performance, measured by the Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS) and Energy Score (ES), the Bayesian hierarchical approaches generally outperformed the independent models. A Nemenyi post-hoc test, which ranks models based on their overall performance across all time series and levels, indicated that the hierarchical Bayes models were statistically superior to independent Bayes and its reconciled counterparts.

## Probabilistic Calibration and Coherence

A major drawback of traditional reconciliation methods like MinT is their impact on calibration. Often, the adjustment process leads to prediction intervals that are too narrow, resulting in undercoverage (e.g., a "95% prediction interval" that only contains the true value 80% of the time). The empirical study found that MinT consistently suffered from undercoverage at upper hierarchical levels. In contrast, the proposed Bayesian framework maintained much better calibration. While level-weighting introduced a slight degree of overconfidence at the focused levels, the coverage remained significantly closer to the nominal 95% target than the MinT methods.

Regarding coherence, the soft penalty was effective at reducing both point and distributional incoherence. Point incoherence was measured using Mean Absolute Incoherence (MAI), while distributional coherence was measured using the Jensen-Shannon Distance (JSD). The study observed that as $\sigma_{\text{coh}}$ was reduced, incoherence decreased, but only up to a point. The remaining JSD even with a tight penalty served as a diagnostic tool, indicating that the base model (ETS) was slightly misspecified and could not perfectly capture the relationships between all levels simultaneously.

## Significance and Future Directions

This work demonstrates that integrating hierarchical constraints directly into the Bayesian learning process provides a flexible and robust alternative to post-hoc reconciliation. By using soft penalties and hierarchical priors, the framework avoids the rigidity of hard constraints and the calibration issues often associated with post-processing. The level-weighting mechanism is a particularly practical contribution, as it allows organizations to tailor their forecasting systems to their specific strategic priorities.

One of the most important takeaways is the framework's ability to handle uncertainty. By propagating parameter and data uncertainty through the hierarchy during estimation, it provides more reliable probabilistic forecasts. This is essential for risk management, where understanding the full range of possible outcomes is more important than just having a single "best guess."

Future research could explore the use of more complex base models, such as neural networks or state-space models, within this Bayesian framework. Additionally, the framework could be extended to handle temporal hierarchies (e.g., ensuring daily forecasts aggregate to monthly and yearly forecasts) or cross-temporal hierarchies where both geographical and temporal constraints must be satisfied simultaneously. The ability of the soft penalty to act as a diagnostic for model misspecification also opens up new avenues for model selection in complex hierarchical environments.
