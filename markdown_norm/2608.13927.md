## Research Landscape and Context

The study of harmonic functions—solutions to Laplace's equation—is a cornerstone of classical analysis and partial differential equations (PDEs). While Euclidean harmonic functions are well-understood, extending their properties to non-Euclidean geometries, such as the hyperbolic upper half-space, introduces significant analytical challenges. A central theme in this area is the "regularity" of functions, which describes how smooth or continuous a function is globally, based on local or directional information.

Historically, researchers have investigated "transversal" regularity: if a function behaves well (e.g., is smooth or continuous) along specific directions, does it necessarily behave well everywhere? In Euclidean settings, it is known that if a harmonic function is uniformly Hölder continuous along vertical lines (perpendicular to the boundary), it is globally Hölder continuous on its domain. This relationship relies on a specific property of the Euclidean Laplacian: the partial derivatives of a harmonic function are themselves harmonic.

However, in hyperbolic geometry, this property does not hold. The real hyperbolic Laplacian $\Delta_h$ on the upper half-space $H^n = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^{n-1} \times \mathbb{R} : y > 0\}$ is defined as:

$$\Delta_h = y^2 \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_i^2} + \frac{\partial^2}{\partial y^2} \right) - (n-2)y \frac{\partial}{\partial y}$$

If $u$ is hyperbolic harmonic (meaning $\Delta_h u = 0$), its vertical derivative $\partial_y u$ generally satisfies an inhomogeneous equation rather than $\Delta_h(\partial_y u) = 0$. Consequently, the classical Euclidean techniques for propagating regularity from vertical lines to the global domain are not directly applicable. This paper investigates the validity of these transversal regularity principles within the hyperbolic framework, focusing on the differences between sub-critical Hölder continuity ($0 < \alpha < 1$) and the critical Lipschitz endpoint ($\alpha = 1$).

## Foundational Framework: Hyperbolic Poisson Extensions

To analyze hyperbolic harmonic functions, the authors utilize the hyperbolic Poisson extension. Any bounded hyperbolic harmonic function $u$ on the upper half-space $H^n$ can be represented as the convolution of a boundary function $f$ (defined on $\mathbb{R}^{n-1}$) with the hyperbolic Poisson kernel $P_y$. For $d = n-1$, the extension is given by:

$$P[f](x, y) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} P_y(x-t) f(t) dt$$

where the kernel $P_y$ is defined as:

$$P_y(x) = c_d \frac{y^d}{(|x|^2 + y^2)^d}$$

and $c_d = \Gamma(d) / (\pi^{d/2} \Gamma(d/2))$ is a normalization constant. This kernel acts as an approximate identity: as $y \to 0$, the function $u(x, y)$ converges to the boundary values $f(x)$.

The analysis heavily involves the Fourier transform of this kernel. Specifically, the frequency-domain representation is $\hat{P_y}(\xi) = m_d(y|\xi|)$, where $m_d(t)$ is a Fourier–Bessel multiplier involving the modified Bessel function of the second kind $K_{d/2}$:

$$m_d(y|\xi|) = \frac{2^{1-d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)} (y|\xi|)^{d/2} K_{d/2}(y|\xi|)$$

The properties of $m_d(t)$ and its derivatives are essential for controlling the approximation error between the interior function $u(x, y)$ and the boundary function $f(x)$. By using dyadic decompositions (breaking the function into frequency bands), the authors establish how vertical smoothness relates to the rate at which $u$ approaches $f$ as one moves toward the boundary.

## Sub-Critical Hölder Regularity ($0 < \alpha < 1$)

The first major finding of the paper concerns the range $0 < \alpha < 1$. In this regime, the researchers establish a quantitative equivalence between vertical regularity and global regularity. 

Define the vertical Hölder seminorm $V_\alpha(u)$ as:

$$V_\alpha(u) = \sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}^d, s, t > 0, s \neq t} \frac{|u(x, s) - u(x, t)|}{|s - t|^\alpha}$$

And the global Hölder seminorm $[u]_{\alpha, H^n}$ as:

$$[u]_{\alpha, H^n} = \sup_{z_1, z_2 \in H^n, z_1 \neq z_2} \frac{|u(z_1) - u(z_2)|}{|z_1 - z_2|^\alpha}$$

The authors prove that for a bounded hyperbolic harmonic mapping $u$, the condition $V_\alpha(u) < \infty$ is equivalent to $[u]_{\alpha, H^n} < \infty$. This means that if a mapping is consistently smooth in the vertical direction, it is guaranteed to be smooth in every direction across the entire upper half-space.

Furthermore, for real-valued functions, the paper explores a modulus-based criterion. It demonstrates that if the modulus of the function $|u|$ approaches the modulus of its boundary trace $|f|$ at a uniform $\alpha$-Hölder rate along vertical lines, then $u$ is globally $\alpha$-Hölder continuous. This is quantified by the condition:

$$M_\alpha(u) = \sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}^d, y > 0} \frac{||u(x, y)| - |f(x)|| }{y^\alpha} < \infty$$

The methodology here relies on inverse approximation theory. By showing that $V_\alpha(u) < \infty$ implies an approximation error $\|u(\cdot, y) - f\|_{L^\infty} = O(y^\alpha)$, the authors use Fourier–Bessel multiplier estimates to prove that the boundary function $f$ must belong to the Hölder space $C^{0,\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^d)$. Once the boundary trace is shown to be Hölder continuous, the regularity propagates back into the interior.

## The Lipschitz Endpoint and the Breakdown of Direct Equivalence

The behavior of harmonic functions often changes significantly at "integer" regularity levels. For $\alpha = 1$, which corresponds to Lipschitz continuity, the simple equivalence observed for $0 < \alpha < 1$ fails. 

The authors demonstrate this by constructing a counterexample. They define a hyperbolic harmonic function $U$ whose boundary trace is a specific lacunary series:

$$f(x) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty 2^{-j} \sin(2^j x_1)$$

This function $f$ is bounded and continuous but is famously not Lipschitz continuous. However, by analyzing the vertical derivative of its Poisson extension $U = P[f]$, the authors show that $U$ is vertically Lipschitz continuous. This proves that vertical Lipschitz regularity does not imply global Lipschitz regularity. In functional-analytic terms, vertical Lipschitz regularity only places the function in the Zygmund class ($B^1_{\infty, \infty}$), which is strictly larger than the Lipschitz class.

## Recovering Regularity: Dini–Zygmund Summability

To bridge the gap between the Zygmund class and the Lipschitz class at the endpoint $\alpha = 1$, the paper introduces an additional requirement: a Dini-type summability condition. This condition ensures that the approximation error $E_u(t) = \|u(\cdot, t) - f\|_{L^\infty(\mathbb{R}^d)}$ decays fast enough as $t \to 0$.

Specifically, the authors prove that a bounded, vertically Lipschitz hyperbolic harmonic mapping $u$ is globally Lipschitz if and only if it satisfies:

$$\int_0^1 \frac{E_u(t)}{t^2} dt < \infty$$

This integral condition is related to the Dini–Zygmund functional $D(g)$, which measures the smoothness of a function $g$ via its second-order modulus of smoothness $\omega_2(g, t)_\infty$:

$$D(g) = \int_0^1 \frac{\omega_2(g, t)_\infty}{t^2} dt$$

The condition $D(g) < \infty$ characterizes the Besov space $B^1_{\infty, 1}$. The paper shows that if the boundary function $f$ (or its modulus $|f|$ for real functions) satisfies this Dini–Zygmund condition, then the hyperbolic harmonic extension is globally Lipschitz. This result provides a precise corrective term that allows the extension of transversal regularity principles to the critical Lipschitz case.

## Significance of the Findings

This work provides a comprehensive characterization of the relationship between directional interior regularity and global smoothness for hyperbolic harmonic functions. Its significance lies in several key areas:

1.  **Geometric Extension:** It successfully adapts classical harmonic analysis results to the hyperbolic setting, where standard Euclidean derivative-harmonicity arguments fail. This requires a more nuanced approach using Bessel function properties and frequency-domain analysis.
2.  **Addressing the "Inverse Problem":** While much of the literature focuses on how boundary smoothness dictates interior behavior (the "direct problem"), this paper solves the "inverse problem"—determining what interior measurements (vertical slices) reveal about the global function and its boundary.
3.  **Boundary-Interior Correspondence:** The results establish a rigid link between the vertical decay of a harmonic function toward the boundary and the smoothness of its boundary trace.
4.  **Refinement of Function Space Theory:** By identifying the exact Dini summability condition needed at $\alpha = 1$, the research clarifies the relationship between Zygmund and Lipschitz classes in the context of non-Euclidean PDEs.

The analytical tools developed—particularly the characterization of the $B^1_{\infty, 1}$ space via Poisson approximation error in hyperbolic space—provide a framework that can be applied to other problems in hyperbolic geometry and potentially to other types of elliptic operators. The study underscores that while hyperbolic and Euclidean geometries differ significantly in their underlying Laplacians, they share fundamental principles of regularity, provided the correct summability conditions are accounted for at critical indices.
