In the quantitative analysis of financial markets, understanding the hidden patterns of price movements and volatility is essential for risk management and asset pricing. One of the most significant parameters used to characterize these patterns is the Hurst exponent $H$. This single value describes the self-similarity and memory of a stochastic process. When a process is self-similar, its statistical properties remain the same regardless of the time scale at which it is observed. Mathematically, for a process $X(t)$, this is expressed as:

$$X(at) \stackrel{d}{=} a^H X(t) \quad \forall a > 0, t \geq 0$$

where $\stackrel{d}{=}$ denotes equality in distribution. The exponent $H \in (0,1)$ determines the nature of the process: $H = 1/2$ corresponds to standard Brownian motion (independent increments), $H < 1/2$ indicates "rough" or anti-persistent behavior (tendency to reverse trends), and $H > 1/2$ indicates long-range dependence or persistence (tendency to follow trends).

While estimating $H$ is a fundamental task, traditional statistical methods encounter a severe theoretical bottleneck known as a "phase transition" when the process exhibits long-range dependence ($H > 1/2$). In these scenarios, standard tests like the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test—which compares the distributions of increments at different scales—break down. Their convergence rates slow down significantly, and the resulting distributions become unstable, making it difficult to perform reliable hypothesis testing. This paper addresses this challenge by introducing a novel filtering technique based on the Grünwald-Letnikov (GL) fractional derivative, which restores statistical stability and allows for accurate detection of market states.

## The Limits of Statistical Self-Similarity Testing

The core principle behind distribution-based tests for self-similarity is scaling invariance. If a process is $H$-self-similar, then the distribution of its increments over a time interval $1$ should be identical to the distribution of its increments over a time interval $a$, provided the latter is scaled by $a^{-H}$. This motivates the use of a two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov type statistic. By comparing the empirical distribution of unit-scale increments with the empirical distribution of scaled multi-scale increments, researchers can identify the value of $H$ that makes these two distributions match.

However, the reliability of this comparison depends on the independence of the observations. In fractional Gaussian noise (fGn), which is the increment process of fractional Brownian motion:

$$Y_k = B_H(k) - B_H(k-1)$$

the correlation between observations decays slowly when $H > 1/2$. This persistent memory creates a "clustering" effect in the data that distorts the empirical distribution function. Previous research demonstrated that for $H \leq 1/2$, the KS statistic converges at the standard $\sqrt{n}$ rate. But when $H > 1/2$, the normalization factor changes to $n^{1-H}$ and the limit distribution becomes a degenerate random variable. This transition makes the test practically unusable for long-memory processes, as the critical values needed for testing depend on the very parameter $H$ one is trying to estimate.

## Decoupling Memory from Scaling with the GL Filter

The paper's primary innovation is the use of the Grünwald-Letnikov (GL) fractional derivative to "filter" the process. The GL derivative of order $\alpha$ is defined as a discrete weighted sum of past observations:

$$\Delta^{\alpha}_{GL,h}f(x) = h^{-\alpha}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \omega_k(\alpha)f(x-kh)$$

where the weights $\omega_k(\alpha)$ are defined by the binomial coefficients:

$$\omega_k(\alpha) = \frac{(-1)^k \Gamma(\alpha + 1)}{\Gamma(k + 1)\Gamma(\alpha - k + 1)} = \binom{\alpha}{k}(-1)^k$$

The author demonstrates that applying this filter to a fractional process has two critical effects. First, it preserves the underlying self-similarity parameter $H$. Even though the filter changes the specific values of the data, the scaling relationship between increments at different time scales remains governed by the same $H$. Second, and most importantly, the filter modifies the memory of the process. By selecting an appropriate filter order $\alpha$, one can effectively "subtract" the long-range dependence.

In the spectral domain, long-range dependence is characterized by a singularity at the zero frequency (low-frequency dominance). The GL filter acts as a high-pass filter that neutralizes this singularity. If the filter order is chosen such that $H - \alpha < 1/2$, the filtered process behaves like a short-memory or anti-persistent process, regardless of how high the original $H$ was. This transformation restores the standard $\sqrt{n}$ convergence rate, allowing for the use of stable Gaussian limits in statistical inference.

## The Grünwald-Letnikov-Kolmogorov-Smirnov (GL-KS) Framework

The proposed GL-KS test statistic, $\tilde{D}_{n,m}(\theta)$, measures the distance between the empirical cumulative distribution functions (ECDFs) of the filtered samples at different scales for a candidate exponent $\theta$:

$$\tilde{D}_{n,m}(\theta) = \sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} |\tilde{F}_n(x) - \tilde{G}_m(x)|$$

The paper proves that for a process with a true Hurst exponent $H_0 > 1/2$, if the GL filter order $\alpha$ is chosen to satisfy $H_0 - \alpha < 1/2$, the standardized statistic:

$$D^{\dagger}_{n,m} = \sqrt{\frac{nm}{n+m}}\tilde{D}_{n,m} \xrightarrow{d} \sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} |\mathcal{G}(x)|$$

converges in distribution to the supremum of a centered continuous Gaussian process. This is a significant result because it provides a universal benchmark for testing self-similarity that does not suffer from the distortions of the long-memory regime.

To find the most likely $H$, the author proposes a minimum-distance estimator:

$$\hat{H}_{GLKS} = \arg\min_{\theta \in H} \tilde{D}_{n,m}(\theta)$$

Because the GL derivative involves an infinite sum, practical implementation requires truncating the series. This introduces a "burn-in" problem: the early observations in a filtered series are less accurate because they lack sufficient historical data. The paper provides a mathematical rule for determining the number of observations to discard, $N_0 = \lfloor n^\gamma \rfloor$. The theoretical analysis shows that if the burn-in exponent $\gamma$ is large enough, specifically:

$$\gamma > \frac{1}{2(1+\alpha-H)}$$

the error from truncation becomes negligible as the sample size $n$ grows.

The effectiveness of the GL-KS framework is validated through extensive Monte Carlo simulations. The author generates thousands of trajectories of fractional Brownian motion with known $H$ values and applies the test.

## Applications to Rough Volatility and Market Efficiency

The GL-KS framework is applied to two major problems in financial mathematics: the rough volatility paradigm and the Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH).

### Rough Volatility Paradigm
Modern financial econometrics has shifted toward the "rough volatility" view, which suggests that the volatility of asset prices is not a standard diffusion process but rather a fractional process with a very low Hurst exponent ($H \approx 0.1$). Using the GL-KS estimator on log-realized volatility data from indices like the S&P 500, FTSE 100, and Nikkei 225, the author finds consistent evidence for this hypothesis. The estimated $H$ values are typically between 0.05 and 0.15, far below the $1/2$ mark of Brownian motion. This "roughness" has significant implications for pricing options and managing tail risk, as it implies that volatility is much more "spiky" and less persistent than previously thought in classical models.

### Weak-Form Efficient Market Hypothesis
The EMH suggests that asset prices should follow a random walk, meaning increments are independent and $H = 1/2$. Any significant deviation from $H = 1/2$ suggests a degree of market inefficiency.
- **Persistence ($H > 1/2$):** Prices exhibit trends; a price increase is likely to be followed by another increase.
- **Anti-persistence ($H < 1/2$):** Prices are mean-reverting; a price increase is likely to be followed by a decrease.

The GL-KS test provides a rigorous way to determine if a market is truly efficient by checking if the confidence interval for $H$ includes $0.5$. The analysis of international stock indices reveals that while many markets appear efficient over long windows, several exhibit statistically significant persistence ($H > 1/2$). These persistent "states" suggest that historical price information could, in theory, be used to predict future movements, challenging the strict weak-form EMH.

## Conclusion

The work presented by Daniele Angelini provides a robust solution to a long-standing obstacle in fractional calculus and financial statistics. By utilizing the Grünwald-Letnikov derivative as a memory-decoupling filter, the GL-KS framework allows researchers to accurately measure self-similarity even in the most challenging long-memory environments. This tool not only validates the rough nature of market volatility but also provides a diagnostic instrument for detecting periods of market inefficiency. The regime-adaptive nature of the method—switching between standard KS for short memory and GL-KS for long memory—ensures its reliability across a wide range of empirical applications.
