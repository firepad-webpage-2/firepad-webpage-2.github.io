## Introduction

The deployment of autonomous systems in real-world environments requires a delicate balance between performance and safety. While performance often involves optimizing a system's behavior to reach a target or follow a trajectory, safety requires the system to strictly adhere to physical constraints, such as staying within a specific area or avoiding actuator saturation. This balance becomes significantly more difficult in the presence of unknown external disturbances, such as wind gusts for drones or varying friction for robotic platforms.

In systems classified as "underactuated," the challenge is further amplified. An underactuated system is one where there are fewer independent control inputs than there are degrees of freedom in the system's configuration. In these cases, a single motor or actuator might be responsible for maintaining multiple safety constraints simultaneously. If these constraints demand conflicting actions from the same actuator, the system may enter an unsafe state.

Traditional control methods like $H_{\infty}$ control are designed to minimize the impact of disturbances on overall performance, providing an integral guarantee of stability (often referred to as an $L_2$-gain guarantee). However, they do not inherently prevent transient violations—short-term "glitches" where the system might briefly exceed a safety boundary. This research introduces a unified framework that integrates robust performance controllers with disturbance observers and safety filters to provide certified safety guarantees even for underactuated systems with limited control authority.

## Core Principles of Robust Safety Filtering

The framework operates on linear time-invariant systems affected by unknown disturbances, represented by the state-space equation:

$$\dot{x} = Ax + Bu + Ed$$

where $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the state, $u \in \mathbb{R}^m$ is the control input, and $d \in \mathbb{R}^p$ is the unknown disturbance. In underactuated systems, the number of independent control channels is less than the number of configuration variables, denoted as $\text{rank}(B) < n_q$.

To manage performance, the framework starts with a baseline $H_{\infty}$ controller. This controller is derived from a zero-sum differential game, where the control input $u$ attempts to minimize a cost function while the disturbance $d$ acts as an adversary attempting to maximize it. The cost function is defined as:

$$J_\gamma(u,d;x_0) = \int_0^\infty (x^T Qx + u^T Ru - \gamma^2 d^T d) dt$$

The result is a state-feedback control law $u_\infty(x) = -R^{-1} B^T P x$, where $P$ is found by solving an algebraic Riccati equation. While this baseline ensures the system behaves well on average, it does not "know" about the specific safety boundaries defined by the environment or the physical limits of the actuators.

## Disturbance Observation and Certified Bounds

A central difficulty in robust safety is the uncertainty of the disturbance $d$. If a controller assumes the worst-case disturbance at all times, it becomes overly conservative, potentially preventing the system from moving at all. Conversely, ignoring the disturbance leads to safety violations.

This research utilizes a Luenberger-type disturbance observer to estimate the realized disturbance in real-time. Let $\hat{d}$ be the estimated disturbance and $e_d = d - \hat{d}$ be the estimation error. The observer assumes that the rate of change of the disturbance is bounded by some value $\|\dot{d}(t)\| \leq \omega_1$. Crucially, the observer provides not just an estimate, but a certified time-varying uncertainty set:

$$D(t) = \{\delta \in \mathbb{R}^p : \|\delta - \hat{d}(t)\| \leq \rho(t)\}$$

The value $\rho(t)$ represents a rigorous bound on the estimation error at time $t$. As the observer gathers more data, this bound evolves. By knowing that the true disturbance $d$ is guaranteed to lie within this set, the safety filter can adjust the system's behavior based on the current estimation quality rather than a static, global worst-case scenario. To further improve behavior, the baseline controller is augmented with a "disturbance-compensated" term:

$$u_{OI}(x, \hat{d}) = F(x - X_d \hat{d}) + U_d \hat{d}$$

where $F$ is the $H_{\infty}$ gain, and $X_d, U_d$ are matrices that pre-calculate the state and input required to offset a constant disturbance.

## The Safety-Critical Control Filter

Safety is mathematically defined using Control Barrier Functions (CBFs). A safe set is described by the region where a function $h(x) \geq 0$. For many physical systems, the control input does not immediately affect $h(x)$; instead, it affects a higher-order derivative (like acceleration affecting position). This requires High-Order Control Barrier Functions (HOCBFs).

For a system with a relative degree $r$, the $r$-th derivative of $h(x)$ contains the control input. The framework defines robust safety conditions that must hold even for the worst-case disturbance within the uncertainty set $D(t)$. For a safety constraint $j$, this results in the condition:

$$\Gamma_{rob,j}(x, u, \hat{d}, t) = \beta_j(x) + a_j(x)u + c_j(x)\hat{d} - \rho(t) \|c_j(x)\| \geq 0$$

where $\beta_j, a_j,$ and $c_j$ are functions derived from the system dynamics and the HOCBF. The term $\rho(t) \|c_j(x)\|$ explicitly accounts for the uncertainty in the disturbance estimate.

The final applied control $u^\star$ is found by solving a Quadratic Program (QP). This optimization minimizes the deviation from the desired performance-oriented input $u_{OI}$ while strictly satisfying all safety and actuator constraints:

$$u^\star(x, \hat{d}, t) = \text{arg} \min_{u \in U} \frac{1}{2} \|u - u_{OI}\|^2_R$$

subject to $\Gamma_{rob,j} \geq 0$ for all constraints $j$. This ensures that the system stays safe ($h(x) \geq 0$) for all future time, provided that a feasible control input exists.

## Feasibility and Performance Trade-offs

One of the unique contributions of this work is the exact feasibility analysis for systems with a scalar (single) control input. In such systems, the multiple safety constraints and actuator limits can be combined into a single, precise interval $[u^S, \bar{u}^S]$. If the lower bound $u^S$ is greater than the upper bound $\bar{u}^S$, the set of safe inputs is empty, signaling a fundamental inability to maintain safety. The difference $\mu_S = \bar{u}^S - u^S$ provides a "feasibility margin," indicating how much room the controller has to maneuver.

Additionally, the paper introduces a way to quantify how much performance is sacrificed to maintain safety. By integrating the $H_{\infty}$ energy balance over a finite time horizon, the authors define an accumulated deviation metric:

$$J_{dev}(T) = \int_0^T \|u^\star(t) - u_\infty(x(t))\|^2_R dt$$

This value $J_{dev}(T)$ represents the "cost of safety"—the extra energy or performance loss incurred because the safety filter had to override the baseline controller to prevent a constraint violation.

## Experimental Validation: Two-Wheeled Balancing Robot

The framework was tested on a Two-Wheeled Balancing Robot (TWBR), a classic underactuated system. The robot must maintain its position $p$ within a tight range while also keeping its body-pitch angle $\theta$ within safe limits to prevent falling. Both objectives share a single wheel-torque input $\tau$.

![twbr-results](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.10872v1/x1.png)
*{fig:twbr-results}: Simulation results comparing the proposed controller with standard LQR and $H_{\infty}$ controllers. The proposed method (solid black line) is the only one to satisfy all constraints (dashed horizontal lines) across different disturbance profiles like step inputs and wind gusts.*

The simulations showed that standard controllers (LQR and $H_{\infty}$) frequently violated safety limits when hit by disturbances like severe wind gusts. The baseline $H_{\infty}$ controller, while robust in an average sense, allowed the robot's position to drift outside the safe boundary during transient responses. In contrast, the proposed safety filter successfully modulated the torque to prioritize safety. 

A key observation was the competition between constraints: during a disturbance, the robot might be forced to "give up" some position accuracy to ensure the pitch angle remains safe, demonstrating how the filter intelligently manages the limited control authority of an underactuated system. The numerical results also validated the finite-horizon performance balance, with the calculated $J_{dev}$ accurately reflecting the deviations observed in the torque profiles.

## Conclusion and Implications

This research provides a rigorous method for ensuring the safety of autonomous systems operating in unpredictable environments. By combining disturbance observation with high-order safety functions, it moves away from overly conservative "worst-case" designs toward adaptive, certified safety. 

The primary contribution lies in the synthesis of these distinct control theories—$H_{\infty}$ for performance, observers for uncertainty, and HOCBFs for safety—into a single, verifiable architecture. For engineers designing underactuated systems like drones, balancing robots, or agile manipulators, this framework offers a way to guarantee that state and actuator limits will not be breached, even when the environment is uncertain. The ability to calculate exact feasibility margins and performance costs further allows for more informed design of safety-critical autonomous behaviors.
