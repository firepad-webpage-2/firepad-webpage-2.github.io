## Introduction to Multivariable Graphical Stability Analysis

In the study of linear time-invariant (LTI) systems, stability analysis often relies on frequency-domain techniques. For single-input single-output (SISO) systems, the Nyquist plot provides a comprehensive visual representation of a system's open-loop frequency response, allowing engineers to determine stability by counting encirclements of the critical point $-1$. However, extending these intuitive geometric insights to multi-input multi-output (MIMO) systems is non-trivial. The standard approach for MIMO systems involves eigenloci—trajectories of eigenvalues of the transfer matrix over frequency—but these suffer from a lack of "compositionality." Specifically, the eigenloci of a large network cannot be easily determined from the eigenloci of its constituent parts.

To manage the complexity of high-dimensional networks, researchers often use over-approximation methods that bound the system's gain or phase. While $H_{\infty}$ theory focuses on gain, newer methods have emerged to characterize the multivariable phase, such as singular angles and the Scaled Relative Graph (SRG). The original SRG was a step toward a multivariable Nyquist plot, but it lacked the ability to distinguish between phase lead and phase lag—a distinction essential for accurate stability margins.

This paper introduces the $\theta$-symmetric SRG, a geometric tool that refines the original SRG by incorporating a directional parameter $\theta$. This allows the representation to capture both phase magnitude and sign. By developing the algebraic properties of this graph, the authors provide a framework to analyze the stability of "cactus networks"—complex structures where multiple feedback loops are interconnected at specific vertices (hubs). This framework allows for a layer-by-layer decomposition of the network, significantly simplifying the stability assessment of large-scale dynamic systems.

## The $\theta$-Symmetric Scaled Relative Graph

The core innovation of this work is the generalization of how angles are measured between complex vectors. For any matrix $C \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$, the relationship between an input vector $x$ and its output $Cx$ can be characterized by their relative gain and phase. The authors define a generalized angle $\angle_{\theta}(x, y)$ that incorporates a reference rotation $e^{j\theta}$:

$$\angle_{\theta}(x, y) = \arccos\left(\frac{\text{Re}\langle x, e^{-j\theta}y \rangle}{\|x\| \|y\|}\right)$$

Building on this, the $\theta$-symmetric SRG of a matrix $C$ is defined as the set:

$$SRG_{\theta}(C) = \left\{ \frac{\|Cx\|}{\|x\|} \exp(j(\theta \pm \angle_{\theta}(x, Cx))) : 0 \neq x \in \mathbb{C}^n \right\}$$

This definition ensures that the graph is symmetric about the ray defined by $e^{j\theta}\mathbb{R}$. Unlike the original SRG, which was always symmetric about the real axis and resulted in conjugate pairs that lost the sign of the phase, the $\theta$-symmetric version can be "rotated" to better fit the actual phase distribution of the system. This rotation allows the graph to represent phase lead or lag explicitly, making it a more precise multivariable analog to the scalar Nyquist plot.

Geometrically, the $\theta$-symmetric SRG encloses the "gain-phase" information of the matrix. The phase aspect is captured by the $\theta$-segmental phase, denoted as $\Psi_{\theta}(C) = [\theta - \Gamma_{\theta}(C), \theta + \Gamma_{\theta}(C)]$, where $\Gamma_{\theta}(C)$ represents the phase spread around the center $\theta$. By choosing an optimal $\theta$, one can minimize the phase spread, creating a "canonical" representation that provides the tightest possible enclosure for stability analysis.

## Computational Framework via Semidefinite Programming

One of the historical hurdles in using segmental phases or singular angles for MIMO systems was the difficulty of computation. The authors resolve this by establishing a direct link between the phase spread and a norm minimization problem. They prove that the phase spread $\Gamma_{\theta}(C)$ satisfies:

$$\Gamma_{\theta}(C) = \arcsin(\min_{\gamma>0} \|\gamma e^{-j\theta}C - I\|)$$

This relationship is significant because it transforms a geometric phase calculation into a standard optimization problem. Specifically, the condition $\min_{\gamma>0} \|\gamma e^{-j\theta}C - I\| \leq \sin \Gamma$ can be reformulated using the Schur complement into a Linear Matrix Inequality (LMI):

$$\begin{bmatrix} \sin^2 \Gamma I & \gamma e^{-j\theta}C - I \\ \gamma e^{j\theta}C^* - I & I \end{bmatrix} \geq 0$$

Using Semidefinite Programming (SDP), researchers can now efficiently compute the tightest phase bounds for high-dimensional matrices. For cases where the phase spread is larger (exceeding $\pi/2$), the paper provides an alternative characterization based on a Generalized Eigenvalue Problem (GEP), ensuring that the method remains robust across all possible system behaviors.

## Compositional Properties for Interconnected Systems

A primary utility of the $\theta$-symmetric SRG is its ability to handle system interconnections. In many engineering applications, complex systems are built by summing signals (parallel interconnections) or cascading outputs (series interconnections). The authors derive fundamental algebraic properties that govern these operations.

For sum-type interconnections, the subadditive property is established:

$$SRG_{\theta}\left(\sum_{i=1}^N A_i\right) \subset \sum_{i=1}^N SRG_{\theta}(A_i)$$

This holds under the "$\theta$-chord property," which requires that the individual SRGs are suitably shaped (essentially that they do not "wrap around" the origin too much). Similarly, for product-type interconnections (cascades), the submultiplicative property states:

$$SRG_{\sum \theta_i}\left(\prod_{i=1}^N A_i\right) \subset \prod_{i=1}^N SRG_{\theta_i}(A_i)$$

These results are crucial because they allow a designer to bound the behavior of a total system by simply performing set-based addition or multiplication on the SRGs of the components. This compositional approach circumvents the need to calculate the eigenvalues of the full system directly, which is often computationally expensive and provides less intuition about which specific component might be driving the system toward instability.

## Stability of Cyclic and Cactus Dynamic Networks

The paper applies these matrix-level properties to dynamical systems, where the components are transfer function matrices $P_i(s)$ evaluated at $s = j\omega$. For a cyclic feedback system consisting of $N$ cascaded subsystems, the loop transfer matrix is $L(j\omega) = P_N(j\omega) \cdots P_1(j\omega)$. 

The authors establish a generalized Nyquist criterion: the cyclic system is stable if the nominal system is stable and the set-based product of the individual SRGs does not contain the critical ray segment:

$$[- \infty, -1] \cap \prod_{i=1}^N SRG_{\theta_i}(P_i(j\omega)) = \emptyset$$

Beyond simple cycles, the paper addresses the stability of cactus networks. A cactus network is a collection of loops where any two loops share at most one vertex. These vertices act as "hubs" where local feedback loops (petals) influence the global behavior.

![sunflower-graph-decomposition](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12591v1/img-14.jpeg)
*{fig:sunflower-graph-decomposition}: A "sunflower" graph, representing a fundamental unit of a cactus network. The stability of the central loop depends on the aggregated behavior of the attached petal loops.*

The analysis follows a hierarchical, recursive procedure:
1.  **Local Stability:** First, verify the stability of the outermost "petal" loops using the cyclic criteria.
2.  **Aggregation:** The effect of a stable petal loop is aggregated into an equivalent system that acts on the shared hub vertex. This is done by bounding the return difference matrix of the petal loop.
3.  **Reduction:** The original cactus graph is reduced by replacing the petals with these equivalent hubs.
4.  **Global Stability:** The process continues inward until only a central loop remains. The stability of this final loop, considering the modified hub dynamics, determines the stability of the entire network.

This decomposition strategy allows for the analysis of massive networks by breaking them into manageable, nested stability problems. By utilizing the flexibility of the $\theta$ parameter at each step, the analysis remains significantly less conservative than previous methods that relied on fixed phase definitions.

## Conclusion and Significance

The introduction of the $\theta$-symmetric SRG fills a critical gap in multivariable control theory. By providing a framework that is both computationally efficient (via SDP) and compositionally flexible, it enables the stability analysis of complex, multi-loop structures like cactus networks. 

The unique contribution of this work lies in the directional parameter $\theta$, which allows for a true multivariable extension of the Nyquist plot that captures phase lead and lag. The developed necessary and sufficient conditions for robust stability under mixed gain-phase uncertainty provide a rigorous foundation for designing reliable large-scale systems. Whether applied to power grids or biological signaling pathways, the $\theta$-symmetric SRG offers a sophisticated geometric lens through which the stability of interconnected dynamics can be understood and ensured.
