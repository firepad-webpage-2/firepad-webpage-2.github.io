In the modern global economy, industries such as aviation, maritime shipping, and logistics operate as integrated networks that span multiple countries and regulatory zones. While these firms coordinate their operations across borders, the climate policies they face are often fragmented, consisting of a patchwork of local, national, and international regulations. This research examines how firms strategically navigate these disjointed regulatory environments and how regulators, in turn, compete or cooperate to manage environmental impacts and local welfare.

## The Interplay of Integrated Networks and Fragmented Policy

A central challenge in climate governance is the "policy gap" between global industry operations and local regulatory reach. For instance, an airline might operate a network connecting New York, London, and Paris. This airline faces the European Union’s Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) for flights within Europe, the global CORSIA offsetting scheme for international legs, and potential future national carbon taxes in the United States. 

When a regulator in one region imposes a carbon charge, the firm does not simply absorb the cost on that specific route. Instead, it adjusts its entire system. These adjustments manifest as changes in ticket prices, the frequency of flights, and the types of aircraft deployed. Because these firms operate in network structures, a cost increase in one market can influence the firm's competitive posture in another. This research demonstrates that environmental policy acts as a system-wide operational shock, propagating through the firm's pricing and capacity allocation across its entire footprint.

## A Game-Theoretic Framework for Regulation

To analyze these complex interactions, the study utilizes a two-stage game-theoretic model. In the first stage, regulators determine their environmental policies (such as carbon charges or subsidies) to maximize their jurisdiction's social welfare. In the second stage, firms compete within the markets, choosing their service levels, prices, and fleet composition based on the regulatory costs they face.

The researchers define social welfare as the sum of several components:
$$
SW = CS + \pi + \text{Revenue} - \text{Damage}
$$
where $SW$ represents social welfare, $CS$ is consumer surplus (the benefit to passengers), $\pi$ represents the profits of the firms, "Revenue" is the government income from environmental charges, and "Damage" is the estimated cost of environmental impact, such as the social cost of carbon.

A critical feature of the model is the integration of the "Mohring effect." In network industries like aviation, consumers benefit not just from low prices but also from higher service frequency. If an airline increases the number of flights, passengers find it easier to schedule their travel, effectively reducing their "wait time" costs. This creates a link between demand and service frequency:
$$
f_i = \frac{q_i}{s}
$$
where $f_i$ is the service frequency, $q_i$ is the number of passengers, and $s$ is the seat capacity of the aircraft. This relationship means that regulatory charges on emissions—which are often tied to fuel burn and thus flight frequency—can indirectly harm consumers by reducing service levels, beyond just raising ticket prices.

## Regulatory Scenarios and Market Distortions

The research compares several governance architectures to understand which leads to the best environmental and economic outcomes.

1.  **Baseline:** A scenario with no environmental charges, serving as a point of comparison.
2.  **Decentralized Regional Regulation:** Independent local regulators (e.g., the US and the EU) set their own charges $(\tau_L, \tau_L')$ to maximize local welfare, often ignoring the impacts on the other region.
3.  **Uniform Global Regulation:** A single global authority sets a common charge $\tau_G$ for all markets to maximize total global welfare.
4.  **Differentiated Global Regulation:** A global authority sets region-specific charges to account for differences in market power, costs, and environmental sensitivity.
5.  **Overlapping Regulation:** A situation where both local and global regulators impose charges simultaneously, a common reality in many sectors today.

One of the study's key findings is that the optimal environmental charge in these industries often deviates from the standard "Pigouvian" benchmark (where the tax equals the marginal environmental damage). Because firms in network industries often possess market power, they already tend to restrict output to keep prices high. If a regulator adds a full carbon tax on top of this, it might restrict service too much, leading to a net loss in social welfare. Consequently, the optimal charge $\theta_r$ is often lower than the social cost of carbon, and in some cases, regulators might even find it beneficial to subsidize airlines to maintain service levels while using other means to address emissions.

## Strategic Firm Responses: Frequency vs. Price

The study finds that firms do not respond to climate policy in a uniform way. Their strategy depends heavily on their business model and the markets they serve.

In the case study of North American and European aviation, "Legacy Carriers" (like Delta or Lufthansa) tend to use their market power in hub-and-spoke networks to pass a significant portion of regulatory costs on to passengers through higher fares. In contrast, "Low-Cost Carriers" (LCCs) act primarily as capacity adjusters. Because LCCs often face more price-sensitive demand, they are more likely to reduce flight frequencies and withdraw aircraft from regulated routes rather than significantly raising fares.

Across the entire network, the research shows that capacity contraction (reducing the number of flights) is a more prominent response than passenger reduction. Airlines actively shrink their service to maintain high load factors (filling more seats per flight) as the cost of operating each flight increases due to carbon charges.

## The Efficiency of Differentiated Regulation

The analysis reveals that **Differentiated Global Regulation** is the most effective at maximizing global welfare. By allowing different charges in different regions—for example, a higher charge in North America where market power might be stronger, and a lower one in Europe—a global regulator can balance the need for emission reductions with the specific market distortions present in each region.

However, the study also highlights a significant barrier to this ideal policy: it is often politically unstable. While the global welfare might be higher, individual regions might find themselves worse off than they would be under decentralized, local regulation. For example, if a global policy requires North America to implement a high carbon charge that primarily benefits the global climate but hurts local consumers and airlines, North American regulators have an incentive to defect from the agreement.

## Cooperation and the Need for Side Payments

The researchers conclude that for the most efficient climate policies to be sustainable, there must be mechanisms for "side payments" or financial transfers between regions. 

In the transatlantic case study, the model suggests that for a coordinated global policy to be stable, Western Europe might need to provide annual transfers to North America. These payments would compensate for the disproportionate loss in local consumer and producer surplus that North America would experience under the globally optimal settings. 

This finding shifts the conversation from merely finding the "correct" price for carbon to designing political and financial frameworks that can support that price. Without these transfer mechanisms, the "strategic cost" of fragmented policy remains: jurisdictions will continue to set suboptimal, uncoordinated charges to protect their own interests, leading to lower overall welfare and less effective climate action.

## Significance for Policy and Industry

This research provides a more nuanced understanding of climate policy in network-driven sectors. It suggests that:

*   **Regulators must look beyond emissions:** Effective carbon pricing must account for market structures, including the competitive behavior of firms and the positive externalities of service frequency.
*   **Uniformity is not always optimal:** A "one-size-fits-all" global carbon price might be less efficient than a coordinated but differentiated approach that respects regional economic differences.
*   **Political feasibility requires redistribution:** The technical efficiency of a climate policy is not enough to ensure its adoption. Policymakers must address the distributional consequences between regions to prevent regulatory "free-riding" or protectionism.

For firms, the research underscores that environmental regulation is a structural change that influences long-term capital decisions, such as fleet renewal, and requires sophisticated revenue management strategies that account for regional regulatory variations. As climate policy continues to evolve, the interaction between firm strategy and regulatory competition will remain a defining feature of global industry.
