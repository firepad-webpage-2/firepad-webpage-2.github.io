## The Diffusion of Generative AI in the Modern Workplace

Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) has rapidly transitioned from a theoretical possibility to a practical tool for knowledge workers. While consumer adoption of tools like ChatGPT has been highly visible, the way formal organizations integrate these models into their professional workflows remains less understood. This research provides a detailed analysis of firm-level adoption using internal telemetry data from ChatGPT Enterprise, offering a window into how businesses are deploying AI at scale.

Generative AI is increasingly categorized as a General Purpose Technology (GPT), similar to the steam engine or the internet, because of its potential to disrupt a wide array of industries and occupations. However, the economic impact of such technologies is rarely immediate. History suggests that the transition from a new capability to realized productivity gains requires a period of "co-invention," where firms experiment with new organizational structures, invest in human capital, and redefine job tasks.

The study focuses on the early stages of this diffusion process, tracking how organizations of various sizes and industries adopt the technology. By linking de-identified usage data with public financial records, the researchers identify which types of firms are leading the charge and how their internal usage patterns differ across worker seniority levels and job functions.

## Measuring Enterprise AI Adoption via Telemetry

Historically, researchers have relied on surveys to understand how businesses adopt new technologies. While useful, surveys can suffer from recall bias and a lack of granularity regarding actual usage. This paper overcomes these limitations by utilizing message-level telemetry data. The researchers analyzed several distinct data samples to build a comprehensive picture:

1.  **Aggregate Usage Sample:** Tracking output tokens—a measure of AI-generated content—across thousands of organizations to observe growth trends.
2.  **Worker Characteristics Sample:** Using job titles to understand which departments (e.g., Finance, Engineering, Marketing) and which roles (e.g., Individual Contributor, Executive) are most active.
3.  **Task Classification Subsample:** Applying an automated classifier (using a model designated as $gpt-5-mini$ in the report) to categorize millions of messages into 60 distinct work tasks.
4.  **Public Company Sample:** Linking usage metrics to financial data from the Compustat database to see how firm performance correlates with AI adoption.

By measuring the number of weekly active users ($WAU$) and the volume of messages per user, the study distinguishes between "breadth" (how many people use it) and "intensity" (how much each person uses it).

## Firm-Level Drivers of AI Adoption

One of the paper's central findings is that the early adopters of enterprise AI are not average firms. In the sample of U.S. public companies, adopters of ChatGPT Enterprise were significantly larger, more valuable, and more heavily invested in intangible assets compared to non-adopters.

![token-growth-by-cohort](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12236v1/fig/enterprise_output_tokens_monthly_by_adoption_cohort.png)
*{fig:token-growth-by-cohort}: Monthly output tokens by adoption cohort, showing that growth comes from both new firms joining and deepening usage within existing adopters.*

The researchers used linear probability models to estimate the likelihood of adoption, represented conceptually as:

$$
P(\text{Adopt}_{it}) = \beta X_{it-1} + \gamma_{j} + \delta_{t} + \epsilon_{it}
$$

Where $X_{it-1}$ represents lagged financial characteristics, $\gamma_{j}$ represents industry effects, and $\delta_{t}$ represents time effects. The results indicate that firms with higher revenue per employee and larger existing stocks of Research and Development ($R\&D$) and Sales, General, and Administrative ($SG\&A$) expenses are more likely to adopt.

This suggests that AI adoption is complementary to existing organizational capital. Firms that have already invested in specialized software, sophisticated management practices, and skilled workforces are better equipped to integrate generative AI. Interestingly, while larger firms are more likely to adopt, they often show lower usage intensity per employee than smaller adopters, suggesting that scaling AI across a massive workforce involves significant organizational friction.

## Distribution of Use Across the Workforce

A common misconception is that generative AI is primarily a tool for software developers or technical specialists. The data shows that ChatGPT Enterprise usage is actually broadly distributed across various business functions.

While Engineering and Technical Practitioners account for approximately $11\%$ of weekly active users at the average firm, substantial usage is also found in:
*   **Executives and Founders:** $9\%$ of active users.
*   **Finance and Accounting:** $5\%$ of active users.
*   **Marketing and Communications:** $5\%$ of active users.

![job-title-user-share](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.12236v1/fig/job_title_class_weekly_active_user_share_snapshots_week26.png)
*{fig:job-title-user-share}: Share of weekly active users across different job title classes. Usage is distributed across a wide variety of professional functions.*

There is also a notable "seniority gradient" in how the technology is used. Seniority level strongly predicts usage intensity: early-career workers and trainees sent significantly more messages per week than managers and executives. Specifically, managers and directors made up $24\%$ of the user base, while individual contributors and senior individual contributors accounted for $15\%$ and $14\%$, respectively. 

This gradient suggests that junior employees may be using AI to bridge knowledge gaps or automate high-volume production tasks, while senior leaders use it more selectively for high-level synthesis or oversight.

## Mapping AI to Specific Work Tasks

To understand the "how" of AI adoption, the researchers categorized the content of interactions. The results reveal that AI is being used as a versatile "assistant" across a wide range of tasks rather than for a single dominant purpose.

The most prevalent tasks—those performed by the largest share of users—include:
*   **Documentation and Technical Writing:** Over half of all users ($56.3\%$) engaged in this task.
*   **Technical Digital Work:** Nearly half ($49.6\%$) used the tool for technical or electronic tasks, including coding.
*   **Interpersonal Messaging:** $41.1\%$ of users used the tool to help draft or refine communications.

However, the "message share" (the percentage of total messages dedicated to a task) tells a different story. While many people use the tool for "Topic Overviews" or "Business Research," these tasks are less message-intensive. In contrast, technical digital work and documentation account for a larger share of the total message volume, indicating that these are high-intensity use cases where workers iterate frequently with the model.

Industry-specific patterns also emerged. In the Finance and Insurance sector, financial and tax-related tasks were significantly more prevalent. In the Professional and Technical Services sector, technical digital work and data analysis were the primary drivers. This variance indicates that firms are successfully tailoring the general-purpose capabilities of the model to their specific domain needs.

## Implications for the Future of Work

The findings of this study have significant implications for how we view the economic impact of AI. The fact that adoption is concentrated among high-value, high-capital firms suggests that AI might initially exacerbate the productivity gap between "superstar" firms and the rest of the economy. If AI requires significant complementary investments in organizational processes, the mere availability of the technology may not be enough for smaller or less sophisticated firms to catch up.

Furthermore, the broad distribution of tasks suggests that AI is not just replacing specific jobs but is instead being integrated into the existing division of labor. By assisting with documentation, coding, and communication, the technology acts as a force multiplier for knowledge workers. The high intensity of use among junior workers is particularly noteworthy, as it could fundamentally change how early-career professionals learn their trades and progress through organizational hierarchies.

Ultimately, the rapid growth in token consumption within existing firms—increasing nearly fourfold for the earliest cohorts—shows that organizations are not just trying AI; they are deepening their reliance on it. This suggests a continuous process of discovery and learning that will likely reshape the nature of professional work over the coming decade. As firms continue to co-invent new ways to use these tools, the focus will likely shift from simple message-based interactions to more complex, integrated AI agents that handle multi-step business processes.
