## The Dynamics of AR(1) Processes and Bernoulli Convolutions

Autoregressive processes of order 1, commonly denoted as AR(1), are foundational models in time series analysis used to describe systems where the current state depends linearly on the previous state plus a random shock. In the study by Peter Kern and Alef E. Sterk, the focus is placed on a specific class of AR(1) processes defined by the relation:

$$X_k = \beta X_{k-1} + \varepsilon_k$$

where $\beta \in (0, 1)$ is a constant parameter and the innovations $\varepsilon_k$ are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables following a Bernoulli distribution. Specifically, the noise $\varepsilon_k$ takes the value $0$ with probability $p$ and the value $1-\beta$ with probability $q=1-p$, for some $p \in (0, 1)$.

This specific model leads to a stationary distribution that is an affine transformation of a Bernoulli convolution. Bernoulli convolutions are probability measures that arise from the infinite sum of scaled Bernoulli variables. The stationary solution to the AR(1) equation above can be written as:

$$X_k = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \beta^j Z_{k-j} \text{ where } Z_i = \varepsilon_{k-i}$$

The properties of these distributions are famously complex. Depending on the choice of $\beta$, the distribution function $F_{\beta,p}$ can be absolutely continuous (having a smooth density) or continuous singular (like a Cantor distribution, which is continuous but has a derivative of zero almost everywhere). The paper investigates the extremal behavior of these processes—how their maximum values behave over long periods—across the entire range of $\beta \in (0, 1)$, generalizing previous work that was restricted to the case where the support of the distribution is a Cantor-like set.

## Structural Insights into the Stationary Distribution

A central contribution of this research is the characterization of the stationary distribution function $F_{\beta,p}(x)$ across the full unit interval $[0, 1]$. The distribution is uniquely defined as the solution to the functional equation:

$$F_{\beta,p}(x) = p F_{\beta,p}\left(\frac{x}{\beta}\right) + q F_{\beta,p}\left(\frac{x-1+\beta}{\beta}\right)$$

for $x \in (0, 1)$. The researchers establish that for any $\beta \in (0, 1)$, the distribution near the origin exhibits a specific power-law behavior modulated by a periodic function. Specifically, they show that:

$$F_{\beta,p}(x) = x^{\log p / \log \beta} \nu_{\beta,p}(\log x)$$

where $\nu_{\beta,p}$ is a continuous function that is log-periodic with period $\log(1/\beta)$. This representation is crucial because the tail behavior of a distribution (how it behaves near its boundaries) dictates its extreme value properties.

Previously, this log-periodic structure was well-understood for the case $\beta \in (0, 1/2]$, where the two terms in the functional equation have disjoint supports. When $\beta \in (1/2, 1)$, the supports overlap, creating "internal" complexity. The authors demonstrate that while the local behavior at the boundaries maintains this log-periodic form, the global behavior of the distribution becomes significantly more intricate. Through numerical analysis, they highlight that for specific values of $\beta$ known as Garsia numbers (such as $2^{-1/2}$), the periodic function $\nu_{\beta,p}$ may actually become constant, which has profound implications for the resulting limit laws of the process's maxima.

## Extreme Value Theory: From Max-Stable to Max-Semistable

Classic extreme value theory (EVT) often focuses on max-stable distributions (Gumbel, Fréchet, and Weibull). If the normalized maximum of $n$ i.i.d. variables converges to a limit, that limit must be max-stable. However, Bernoulli convolutions often do not belong to the domain of attraction of these classical laws due to their inherent scaling symmetry. Instead, they belong to the domain of geometric partial attraction of max-semistable laws.

A distribution $H$ is max-semistable if it satisfies the functional equation:

$$H(cx) = H^c(x) \text{ for some } c > 1$$

These laws are more general than max-stable laws and are characterized by log-periodic oscillations in their tails. The authors prove that the stationary distribution $F_{\beta,p}$ leads to a max-semistable limit of Weibull-type, with shape parameters:

$$\alpha = \log q / \log \beta > 0$$
$$c = q^{-1} > 1$$

The resulting limiting distribution for the maxima takes the form:

$$\exp(-(-x)^\alpha \nu_{\rightarrow, \beta,q}(\log(-x)))$$

where $\nu_{\rightarrow, \beta,q}$ is an extension of the periodic function derived from the distribution's structure. This result signifies that the extremes of these AR(1) processes do not settle into a single smooth distribution but rather maintain a periodic signature of the underlying fractal measure.

## The Merge Theorem and the Extremal Index

One of the most significant theoretical results in the paper is the "Merge Theorem" (Theorem 4.3). In many cases involving max-semistable laws, convergence only occurs along specific subsequences of time $n$. The Merge Theorem established here is a stronger result: it proves uniform convergence for the entire sequence of natural numbers $n$.

For the AR(1) process, the maximum $M_n = \max\{X_0, \ldots, X_{n-1}\}$ satisfies:

$$\sup_{x \in \mathbb{R}} |P(a_n^\circ(M_n - 1) \leq x) - H_{pr_n}^{\beta,q}(x)| \to 0$$

as $n \to \infty$. Here, $a_n^\circ = \beta^{-m_n}$ is a scaling sequence and $r_n = q^{m_n}n \in [1, q^{-1})$ is a factor that accounts for the position within the log-periodic cycle.

A critical discovery is the identification of the extremal index. The extremal index, often denoted by $\theta$, measures the degree of clustering of extreme events. For i.i.d. sequences, $\theta = 1$. For dependent sequences, $\theta < 1$ usually indicates that extremes happen in clusters. The authors prove that for this AR(1) process, the extremal index is exactly $p$. This means the dependence in the AR(1) process reduces the "effective" number of independent observations by a factor of $p$. When the noise $\varepsilon_k$ is likely to be zero (high $p$), the process stays near its previous value, leading to clusters of high values whenever the process approaches its upper boundary.

## Connecting Stochasticity to Dynamical Systems

The research further bridges the gap between stochastic processes and dynamical systems. The authors show that the stationary distribution $F_{\beta,p}$ is intimately related to both deterministic and random maps.

In the deterministic setting, they define a two-dimensional map $S_{\beta,p} : [0, 1]^2 \to [0, 1]^2$, which is a generalization of the classic baker's transformation. They prove that the measure $\mu_{\beta,p} = (F_{\beta,p} \circ \text{pr}_1)^{-1}$, where $\text{pr}_1(x, y) = x$, is invariant under this map. This establishes that the statistical properties of the AR(1) process are equivalent to the long-term behavior of a chaotic deterministic system.

In the random setting, the distribution $F_{\beta,p}$ arises as the solution to a problem involving the divergence of orbits. If one randomly iterates between two functions:

$$f_0(x) = x/\beta$$
$$f_1(x) = x/\beta + 1 - 1/\beta$$

with probabilities $p$ and $q$ respectively, then $F_{\beta,p}(x)$ represents the probability that the orbit starting at $x$ eventually diverges to infinity. This connection provides a geometric interpretation of the distribution and explains why the extremal behavior is so closely tied to the scaling parameter $\beta$.

## Significance and Practical Implications

The paper's extension of these results to the full range $\beta \in (0, 1)$ is significant for several reasons. First, it provides a rigorous mathematical framework for understanding AR(1) processes with singular or "near-singular" marginals, which are common in various high-frequency data applications. Second, the confirmation of the extremal index $p$ allows researchers to precisely quantify how much more likely clusters of extreme events are in these processes compared to independent sequences.

Finally, the use of max-semistable laws and the Merge Theorem offers a more robust tool for risk assessment. Traditional EVT might suggest a standard Weibull limit, but this work shows that such an approximation would ignore the log-periodic oscillations inherent in the system. By accounting for these oscillations, one can achieve a much more accurate asymptotic description of rare, high-impact events in systems governed by autoregressive dynamics and fractal noise.
