## Reimagining Recommenders as Allocation Devices in Spot-Work Markets

The growth of the "gig economy" has led to the rise of spot-work platforms, which connect workers with short-term, on-demand labor opportunities. Unlike traditional job boards that facilitate long-term employment, these platforms manage a high volume of perishable, time-sensitive vacancies. In this environment, recommender systems are not merely tools for reducing information friction; they function as critical market design components that allocate limited worker attention across a finite supply of jobs.

![workflow-from-recommendation-to-match](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2606.17397v1/figuretable/flow_from_recommendation_to_match_m2.png)
*{fig:workflow-from-recommendation-to-match}: The two-stage matching process on the Timee platform. Workers first build a favorite list based on template recommendations and then receive notifications to apply for specific offerings.*

In spot-work markets, the matching process often involves a two-stage mechanism. On platforms like Timee, Japan's largest spot-work provider, workers do not usually apply directly from a recommendation list. Instead, they "favorite" a job template (e.g., "Dishwasher at Restaurant X"). This adds the template to their favorite list, and the platform subsequently sends them push notifications whenever a new specific "offering" (a shift) is posted for that template. Because these shifts are often filled on a first-come, first-served basis within minutes, the favorite list acts as a "stock of latent demand," determining which workers have the first opportunity to see and claim a job.

## The Pitfalls of Greedy Engagement Optimization

Standard recommender systems typically employ a "Greedy" approach. They use machine learning to predict the probability that a specific user $i$ will engage with (in this case, favorite) a specific item $k$. The system then recommends the items with the highest predicted probabilities. While this maximizes short-term engagement metrics, it often fails at the market level in capacity-constrained environments.

The authors identify a phenomenon called "misdirected concentration." Because Greedy systems only care about the probability of a "favorite," they tend to funnel massive amounts of exposure to popular templates. However, a template that is highly attractive might only offer a single shift per week. If thousands of workers favorite that template, the resulting congestion means the probability of any single worker actually landing a shift becomes negligible. 

Conversely, templates representing high-demand areas with many unfilled shifts—perhaps because they are less "glamorous"—may receive insufficient exposure. This leads to a market imbalance where popular jobs are oversubscribed while critical labor needs remain unmet, ultimately lowering the overall job-finding rate for workers and the fill rate for firms.

## Proposed Solutions: From Quotas to Thresholds

To address these inefficiencies, the researchers propose moving away from engagement-only optimization toward algorithms that incorporate supply-side constraints. They introduce three main designs:

1.  **Static Quota (SQ):** This approach sets a uniform limit $q$ on the number of workers who can be recommended a specific template in a given round. This prevents any single template from monopolizing platform exposure.
2.  **Adaptive Quota (AQ):** Recognizing that labor demand is heterogeneous, AQ adjusts the quota for each template based on its market activity. Templates with more posted shifts or more unfilled capacity receive higher quotas.
3.  **Thresholded Eligibility Control (TEC):** While AQ is effective in simulations, its sequential nature (assigning jobs to workers one by one) makes it difficult to scale to platforms with millions of users. TEC is designed to be a parallelizable approximation of AQ.

### The TEC Mechanism

TEC manages exposure by assigning each template $k$ an eligibility threshold $\tau_k$. The system calculates a score $p_k$ for each template based on its recent activity:

$p_k = w_0 x_{0k} + w_1 x_{1k}$

Where $x_{0k}$ is the capacity posted recently, $x_{1k}$ is the unfilled capacity from the previous round, and $w_0$ and $w_1$ are weights. These scores are then capped to prevent extreme concentration and normalized into thresholds. 

To generate recommendations, the system assigns a random "selection timing" $z_{is}$ to each slot $s$ in worker $i$'s recommendation list. A template $k$ is eligible for that slot only if:

$z_{is} \leq \tau_k$

Among all eligible templates, the system then selects the one with the highest predicted favoriting probability $\alpha_{\theta(i)\theta(k)}$. This allows the platform to prioritize templates with high labor demand while still respecting user preferences, all while allowing the computation for each worker to happen independently and in parallel.

## Simulating Market Dynamics

Before deploying in the real world, the authors calibrated an economic model using data from Timee. The simulation compared the long-run steady states of Greedy, SQ, AQ, and TEC recommenders. 

The results indicated that exposure control significantly improves market outcomes. In the baseline simulation, the Greedy approach resulted in a job-finding rate of 57.6%. Implementing TEC increased this rate to 70.0%. The fill rate—the percentage of job openings successfully filled—similarly rose from 67.4% to 82.2%. 

Crucially, the simulation revealed how the different algorithms distribute exposure. While Greedy and SQ allocated similar amounts of recommendations to both "active" (high-demand) and "inactive" templates, TEC successfully redirected exposure. Under TEC, active templates were recommended approximately 35.8 times per round, while inactive ones were recommended only 2.8 times. This strategic reallocation ensures that worker attention is funneled where it is most likely to result in a successful match.

## Field Experiment: Real-World Validation

The researchers conducted a prefecture-level randomized controlled trial (RCT) on the Timee platform. They designated Aomori prefecture as the treatment group (receiving the TEC recommender) and Iwate prefecture as the control group (remaining on the Greedy recommender). 

### Impression-Level Insights

The field experiment yielded several findings that went beyond the simulation results. One of the most striking outcomes was that TEC actually increased the probability that an individual impression would result in a "favorite" by 0.5 percentage points. This was unexpected because the Greedy system is explicitly designed to maximize this metric.

The authors attribute this to two factors:
*   **Reduced Redundancy:** By limiting exposure to the most popular templates, TEC naturally introduced more variety into workers' recommendation lists. The data showed that workers are less likely to favorite a template they have already seen multiple times.
*   **Signaling and Novelty:** Even if a template is slightly less preferred on average, seeing a "fresh" recommendation that has a higher likelihood of leading to a real job may be more attractive to active job seekers.

### Market-Level Impact

Within the one-month experimental window, the deployment of TEC in Aomori led to a statistically significant increase of approximately 9 realized matches per day compared to the control.

The experiment also confirmed that TEC successfully shifted the distribution of recommendations. As shown in the distributional analysis, there was a significant reduction in the number of templates receiving near-zero exposure. This suggests the algorithm succeeded in "broadening the funnel" and ensuring a wider variety of firms could reach potential workers.

## Educational Takeaways: Recommenders as Allocation Tools

This research highlights a fundamental shift in how we should think about recommender systems in matching markets. In standard e-commerce, recommending a popular book to everyone might be optimal because the digital or physical inventory is often large enough to satisfy all demand. However, in labor, dating, or housing markets, "items" have strict capacity constraints.

Key principles demonstrated by the paper include:

*   **Stock vs. Flow:** In spot-work, the "favorite" is a stock of latent demand. Recommenders must manage this stock to ensure it is distributed across the "flow" of incoming job offerings.
*   **Congestion Management:** In a matching market, giving too much exposure to a single item creates congestion, which wastes worker effort and platform resources.
*   **Supply-Side Awareness:** Recommenders perform better when they are aware of the "fill rate" or "unmet demand" on the supply side, rather than just focusing on user clicks.
*   **Diversity as a Byproduct of Efficiency:** Controlling exposure to popular items naturally increases the diversity and novelty of recommendations, which can paradoxically improve the very engagement metrics that Greedy systems try to optimize.

By integrating market design principles into algorithmic recommendation, platforms can move beyond simple preference matching to active market stabilization, ensuring that both sides of the marketplace—workers seeking income and firms seeking labor—achieve better outcomes.
