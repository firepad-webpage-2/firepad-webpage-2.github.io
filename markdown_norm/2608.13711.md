## Context and the Translational Gap in Polyp Detection

Colonoscopy remains the primary intervention for the prevention of colorectal cancer, allowing for both the identification and immediate removal of precancerous lesions. Despite its clinical efficacy, the procedure is inherently difficult for endoscopists. Factors such as the complex morphology of polyps, the presence of anatomical folds, fluctuating lighting conditions, and debris within the colon lead to significant miss rates. Estimates suggest that up to 27% of polyps may be overlooked during standard examinations. To mitigate this, computer-aided detection (CADe) systems—specifically deep learning-based object detectors—have been developed to serve as a supportive second pair of eyes in the procedure room.

While many CADe systems report exceptionally high accuracy on academic benchmarks, their translation into routine clinical practice has been slow. This "translational gap" stems from a disconnect between how these systems are trained and evaluated in a laboratory setting versus how they perform in a real-world clinical environment. Academic benchmarks often rely on "curated" datasets—sets of short video clips or images where a polyp is clearly visible and centered. These datasets lack the long, monotonous stretches of negative frames (frames without polyps) and the frequent procedure-related artifacts like motion blur, specular highlights, and instrument interference that characterize an actual, unedited colonoscopy. Consequently, a model that performs well on a curated benchmark may generate an overwhelming number of false alerts when faced with the messy reality of a full clinical procedure, leading to clinician fatigue and distraction.

## Standardizing Evaluation with TRUE-Colon

To bridge this gap, this research introduces the TRUE-Colon protocol, a standardized benchmarking framework designed to evaluate CADe systems using deployment-relevant characteristics. Traditional object detection research relies heavily on mean Average Precision ($mAP$), a metric that summarizes how well a model localizes objects across various confidence levels and overlap requirements. While useful, $mAP$ does not fully capture the operational experience of an endoscopist.

The TRUE-Colon protocol expands the evaluation to include four critical dimensions:

1.  **Localization Accuracy:** Measured through standard metrics like $mAP_{50}$ (at an Intersection-over-Union threshold of 0.50) and $mAP_{50:95}$ (averaged over multiple thresholds). This provides a baseline for spatial precision.
2.  **False-Alert Burden:** The research collapses detector outputs into a binary "alert" or "no alert" decision at a specific confidence threshold $\tau$. By calculating the frame-level True Positive Rate ($\text{TPR}$) and False Positive Rate ($\text{FPR}$), the protocol quantifies how often a clinician would be interrupted by correct versus incorrect detections.
3.  **Detection Latency:** In a real-time procedure, every second counts. Latency measures the time-to-first-detection—how many frames pass from the moment a polyp first appears until the system triggers a valid alert.
4.  **Temporal Reliability:** This metric assesses the consistency of detections over time. It measures whether a lesion is detected at least once (consistency) and whether it remains detected for at least half of its visible duration (persistence).

By reporting these metrics at a "matched operating point," where each model is adjusted to produce the same false-alert rate, TRUE-Colon allows for a fair, clinical-grade comparison between different AI architectures.

## The Pitfalls of Dataset Curation

A core contribution of this work is the empirical demonstration of "transfer asymmetry." The researchers investigated two types of data distributions: curated data ($D_{cur}$) and full-procedure data ($D_{proc}$). Curated datasets, such as SUN and PICCOLO, are highly focused on lesions. In contrast, procedure-level datasets like REAL-Colon contain unedited videos where more than 85% of the frames are negative, providing a realistic representation of clinical workflow.

![challenges-in-real-time-colonoscopy](https://paper-assets.alphaxiv.org/figures-normalized/figures/2608.13711v1/images/challenges.png)
*{fig:challenges-in-real-time-colonoscopy}: Common artifacts in unedited colonoscopy videos, including motion blur, specular highlights, and instrument interference, which frequently cause false alerts in models trained only on curated data.*

The researchers found that models trained exclusively on $D_{cur}$ fail significantly when tested on $D_{proc}$. For example, the $mAP_{50}$ of a state-of-the-art detector plummeted from $mAP_{50} = 0.724$ on a curated test set to $mAP_{50} = 0.164$ when faced with full-procedure videos. This performance drop highlights that curated benchmarks provide an inflated sense of reliability. Conversely, models trained on $D_{proc}$ were found to be remarkably robust. These models not only performed well on full procedures but also maintained competitive accuracy when transferred back to curated benchmarks. This suggests that the inclusion of negative frames and artifacts in the training data provides a critical "negative signal," teaching the model to ignore distractions and focus only on true lesions.

## Quantifying Transfer Asymmetry

The transfer experiments were structured to test the generalizability of four major real-time detectors: Faster R-CNN, YOLOv8, YOLOv11, and RT-DETR. The results revealed a consistent pattern across all architectures.

In the $D_{cur} \to D_{cur}$ setting, models appeared highly successful. However, in the $D_{cur} \to D_{proc}$ setting, the lack of exposure to the vast variety of negative colon tissue and procedure-related debris led to a surge in false positives. The models "overfit" to the specific, clean appearance of polyps in curated clips and struggled to distinguish them from similar-looking artifacts in full videos.

The $D_{proc} \to D_{proc}$ setting, while showing lower absolute $mAP$ scores than curated settings (reflecting the true difficulty of the task), yielded models that were clinically usable. Most importantly, the $D_{proc} \to D_{cur}$ transfer proved that training on realistic data does not harm the model's ability to identify clear-cut polyps; instead, it hardens the model against the noise inherent in clinical practice. This finding strongly advocates for a shift in the community: training on full-procedure data should be the standard, rather than a niche approach.

## Benchmarking Real-Time Architectures

The study provides a detailed comparison of contemporary detectors within the TRUE-Colon framework, equalizing them at a matched false-alert burden (where the frame-level $\text{FPR}$ is approximately 4–5%).

*   **RT-DETR (Real-Time Detection Transformer):** This model exhibited the best overall sensitivity and temporal reliability. It achieved the lowest detection latency, identifying polyps an average of 38.3 frames after they became visible. It also showed the highest persistence, though this came at the cost of lower throughput (51.0 frames per second) compared to other models.
*   **YOLOv8 and YOLOv11:** These models offered a balance of high speed and strong performance. YOLOv11, despite being newer, performed similarly to YOLOv8 in this specific task. Both achieved extremely high throughput (>160 FPS), making them suitable for hardware with lower computational power.
*   **Faster R-CNN:** While a classic architecture, it generally lagged behind the newer YOLO and Transformer-based models in both sensitivity and speed when evaluated at the same false-alert rate.

A shared failure mode across all architectures was the detection of small polyps. The detection accuracy for small lesions was nearly zero, indicating that even state-of-the-art models require significantly more development to match human performance on subtle, early-stage polyps. Additionally, histological subtypes that were absent from the training set, such as sessile serrated lesions, were consistently missed, highlighting a need for more diverse data.

## Clinical Implications and Future Directions

The TRUE-Colon study emphasizes that the path to clinical deployment for CADe systems requires more than just better algorithms; it requires a fundamental change in evaluation philosophy. The "translational gap" is largely a byproduct of an evaluation paradigm that ignores the false-alert burden and temporal dynamics of a real-world procedure.

Key takeaways for the field include:
*   **Prioritize Procedure-Level Data:** Researchers should prioritize the acquisition and use of full, unedited procedure videos for both training and validation.
*   **Report Deployment Metrics:** Evaluation should move beyond $mAP$ to include latency and false-alert rates at matched operating points. This ensures that a model's performance is not just a result of optimistic thresholding.
*   **Address Edge Cases:** The consistent failure on small polyps and specific artifacts (like specular highlights) suggests that future research should focus on data augmentation or architectural changes specifically designed to handle these clinical realities.

By exposing the transfer asymmetry and providing a standardized protocol, this work offers a roadmap for developing CADe systems that are not only accurate in the lab but also reliable and trustworthy in the hands of an endoscopist. The ultimate goal is a system that reduces the polyp miss rate without becoming a source of distraction, and TRUE-Colon provides the metrics to measure progress toward that ideal.
