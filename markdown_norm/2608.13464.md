The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) Mid-Infrared Instrument (MIRI) Medium Resolution Spectrometer (MRS) is a primary tool for mid-infrared spectroscopy, providing a spectral resolution of $R \sim 3000$ over a wavelength range from $5-28 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$. While the MRS offers unprecedented sensitivity, researchers have identified that residual instrumental signatures, known as fixed pattern noise, limit the achievable signal-to-noise ratio ($S/N$) for point source observations.

Researchers Karl D. Gordon and David R. Law from the Space Telescope Science Institute (STScI) have developed a set of Point Fixed Pattern Corrections (PFPCs) designed to mitigate these residuals. By combining observations from a diverse set of calibration targets—including O, A, and G dwarf stars as well as asteroids—this work provides a more robust calibration for MIRI MRS point source spectra than previously available through the standard pipeline.

## The Challenge of Fixed Pattern Noise

In high-precision spectroscopy, fixed pattern noise refers to non-random variations in the recorded signal that remain constant over time but vary with wavelength or detector position. For the MIRI MRS, these artifacts typically manifest at the $1.5\%$ to $2\%$ level. For many science cases, particularly those involving faint spectral lines or the search for broad molecular features, these artifacts can be confused with real astrophysical signals.

Several factors contribute to the fixed pattern noise in MIRI MRS:
1. **Residual Fringes:** The MRS instrument contains internal optics that cause interference, resulting in a periodic "fringe" pattern in the spectra. While a static fringe flat is applied during standard processing, point sources produce fringes that vary depending on their exact location on the detector.
2. **Flat Field Uncertainties:** The standard flat fields used by the pipeline are derived from extended sources. When observing a point source, the interaction between the Point Spread Function (PSF) and the detector pixels can lead to small-scale flux variations that are not perfectly corrected.
3. **Sampling Artifacts:** Because the MRS uses an Integral Field Unit (IFU) to slice the field of view into distinct spatial elements, the way a point source is sampled can introduce instrumental features, especially if the source is not perfectly centered.
4. **Broad Instrumental Features:** Some features, such as a prominent absorption-like dip near $5.8 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$, have been observed in many datasets but are known to be instrumental rather than astrophysical.

## Constructing Point Fixed Pattern Corrections (PFPCs)

The authors developed the PFPCs by analyzing a large collection of calibration data from JWST Cycles 1, 2, and 3. The goal was to create a set of corrections that are dither-dependent, meaning they account for the specific artifacts produced when a source is placed at different positions within the MRS field of view.

The corrections were derived for all 12 spectral segments (Channel 1-4, each with Short, Medium, and Long gratings) and for the four standard dither positions used in most point source programs. This resulted in a total of 48 individual correction vectors.

To ensure the corrections were high-quality and free of astrophysical contamination, the researchers utilized a mix of sources:
- **Hot Stars (O and A dwarfs):** These stars have well-understood, relatively simple spectra. However, they possess hydrogen and helium absorption lines that must be masked.
- **Cooler Stars (G dwarfs):** These provide higher flux at longer wavelengths but contain complex molecular lines at shorter wavelengths.
- **Asteroids:** Because asteroids in the mid-infrared behave nearly like blackbodies, they offer a featureless continuum that is ideal for identifying instrumental artifacts without the interference of stellar absorption lines.

By dividing the observed spectra of these sources by their theoretical models (such as CALSPEC models for stars or quadratic fits for asteroids), the researchers isolated the instrumental residuals.

## Masking and Combination Strategy

A critical part of the methodology involved a sophisticated masking strategy. If a stellar absorption line in a calibration star were not masked, it would be incorrectly included in the PFPC as an instrumental feature, creating a "fake" emission line in any science data corrected with that PFPC.

The authors applied specific masks based on the source type:
- For A stars, regions $\pm 0.02 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$ around hydrogen lines were excluded.
- For G stars, segments 1A and 1B were excluded entirely due to dense molecular features, and other segments were masked $\pm 0.04 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$ around known lines.
- For O stars, hydrogen lines at wavelengths greater than $6.5 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$ were masked $\pm 0.02 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$.

By using this diverse set of sources, the gaps left by masking one star could be filled by data from another. For example, O stars were used to provide information in regions where A stars had strong hydrogen lines. Asteroids were particularly useful in Channel 4, where stellar flux is low and throughput is limited. The individual measurements were then combined using sigma-clipping to remove outliers, such as residual cosmic rays or bad pixels.

## Performance and Noise Reduction

The application of PFPCs results in a significant increase in the $S/N$ of MRS spectra. The authors tested their corrections on data not used in the creation of the PFPCs, such as observations of the star HD 163466.

The results showed that for single observations where the pipeline's residual fringe correction was not used, the PFPCs improved the $S/N$ by factors of 2 to 6, and in some cases up to 8. This is particularly important for sources with many spectral lines, where the standard pipeline's fringe-fitting algorithm might accidentally remove real astronomical features.

One of the most notable successes of the PFPCs is the removal of the instrumental feature at $5.8 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$. Previous researchers had noticed a broad absorption feature at this wavelength, which could easily be mistaken for a carbonyl ($C=O$) group in an astrophysical source. The PFPCs demonstrate that this feature is purely instrumental and varies by dither position. By applying the correction, this "phantom" feature is entirely eliminated, allowing for a more accurate interpretation of the science data.

Furthermore, the PFPCs improve the "stitching" between different spectral segments. Normally, researchers must apply manual scaling factors to ensure that the flux level is consistent when moving from one segment to the next (e.g., from 1A to 1B). With PFPCs, these required corrections are reduced significantly, often staying within $1\%$ of unity.

## The MRS-PFPC Software Package

To make these corrections accessible to the broader astronomical community, the authors have released a Python package called `MRS-PFPC`. This tool allows users to process their own MIRI MRS data using the improved calibration.

The workflow involves:
1. Running the standard `jwst` pipeline up to the 1D extraction stage, but using specific settings (such as autocentroiding and specific cube-building parameters) to ensure the data is consistent with the calibration set.
2. Applying the `pfpc_cor` command, which divides each dithered spectrum by the appropriate PFPC.
3. Combining the corrected dithers into a final, high-$S/N$ spectrum.

The authors note that the $S/N$ of the PFPCs themselves is currently high enough that they do not add significant noise to single observations. However, for programs that coadd many hours of data to reach extremely high $S/N$ levels (above $1000$), the noise in the PFPC itself may eventually become the limiting factor.

## Significance and Outlook

The development of dither-dependent PFPCs represents a significant step forward in the calibration of the MIRI MRS. By moving beyond the static flat fields and generic fringe corrections of the standard pipeline, this work enables researchers to reach the full potential of JWST's mid-infrared capabilities.

The primary benefits are:
- **Reliability:** Removing the risk of the pipeline's residual fringe correction distorting real spectral lines.
- **Precision:** Achieving sub-$1\%$ residuals in the spectral continuum.
- **Clarity:** Eliminating known instrumental artifacts like the $5.8 \text{ }\mu\text{m}$ dip.

While the current PFPCs are optimized for the standard 4-point point-source dither pattern, the methodology could be extended to other dither patterns or even to extended sources in the future. The authors also suggest that as more calibration data is collected during the lifetime of JWST, the $S/N$ of the PFPCs can be further improved, pushing the boundaries of what is detectable in the mid-infrared universe.

The stability of the instrument over the first three cycles of JWST operations suggests that these corrections will remain valid for the foreseeable future, providing a stable foundation for high-precision spectroscopy with MIRI.
