In computational number theory and algebraic geometry, calculating rational points on curves is a central challenge. This often requires working with sophisticated geometric objects like Abelian varieties and their Jacobians. A particularly important structure in this domain is the Poincaré bundle, which characterizes the relationship between an Abelian variety and its dual. In the paper "Poincaré à la Makdisi," Nicolas Mascot develops a concrete algorithmic framework for computing with the Poincaré torsor—the punctured version of the Poincaré bundle—on the Jacobian of an algebraic curve. By extending the efficient linear algebra techniques established by Kamal Khuri-Makdisi, this work provides a bridge between abstract geometric theory and the practical requirements of modern arithmetic geometry, such as the "Quadratic Chabauty" method.

## Context and Theoretical Foundation

An Abelian variety $A$ is a projective algebraic variety that also possesses a group structure. Every Abelian variety has a dual variety $A^\vee$, which parametrizes line bundles on $A$. The Poincaré bundle $P$ is a unique line bundle defined on the product $A \times A^\vee$. It acts as a "universal" bundle: if you fix a point $y$ in the dual $A^\vee$, the restriction of $P$ to the slice $A \times \{y\}$ is exactly the line bundle represented by $y$. Conversely, its restriction to $\{0\} \times A^\vee$ is trivial.

When $A$ is the Jacobian $J$ of a "nice" algebraic curve $C$ (nonsingular, complete, and geometrically integral), the theory becomes more concrete. The Jacobian $J$ can be identified with its own dual $J^\vee$ via a canonical polarization. In this setting, the Poincaré bundle can be viewed as a bundle on $J \times J$. The primary focus of Mascot’s work is the Poincaré torsor $P^\times$, which is the Poincaré bundle with the zero section removed. This object is a "universal biextension" of $J \times J$ by the multiplicative group $G_m$. Being a biextension means that $P^\times$ possesses two partial group laws, allowing elements to be "added" if they share a common coordinate in either the first or the second factor of $J \times J$.

## The Makdisi Algorithmic Framework

The foundation for these computations is the framework developed by Kamal Khuri-Makdisi. Standard methods for computing on Jacobians often rely on the Riemann-Roch theorem or Cantor’s algorithm for hyperelliptic curves. However, Makdisi proposed a more general and efficient approach using linear algebra on spaces of global sections of line bundles.

For a curve $C$ of genus $g$, one fixes a line bundle $L$ of sufficiently high degree $\delta \geq 2g+1$. The core idea is to represent points on the Jacobian by looking at subspaces of sections. Specifically, the space of global sections is defined as:
$$V_n = H^0(C, L^{\otimes n})$$
A point $x$ on the Jacobian can be represented via an effective divisor $D$ of degree $\delta$. In Makdisi's system, this point is encoded by the subspace $V_2(-D) \subset V_2$ consisting of sections that vanish on $D$. These subspaces are stored as matrices, and group operations on the Jacobian are performed using linear algebra routines like `SecMul` (multiplication of sections), `DivAdd`, and `DivSub`. These algorithms are highly efficient, with a complexity of $O(g^\omega)$ operations in the base field $K$, where $\omega$ is the exponent for linear algebra (typically between $2.37$ and $3$).

## Representation of the Poincaré Torsor

Mascot extends this representation to the Poincaré torsor $P^\times$. To represent a point in $P^\times$, the author fixes two reference divisors $D_0$ and $E_0$ (associated with sections $d_0$ and $e_0$). A point $x \in J(K)$ is represented as $x = [D - D_0]$ and a point $y \in J(K)$ as $y = [E - E_0]$, where $D$ and $E$ are effective divisors of degree $\delta$.

An element $\pi$ in the fiber of the torsor over $(x, y)$ is represented as a triple:
$$(M_D, M_E, \lambda)$$
In this triple, $M_D$ and $M_E$ are the Makdisi matrix representations for the divisors $D$ and $E$. The scalar $\lambda \in K^\times$ relates the element to a canonical reference point $[D, E]$ in the fiber. The definition of this reference point uses the norm functor $N$:
$$\pi = \lambda \cdot [D,E] \text{ with } \lambda \in K^\times \text{ and } [D,E] = N_{D-D_0}(1 \in \mathcal{O}_C(E-E_0))$$
This setup adheres to a "golden rule" of disjointness: the algorithms assume $D$ and $E$ are disjoint to ensure the evaluations remain well-defined. If they are not disjoint, a "moving lemma" is employed to find linearly equivalent divisors that satisfy the condition.

## Core Algorithms for Element Manipulation

A major contribution of the paper is the development of algorithms to compare different representations of the same point and to handle the canonical involution.

### Comparing Points
Because a point in $J$ can be represented by many different divisors $D$, a point in $P^\times$ can likewise have multiple representations. If we have two triples representing elements in the same fiber, the algorithm must determine the scalar ratio between them. This is non-trivial because it involves finding the rational functions that define the linear equivalences between the divisors and then evaluating those functions at specific points. Mascot introduces "Algorithm 4" to evaluate the ratio of two sections at a divisor using linear algebra, avoiding the need to explicitly find the functions themselves.

### Canonical Involution
The Poincaré bundle is symmetric, meaning there is an isomorphism $\tau: P^\times \to P^\times$ that swaps the coordinates:
$$\tau: (x,y) \mapsto (y,x)$$
Computing this involution explicitly is required for many applications. The paper provides a formula based on a generalization of Weil reciprocity. The involution $\tau$ maps $\lambda \cdot [D, E]$ to a point $\lambda' \cdot [E, D]$. The calculation of the new scalar $\lambda'$ involves evaluating the reference sections $d_0$ and $e_0$ at the divisors $D$ and $E$, while accounting for a sign factor $(-1)^\delta$.

## Partial Group Laws and Alignment

As a universal biextension, $P^\times$ allows for addition in either the first or second coordinate. For instance, if we have two elements $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$ in the fibers over $(x, y)$ and $(x', y)$ respectively, we can add them to get an element in the fiber over $(x+x', y)$. This is denoted as the left partial group law:
$$\pi_1 \otimes_1 \pi_2 \in P^\times_{x+x', y} \text{ for } \pi_1 \in P^\times_{x,y}, \pi_2 \in P^\times_{x',y}$$
Implementing this requires an "alignment" step. In practice, $\pi_1$ might be represented using a divisor $E_1$ and $\pi_2$ using a divisor $E_2$. Even if $[E_1 - E_0] = [E_2 - E_0] = y$, the physical divisors $E_1$ and $E_2$ might be different. The alignment algorithms (Algorithm 11 and 12) use the moving lemma to re-represent these points so they share the same divisor $E$, after which the partial group law can be computed by tracking the change in the scalar $\lambda$ and applying Makdisi's `AddFlip` routine.

This framework naturally extends to fast exponentiation. Just as one can compute $n \cdot x$ on a Jacobian in $O(\log n)$ steps, one can compute $\pi^{\otimes_1 n}$ efficiently. This allows for the computation of complex bilinear combinations:
$$\sum_i m_i \left( \sum_j n_{i,j} \pi_{i,j} \right)$$
which are essential for height pairings and other arithmetic constructions.

## Handling Explicit Points and p-adic Precision

In many practical scenarios, points are not given in the Makdisi matrix format but as sums of explicit points on the curve (e.g., $D = P_1 + P_2 - 2P_0$). The paper provides algorithms to convert these explicit divisor sums into the matrix representations required for the Poincaré torsor.

A significant challenge in p-adic settings (frequently used in number theory) is the loss of precision when evaluating functions. For example, evaluating a ratio $s_1/s_2$ at a point $P$ might result in a $0/0$ form if both sections vanish at $P$. Mascot introduces Algorithm 20 to "solve" these indeterminate forms. It finds alternative sections $s_1'$ and $s_2'$ such that $s_1/s_2 = a \cdot s_1'/s_2'$ where $s_2'(P) \neq 0$, ensuring that the computation remains stable and accurate in p-adic arithmetic.

## Computational Impact and Applications

The primary significance of this work lies in its efficiency and its direct applicability to the "Quadratic Chabauty" method. Quadratic Chabauty is a technique used to find rational points on curves where the classical Chabauty-Coleman method fails (specifically when the rank of the Jacobian is equal to or greater than the genus). This method requires explicit calculations of p-adic heights and biextension data.

Prior to Mascot’s work, computing these values was often a bottleneck, particularly for non-hyperelliptic curves or curves of higher genus. By providing an $O(g^\omega)$ approach, this paper enables the application of Quadratic Chabauty to a much broader class of curves. The author notes that these algorithms were implemented and tested as part of a collaboration involving non-split Cartan modular curves, demonstrating that the methodology is not just theoretical but ready for use in computational number theory.

By generalizing the Makdisi framework to the Poincaré torsor, Mascot has provided a robust, efficient, and general-purpose toolkit for one of the more difficult areas of computational algebraic geometry. This work ensures that as the mathematical community moves toward more complex "equationless" methods—where the curve is defined by its properties rather than a single polynomial—the necessary computational infrastructure is in place to handle the underlying geometric objects.
